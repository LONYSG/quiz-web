A '''child safety seat''', sometimes called an '''infant safety seat''', '''infant car seat, child restraint system''', '''child seat''', '''baby seat''', '''car seat''', or a '''booster seat''', is a seat designed specifically to protect children from injury or death during [[vehicle collision]]s. Most commonly these seats are purchased and installed by car owners, but car manufacturers may integrate them directly into their vehicle's design and generally are required to provide anchors and ensure seat belt compatibility. Many jurisdictions require children defined by age, weight, or height to use a government-approved child safety seat when riding in a vehicle. Child safety seats provide passive restraints and must be properly used to be effective. However, research indicates that many child safety restraints are often not installed or used properly.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.nj.gov/oag/hts/childseats/index.html |title=State of New Jersey |website=Nj.gov |access-date=2013-11-19}}</ref> To tackle this negative trend, health officials and child safety experts produce child safety videos to teach proper car seat installation to parents and caregivers.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.cdc.gov/injury/features/child-passenger-safety/index.html|title=Child Passenger Safety|last=CDC|date=2019-09-25|website=Centers for Disease Control and Prevention|language=en-us|access-date=2020-03-23}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.texomashomepage.com/healthy-you/car-seat-safety-healthy-you/|title=Car Seat Safety (Healthy You)|date=2020-02-25|website=Texomashomepage.com|language=en-US|access-date=2020-03-23|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201009150218/https://www.texomashomepage.com/healthy-you/car-seat-safety-healthy-you/|archive-date=October 9, 2020|url-status=dead}}</ref>

== History ==
Since the first [[car]] was manufactured and put on the market in the early 1900s, many modifications and adjustments have been implemented to protect those that drive and ride in motorized vehicles. Most restraints were put into place to protect adults without regard for young children. Though child seats were beginning to be manufactured in the early 1930s, their purpose was not the safety of children, but rather to act as booster seats to bring the child to a height easier for the driving parent to see them.<ref name=mentalfloss/>

It was not until 1962 that two designs with the purpose of protecting a child were developed independently.<ref name=mentalfloss>{{cite web|title=A Brief History of 7 Baby Basics |url=http://mentalfloss.com/article/49280/brief-history-7-baby-basics|website=Mental Floss|access-date=26 December 2014|date=12 March 2013}}</ref> In that year, British inventor Jean Ames created a rear-facing child seat with a Y-shaped strap similar to today's models;<ref>{{cite web|title=Child Safety Seat|url=http://www.madehow.com/Volume-5/Child-Safety-Seat.html|website=Made How|access-date=26 December 2014}}</ref> and American Leonard Rivkin, of [[Denver, Colorado]], designed a forward-facing seat with a metal frame to protect the child.<ref>{{cite web|publisher=United States Patent and Trademark Office|title=USPTO Full-Text and Image Database. Patent #3,107,942|date=March 5, 1962|url=http://patft.uspto.gov/|access-date=16 May 2018|archive-date=October 19, 2012|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121019041123/http://patft.uspto.gov/|url-status=dead}}</ref>

Seat belts for adults were not standard equipment in automobiles until the 1960s. Child safety seats first became available in the late 1960s, but few parents used them.<ref>
[https://www.thehenryford.org/collections-and-research/digital-collections/expert-sets/101263/ "Children's Car Seats"] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201009150219/https://www.thehenryford.org/collections-and-research/digital-collections/expert-sets/101263/ |date=October 9, 2020 }}.</ref>

The capsule, designed specifically for car travel, is rearward-facing and secured to the back seat of the car, and a separate [[bassinet]] fits inside the capsule. The outer shell is made of toughened brown plastic. A seat belt extends from the rear of the capsule to secure the capsule in place.  There is a cream-coloured plastic bassinet, lined with woollen underblanket, that fits inside the capsule. A belt, fastened by [[velcro]] tabs, keeps the baby secure within the bassinet.<ref name=mv>{{cite web | title=Baby Safety Capsule | website=[[Museums Victoria Collections]] | url=https://collections.museumsvictoria.com.au/items/256716 | access-date=11 November 2026}} Text content on this page is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International licence.</ref> The velcro body belts were phased out from 1993 to 1996, owing to the introduction of a stricter [[Australian Standard]] in 1991, requiring a full body harness to protect babies better in case of a [[vehicle rollover]].<ref name=power>{{cite web | title=Safe N Sound baby safety capsule | website=[[Powerhouse collection]] | date=11 May 1987 | url=https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/76446 | access-date=11 November 2026}} Text is released under Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives licence.</ref> It has a release mechanism that enables the bassinet to rotate in the event of a collision, which distributes the forces more uniformly over the body of the infant, and keeps it more upright. There is also an "impact-absorbing bubble" in the base for the bassinet to push against. The bassinet is removable so that it can be removed from the car, and the baby can be carried around in it.<ref name=power/>

In October 2026, the invention was commemorated when the [[Royal Australian Mint]] issued a five-coin set commemorating various inventions, including the baby capsule.<ref name=whitehouse>{{cite web | last=Whitehouse | first=Annabel | title=Royal Australian Mint unveils new coins featuring celebrated Australian inventions | website=ABC News | date=7 October 2026 | url=https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-07/mint-unveils-new-coins-featuring-australian-inventions/107239858 | access-date=10 November 2026}}</ref><ref name=ram>{{cite web | title=Celebrating Australian Inventions with Bright Ideas | website=Royal Australian Mint | date=8 October 2026 | url=https://www.ramint.gov.au/about-us/whats-new/celebrating-australian-inventions-bright-ideas | access-date=11 November 2026}}</ref>

The old design is no longer in use, but Britax and other brands continue to manufacture restraints marketed as "capsules", some of which have handles to lift the whole unit out of the car.<ref>{{cite web | title=Best Car Seats for Babies | website=Britax AU | url=https://www.britax.com.au/root/car-seats/babies/5637170083.c | access-date=11 November 2026}}</ref><ref>{{cite web | title=Baby Car Capsule | website=www.babybunting.com.au | url=https://www.babybunting.com.au/car-seats/capsules | language=en-au | access-date=11 November 2026}}</ref>

==Regulation==
In 1990, the [[International Organization for Standardization|ISO]] standard [[ISOFIX]]<ref>International Standard ISO 13216. Anchorages in vehicles and attachments to anchorages for child restraint systems. [[International Organization for Standardization]], Geneva.</ref> was launched in an attempt to provide a standard for fixing car seats into different makes of car. The standard now includes a [[top tether]]; the U.S. version of this system is called [[LATCH]]. Generally, the ISOFIX system can be used with Groups 0, 0+ and 1.

In 2013, a new car seat regulation was introduced: "'''i-Size'''" is the name of a new European safety regulation that affects car seats for children under 15 months of age. It came into effect in July 2013 and provides extra protection in several ways, most notably by providing rearward facing travel for children up to 15 months instead of 9 to 12 months, which the previous EU regulation advised.

There are several types of car seats, which vary in the position of the child and size of the seat. The [[United Nations]] European Regional standard ECE R44/04<ref>Regulation No 44 of the Economic Commission for Europe of the United Nations (UN/ECE) — [http://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:42011X0909(02) Uniform provisions concerning the approval of restraining devices for child occupants of power driven vehicles (‘child restraint systems’)] -  Retrieved 2014-08-04</ref> categorizes these into 4 groups: 0–3. Group 0 carrycots hold the baby lying on its back.  Carrycots are secured by both seat belts in the rear seat of the car. Both types have handles to allow them to be easily moved into and out of the car.{{cn|date=November 2026}}

*Recommended weight: Birth to {{convert|10|kg|lb|abbr=on}}
*Approximate age: Birth to 15 months

====Fastened carrycots====
''Carrycots'' or '' infant car beds'' are used for infants that cannot sit in a regular baby seat, such as premature infants or infants that suffer from apnea. A carrycot is a restraint system intended to accommodate and restrain the child in a [[supine position|supine]] or [[prone position]] with the child's spine perpendicular to the median longitudinal plane of the vehicle. Carrycots are designed to distribute the restraining forces over the child's head and body, excluding its [[Limb (anatomy)|limbs]], in the event of a big crash. It must be put on the rear seat of the car. Some models can be changed to face forward after the baby has reached the weight limit which is normally about {{convert|15|-|20|kg|lb|abbr=on}}.

Carrycots generally include a stomach belt and a connection to the (three points) [[safety belt]].

====Infant carriers====
[[Image:Rear-facing_infant_car_seat.jpg|thumb|Rear-facing infant car seat]]
Infant carrier means a [[Safety harness|restraint]] system intended to accommodate the child in a rearward-facing semi-[[wikt:recumbent|recumbent]] position. This design distributes the restraining forces over the child's head and body, excluding its [[Limb (anatomy)|limb]]s, in the event of the frontal collision.

For young infants, the seat used is an infant carrier with typical recommended weight range from {{convert|4|lb}} to {{convert|35|lb}}. Most infant seats made in the US can now be used up to at least {{convert|30|lb}} and {{convert|30|inch|cm}}, with some going up to {{convert|35|lb}}. In the past, most infant seats in the US went to {{convert|20|lb}} and {{convert|26|inch|cm}}. Infant carriers are often also called "Bucket Seats" as they resemble a bucket with a handle. Some (but not all) seats can be used with the base secured, or with the carrier strapped in alone. Some seats do not have bases. Infant carriers are mounted rear-facing and are designed to "cocoon" against the back of the vehicle seat in the event of a collision, with the impact being absorbed in the outer shell of the restraint. Rear-facing seats are deemed the safest, and in the US, laws vary and children must remain in this position until they are at least 1 year of age in most states and at least 2 years of age in many states. More states are implementing a 2-year minimum. It is always recommended to keep them rear-facing until at least 2 years old or until they outgrow the rear-facing car seat height and weight, whichever is longer.{{Citation needed|date=March 2020}}

===Group 0+===
Group 0+ car seats commonly have a [[chassis]] permanently fixed into the car by an adult [[seat belt]] and can be placed into some form of [[baby transport]] using the integral handle if it is the specific model. Rear-facing child seats are inherently safer than forward-facing child seats because they provide more support for the child's head in the event of a sudden deceleration. Although some parents are eager to switch to a forward-facing child seat because it seems more "grown up," various countries and car seat manufacturers recommend that children continue to use a rear-facing child seat for as long as physically possible.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.aap.org/advocacy/releases/carseat2011.htm|title=AAP Updates Recommendation on Car Seats|date=22 March 2011|access-date=27 May 2017|url-status=bot: unknown|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110322120421/http://www.aap.org/advocacy/releases/carseat2011.htm|archive-date=22 March 2011}}</ref>

*Position: Sitting, '''rear-facing''', '''no [[airbag]]'''(with the exception of curtain airbags).
*Recommended weight: '''Birth (2–3&nbsp;kg) to 13&nbsp;kg''' (29&nbsp;lb).
*Approximate age: Birth to 15 months

====Convertible seats====
[[File:Convertible Car Seat (Toddler).png|thumb|Convertible Car Seat (Toddler)]]

Convertible seats can be used throughout many stages. Many convertible seats will transition from a rear-facing seat, to a forward-facing seat, and some then can be used as a booster seat. Many convertible seats allow for {{convert|2.3|–|18|kg|lb|abbr=on}} rear-facing, allowing children to be in the safer rear-facing position up to a weight of {{convert|18|kg|lb|abbr=on}}.

Convertible safety seats can be installed as either rear-facing or forward-facing. There is a large selection available to choose from and weight limits, height limits, and extra features vary from seat to seat and by manufacturer. Seats with a 5-point harness are considered safer than those with an overhead shield.<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.cpsafety.com/articles/fivepointshield.aspx |title=Five-Point Harness or Shield—Choosing a safer seat |access-date=2012-04-17 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120421021103/http://www.cpsafety.com/articles/fivepointshield.aspx |archive-date=2012-04-21 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

Convertibles are not considered the best choice for a newborn because the bottom harness slots are often above the shoulders of most newborns. A seat with low-bottom harness slots can be used if it is desired to use a convertible from birth.

Rear-facing weight limits range from {{convert|9|to|23|kg|lb|abbr=on}} depending on the manufacturer and [[country of origin]]. Forward-facing limits range from {{convert|9|to|40|kg|lb|abbr=on}} depending on the seat model and the manufacturer and country of origin.

All convertible seats in the U.S. have at least a {{convert|35|lb}} rear-facing weight limit, most now to go to {{convert|40|lb}}, and some go to {{convert|50|lb}}. The [[American Academy of Pediatrics]] (AAP) recommends that children remain rear-facing until they outgrow their convertible seat, regardless of how old they are. Children can remain in a rear-facing seat until they have either outgrown the weight limit for their seat, or the top of their head is within {{convert|25|mm|inch|sigfig=1|abbr=on}} of the top of the shell of the car seat.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.aap.org/family/carseatguide.htm|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060216025350/http://www.aap.org/family/carseatguide.htm|url-status=dead|archive-date=16 February 2006|title=AAP—Car Safety Seats: A Guide for Families—2005|date=16 February 2006|access-date=16 May 2018}}</ref>

===Group 1===
A permanent fixture in the car using an adult seat belt to hold it in place and a five-point baby harness to hold the infant.

*Position: Sitting, recommended rear-facing but forward-facing is legal, '''no [[airbag]]''' (with the exception of curtain airbags).
*Recommended weight: '''9&nbsp;kg to 18&nbsp;kg''' (20&nbsp;lb to 40&nbsp;lb)
*Approximate age: 9 months to 4 years (Although older children can fit too sometimes)

===Group 2===
A larger seat than the Group 1 design. These seats use an adult seat belt to hold the child in place.

*Position: Sitting, forward-facing or rear-facing (make sure the seat is certified for up to {{convert|25|kg|lb|abbr=on}}
*Recommended weight: '''15&nbsp;kg to 25&nbsp;kg''' (33&nbsp;lb to 55&nbsp;lb)
*Approximate age: 4 to 6 years (Although older children can sometimes fit)

===Group 3===
Also known as booster seats, these position the child so that the adult seat belt is held in the correct position for safety and comfort.

*Position: Sitting, forward-facing
*Recommended weight: '''22&nbsp;kg to 36&nbsp;kg''' (48&nbsp;lb to 76&nbsp;lb)
*Approximate age: 4 to 10, and above if the child is not {{convert|36|kg|lb|abbr=on}} yet

====Booster seats====
{{Redirect|Booster seat|the song|Booster Seat}}
[[File:Kindersitz Sitzerhöhung.JPG|thumb|Backless booster seat]]
[[File:Jacobstarriser.jpg|thumb|upright=0.7|Child in a booster seat]]
Booster seats are recommended for children until they are big enough to properly use a [[seat belt]]. Seat belts are engineered for adults, and are thus too big for small children. In the United States, for children under the age of 4 and/or under {{convert|40|lb}}, a seat with a 5-point harness is suggested instead of a booster seat.<ref>{{Citation|url=http://www.nhtsa.dot.gov/CPS/newtips/pages/Tip2.htm|publisher=NHTSA DoT |place=[[United States of America|US]] |title=CPS |contribution=Tips |id=2 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080515214643/http://www.nhtsa.dot.gov/CPS/newtips/pages/Tip2.htm |archive-date=May 15, 2008}}</ref>

Booster seats lift the child and allow the seat belt to sit firmly across the collar bone and chest, with the lap portion fitted to the hips. If the seat belt is not across the collar bone and the hips, it will ride across the neck and the stomach and cause internal injuries in the event of a collision.

There are two main types of boosters: high back (some of which have energy absorbing foam) and no back. A new generation of booster seats comes with rigid [[Isofix]] (Latch) connectors that secure to the vehicle's anchors, improving the seat's stability in the event of a collision. There are also 3-in-1 car seats that can first be used as a rear-facing baby car seat, then as a forward-facing seat, then finally as a booster seat when the child reaches the recommended height and weight.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://importedautomobile.com/baby-car-seats/|title=Baby Car Seats—All The Information You Need To Find The Perfect One|date=2019-07-01|publisher=Imported Auto|language=en-US|access-date=2019-07-07|archive-date=October 9, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201009150232/https://importedautomobile.com/baby-car-seats/|url-status=dead}}</ref> Unlike many booster seats, 3-1 car seats may use the LATCH system to be secured inside the car.

Some consumer groups {{Which|date=October 2018}} are calling on manufacturers and retailers to phase out backless boosters, as it says they do not provide enough protection in side-impact crashes and could put children at risk.<ref>{{Citation|publisher=Which |place=[[United Kingdom|UK]] |url=http://www.which.co.uk/cars/safety/car-safety-and-child-seats/child-safety-week-2010/ |title=Car safety & child seats |contribution=Child safety week |year=2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120422094733/http://www.which.co.uk/cars/safety/car-safety-and-child-seats/child-safety-week-2010/ |archive-date=April 22, 2012 }}</ref> While backless booster cushions are better than using no child seat at all, they do not provide adequate protection in all circumstances.

====Front-facing restraints====
Used for Groups I, II and III.

After reaching one year of age and {{convert|20|lb|0}}, children may travel in forward-facing seats. Most Scandinavian countries require children to sit rear-facing until at least the age of 4 years. This has contributed to Sweden having the lowest rate of children killed in traffic in international comparisons.<ref>[https://injuryprevention.bmj.com/content/injuryprev/6/1/41.full.pdf ''Seat belts & child restraints'', International comparisons of child injuries and prevention programs: recommendations for an improved prevention program in Germany, August 4, 2014] Retrieved August 5, 2018</ref>

According to Canadian law, and some US states{{which|date=June 2024}}, children must be restrained until they are 4 years old and {{convert|40|lb}}.{{Citation needed|date=June 2024}} After the requirement is met, they may use a booster seat instead.

== Types ==
<!---there seems to be overlap between the above and this section - look at integrating?--->
[[Image:Child car seat.JPG|thumb|right|Child safety seat]]
There are different types of child safety seats for children of different sizes and ages.

* '''Infant seats''': Child safety seats made specifically for infants are the smallest and have carrying handles for easy carrying and loading. Newborns are most often placed in a rear-facing seat. These seats are designed for infants is up to {{convert|22|to|32|lb|kg}}, depending on the model.
* '''Convertible seats''': Similar to the infant seat, the convertible seat can be used in a rear- or forward-facing position and is used for children typically beginning at {{convert|5|lb|kg}} up to {{convert|50|lb|kg}}. The rear-facing position is used for children until they weigh more than {{convert|20|lb|kg}} and are at least two years old. Research studies and [[crash test]] results show that children are safer in a rear-facing child safety seat. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends keeping children in a rear-facing seat until "they are 2 years of age or until they reach the highest weight or height allowed by their car safety seat’s manufacturer" (www.healthychildren.org).

Some seats allow the child to stay rear-facing until {{convert|45|lb|kg}}. Once the child has reached the minimum requirements to be forward facing, the seat can be turned around and used as a forward-facing seat.{{citation needed|date=June 2015}}

* '''Combination seats''': The combination seat or five-point booster is a forward-facing seat that has a five-point harness system. It can be modified to a belt-positioning booster by removing some webbing<ref>NHTSA. "National Child Passenger Safety Certification Training Program Manual. Some combination seats must be converted to a booster after the child reaches {{convert|40|lb|kg}}, while others have much higher weight limits for use of the 5-point harness (e.g., up to {{convert|85|lb|kg}}). Children are safest in a five-point harness provided it is installed correctly and the child is within the height and weight limits for that particular seat." Safe Kids Worldwide, April 2007.</ref>
* '''Booster seats''': The earlier described combination seat can become a high-back belt-positioning booster. There is also a high-back belt positioning-booster that is available for that purpose only. The other type of belt-positioning booster is the low-back or no-back booster. The major differences between the low- and high-back booster seats are head support and improved protection in side-impact collisions. Vehicles with a bench seating and no headrest are required to use a high back booster. The purpose of the booster seats is to provide elevation to the child so the shoulder belt rests appropriately on the shoulder blade and does not ride up the child's neck. It also provides "hips" that the seat belt can rest securely across and remain on the large bones of the child instead of resting across the soft tissue of the abdomen. This seat is available for children over {{convert|40|lb|kg}} up to over {{convert|100|lb|kg}}.<ref name="NHTSA 2007">NHTSA. "National Child Passenger Safety Certification Training Program Manual." Safe Kids Worldwide, April 2007.</ref> From the ages of eight to twelve, children may have outgrown their booster seats and can be permitted to use regular adult seat restraints. It is suggested that, until the age of thirteen, the child remains in the back seat.

==Safety information==
Like [[motorcycle helmet|motorcycle]] and [[racing helmet|race car helmets]], child restraints are tested for use in just one crash event. This means that if the restraint is compromised in any way (with or without the child in it), owners are strongly suggested to replace it. This is due to the uncertainty with how a compromised child restraint will perform in subsequent crashes.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://ntf.se/konsumentupplysning/barn-i-bil/checklista-vid-kop/livslangd/ |title=Bilbarnstolars livslängd |quote=Bilbarnstolen ska alltid bytas ut om den har skadats i samband med krock på ett sätt som syns eller som påverkar funktionen i något avseende |publisher=Nationalföreningen för trafiksäkerhetens främjande |access-date=2021-05-15 }}</ref>

The [[National Highway Traffic Safety Administration|National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)]] provides guidance on the reuse of child restraint systems after a crash. Replacement of child restraints is recommended following a moderate or severe crash in order to ensure a continued high level of protection for child passengers. However, recent studies demonstrate that child restraints can withstand minor crash impacts without any documented degradation in subsequent performance.<ref name="NHTSA">{{cite web|title=NHTSA Advises Parents, Caregivers: Child Safety Seats Can Be Reused after Minor Crash|url=http://www.nhtsa.gov/About+NHTSA/Press+Releases/2004/NHTSA+Advises+Parents,+Caregivers:+Child+Safety+Seats+Can+Be+Reused+after+Minor+Crash|website=National Highway Traffic Safety Administration|publisher=U.S. Department of Transportation|access-date=2 August 2016|archive-date=October 5, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161005114549/http://www.nhtsa.gov/About%20NHTSA/Press%20Releases/2004/NHTSA%20Advises%20Parents%2C%20Caregivers%3A%20Child%20Safety%20Seats%20Can%20Be%20Reused%20after%20Minor%20Crash|url-status=dead}} {{PD-notice}}</ref>

A minor crash is defined by the NHTSA as one in which all of the following apply:
* A visual inspection of the child safety seat, including inspection under any easily movable seat padding, does not reveal any cracking over 2 inches that might have been caused by the crash
* The vehicle in which the child safety seat was installed was capable of being driven from the scene of the crash
* The vehicle door nearest the child safety seat was undamaged
* There were no injuries to any of the vehicle occupants
* The air bags (if any) did not deploy
Crashes that meet all of these criteria are much less severe than the dynamic testing requirement for compliance with [[Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards|Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard (FMVSS) 213]] and are highly unlikely to affect future child safety seat performance.<ref name="NHTSA"/>

Child restraints are sometimes the subject of manufacturing recalls.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www-odi.nhtsa.dot.gov/cars/problems/recalls/childseat.cfm|title=Keeping You Safe—Safercar|publisher=National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA)|access-date=27 May 2017|archive-date=September 1, 2006|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060901201917/http://www-odi.nhtsa.dot.gov/cars/problems/recalls/childseat.cfm|url-status=dead}}</ref> Recalls vary in severity; sometimes the manufacturer will send an additional part for the seat, other times they will provide an entirely new seat.

The purchase of a used seat is not recommended. Due to the aforementioned concerns regarding expiry dates, crash testing, and recalls, it is often impossible to determine the history of the child restraint when it is purchased second-hand.

Children traveling by plane are safer in a child safety seat than in a parent's arms. The FAA and the AAP recommend that all children under 40&nbsp;lb use a child safety seat on a plane. Booster seats cannot be used on airplanes because they do not have shoulder belts.

Parents should not put children into safety seats with thick winter coats on. The coat will cushion the child in an accident and the straps will not cause damage and keep the child safe.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.consumerreports.org/cro/news/2012/12/winter-coats-and-car-seats-keeping-your-child-safe-and-warm/index.htm |title=Winter coats and car seats: Keeping your child safe and warm |publisher=Consumer Reports |date= December 20, 2012<!-- 10:08 AM -->|access-date=2013-09-19}}</ref> An alternative would be placing a coat on the child backwards after buckling the child in.

Straps on the harness should be snug on the child, parents should not be able to pinch the straps away from the shoulders of the child. The straps also need to be placed at the proper height for the child.

==Seat placement==
A study of car crash data from 16 U.S. states found that children under the age of 3 were 43% less likely to be injured in a car crash if their car seat was fastened in the center of the back seat rather than on one side. Results were based on data from 4,790 car crashes involving children aged 3 and younger between 1998 and 2006. According to data, the center position was the safest but least used position.

The move from having car seats in the front passenger seat to having them in the back seat, facing backwards, may make it easier for parent or caregiver to [[forgotten baby syndrome|forget an infant in the car]].<ref name=FBS /> Each year, between 30 and 50 infants die of [[heat illness]] and [[hyperthermia]] in the United States after being left in a car.<ref name=FBS >{{cite news |date=March 26, 2009 |title=The Last Word: Forgotten Baby Syndrome |journal=The Week |url=http://www.theweek.com/article/index/94741/The_last_word_Forgotten_baby_syndrome |access-date=April 17, 2012 |archive-date=July 5, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140705171240/http://theweek.com/article/index/94741/The_last_word_Forgotten_baby_syndrome |url-status=dead }}</ref>

== Law ==
[[File:Stamp-russia2016-child-safety-partblock.png|thumb|right|Child safety postage stamp of Russia showing a child safety seat]]
Baby car seats are legally required in many countries, including most Western [[Developed country|developed countries]], to safely transport children up to the age of 2 or more years in [[car]]s and other [[vehicle]]s.

Other car seats, also known as "booster seats," are required until the child is large enough to use an adult seat belt. This is usually, but not always, when the child is {{cvt|135|cm|ft in}} <ref name="ec.europa.eu">{{cite web |url=https://ec.europa.eu/transport/road_safety/users/children_en |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161109173624/http://ec.europa.eu/transport/road_safety/users/children_en |archive-date=November 9, 2016 |title=Children—European Commission}}</ref> tall. The child needs to meet five criteria before moving out of the booster seat, including the child's seating position, shoulder belt position, lap belt position, knee position, and ability to sit properly for the length of the trip.

Generally, countries that regulate [[Automobile safety|passenger safety]] have child safety laws that require a child to be restrained appropriately depending on their age and weight. These regulations and standards are often minimums, and with each graduation to the next kind of safety seat, there is a step down in the amount of protection a child has in a collision.<ref>{{Cite web|title = Car Seats {{!}} Parents Central {{!}} Keeping Kids Safe|url = http://www.safercar.gov/parents/CarSeats/Right-Seat-Age-And-Size-Recommendations.htm|website = Safercar.gov|access-date = 2016-02-21|archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20160219202235/http://www.safercar.gov/parents/CarSeats/Right-Seat-Age-And-Size-Recommendations.htm|archive-date = 2016-02-19|url-status = dead}}</ref> Some countries, such as Australia and the United States, forbid rear-facing child seats in a front seat that has an airbag. A rear-facing infant restraint put in the front seat of a vehicle places an infant's head close to the airbag, which can cause severe head injuries or death if the airbag deploys. Some modern cars include a switch to disable the front passenger airbag for child-supporting seat use.

=== European Union and UNECE regulations===
[[Directive (European Union)|Directive]] 2003/20/EC of the European Parliament and the Council<ref>Directive 2003/20/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 April 2003 amending Council Directive 91/671/EEC, Official Journal L 115, 2003-05-09 P. 63.</ref> has mandated the use of child-restraint systems in vehicles effective May 5, 2006. Children less than {{convert|135|cm|in}} tall in vehicles must be restrained by an approved child restraint system suitable for the child's size.<ref>{{cite web|title=European Commission Rules on Seat Belts|url=http://ec.europa.eu/transport/road_safety/topics/vehicles/seat_belts/index_en.htm|website=European Commission|access-date=2 August 2016}}</ref> In practice, child restraint systems must be able to be fitted to the front, or other rows of seats. 

For a child restraint to be sold or used within any of the 56 UNECE member states it must be approved by the standards of UNECE Regulation 44/04, Directive 77/541/EEC or any other subsequent adaptation thereto. In order to be granted ECE R44 approval the child restraint must comply with several design, construction and production conformity standards.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.childseatcentre.com/child-car-seat-regulations/|title=Child car seat design and production regulations|website=childseatcentre.com|access-date=27 May 2017|archive-date=October 9, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201009150248/http://childseatcentre.com/child-car-seat-regulations/|url-status=dead}}</ref> If approval is granted the seat can display an orange label with the unique approval license number, the type of approval, the mass group approved for and the details of the manufacturer.

However, until May 9, 2008, member states may have permitted the use of child restraint systems approved in accordance with their national standards. [[Euro NCAP]] has developed a child-safety-protection rating to encourage improved designs. Points are awarded for universal child-restraint anchorages [[ISOFIX]], the quality of warning labels and deactivation systems for front-passenger airbags.

2013: New EU I-Size regulation is introduced: "i-Size" is the name of a new European safety regulation, UNECE Regulation 129 that affects car seats for children under 15 months of age. It came into effect in July 2013 and provides extra protection in several ways, most notably by providing rearward facing travel for children up to 15 months instead of 9 to 12 months, which the previous EU regulation advised. This new regulation is to be phased in between 2013 and 2018 and will be run in parallel to UNECE R44/04 until 2018 when it completely supersedes it.

===Australia===
[[Image:Australianstandard1.gif|thumb|AS1754]]

Australian laws regarding infants in motor vehicles were revised on November 9, 2009.{{citation needed|date=February 2016}}

By law every child restraint sold in Australia must carry the Australian Standard AS/NZ1754 sticker (pictured right).
Most overseas child restraints, including restraints from Europe and the US, do not comply with these Standards and cannot legally be used in Australia. This also applies for ISOFIX child restraints imported from Europe or the USA.

* Children under seven must be restrained in a suitable, approved child restraint or booster seat.
* Children under six months must be restrained in a rear-facing position.
* Children between six months and under four years must be restrained in a rear- or forward-facing restraint.
* Children between four and under seven must be restrained in a forward-facing restraint or booster seat.

In Australia there are six different types (Type A to Type F) of child restraints under the mandatory standard. Note: these restraints are NOT based on weight but on HEIGHT. All car seats with the AS/NZ1754 sticker will have height markers. These markers show clearly for what height the seat is appropriate.

The six types are:
# Type A: Rearward-facing or transversely installed restraint with a harness or other means of holding the child back 
# Type B: Forward-facing chair with a harness
# Type C: Forward-facing harness without a chair
# Type D: Rearward-facing chair with harness
# Type E: a restraint that consists of either: 
#* a booster seat or booster cushion used with a Type C child restraint and a seat belt, or with a lap-sash seat belt, 
#* a converter seat suitable for children who weigh {{convert|18|to|32|kg|lb|abbr=on}}.
# Type F: A restraint that consists of either:
#* a booster seat used in conjunction with a Type C child restraint and a seatbelt, or with a lap-sash seatbelt, suitable for children approximately 4 to 10 years of age, or
#* a converter used in conjunction with a seatbelt, suitable for children approximately 8 to 10 years of age.

Combination Type A/B: Child restraints can also be a combination of the above types. For example, a Type A/B converter seat.

The responsibility for children under the age of 16 using restraints or safety belts correctly rests with the driver. In Queensland, penalties for drivers not ensuring that passengers under the age of 16 are properly restrained involve a fine of A$300 and three [[demerit point]]s. In Victoria the penalty is a fine of A$234 and three demerit points.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.vicroads.vic.gov.au/safety-and-road-rules/road-rules/a-to-z-of-road-rules/seat-belts-and-child-restraints/ |title=Seat belts & child restraints |publisher=[[VicRoads]] |date=August 4, 2014 |access-date=August 5, 2018}}</ref> Possible suspension or cancellation of license may also apply.

====Exemptions to the law====
* Laws regarding taxis vary by state for infants. For children up to seven, a child restraint must be used if available, otherwise the child must use a properly fastened and adjusted seat belt.{{citation needed|date=February 2015}}
* A child traveling in a police or [[emergency vehicle]].
* If a child has a medical condition or [[physical disability]] that makes it impractical to use a child restraint and the driver has a certificate from a doctor indicating this is the case.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.rta.nsw.gov.au/roadsafety/children/childrestraints/index.html|title=Child restraints < Children < NSW Centre for Road Safety |date=5 March 2013|access-date=27 May 2017|url-status=bot: unknown|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130305075602/http://www.rta.nsw.gov.au/roadsafety/children/childrestraints/index.html|archive-date=5 March 2013}}</ref>

== Manufacturing ==
Though there are hundreds of variations of makes and models in the world of child safety seats, the materials used in the manufacturing process are basically the same. Factories in which the seats are put together receive loads of [[polypropylene]] pellets.<ref name=AAP>AAP. "Car Safety Seats: A Guide For Families 2009." American Academy of Pediatrics. 2009. American Academy of Pediatrics. 22 Feb. 2009".</ref> Foam makes up the padding of the individual seats, while vinyl and fabrics are used to make up the covers for the seats as well as the harnesses.

A safety seat increases the safety of a properly restrained child in the case of a motor vehicle accident. The safety seat includes foam padding, fabric covers, a harness, and buckles or attaching mechanisms. Labels and instructions are also attached. Every child safety seat will have an expiration date on it. The Safe Kids USA organization does not recommend using a child safety seat that is more than 6 years old.{{citation needed|date=April 2014}} Periodically, child safety seats are recalled by manufacturers for safety reasons. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration posts a link to recent recall information at its website.

== Hazards ==
Manufacturers have quality controls to ensure seats are properly put together and packaged. However, it is not guaranteed that the included instructions are always adhered to and correctly followed. Up to 95% of the safety seats that are installed may not be the right seat for the child, may be hooked into the vehicle loosely, may be hooked with an incompatible belt in the vehicle, may have harnesses incorrectly fastened in some way, or may be incorrectly placed in front of air bags. In 1997, six out of ten children who were killed in vehicle crashes were not correctly restrained.<ref name=AAP/>

[[File:Maui Police Department Child Safety Seat Inspection 1.JPG|thumb|right|200px|Child Safety Seat Inspection site by the [[Maui County Police Department|Maui Police Department]]]]

Along with the problem of instructions not being followed properly, there are other hazards that can affect children involving these safety seats. A recent study{{Clarify|date=April 2011}} attributed many cases of [[sudden infant death syndrome]] (SIDS) to the prolonged sitting or lying position these infants are in when putting the safety seats to use. When researchers reviewed more than 500 infant deaths, it was found that 17 of these deaths occurred while the infant was in a device such as a child safety seat. The age of the most occurring rates of death by SIDS in a child safety device was found to be under one month, having six of the 17 deaths happen in this age group. Although SIDS has been found to be a high risk regarding child safety seats, a coroner in Quebec also stated that "putting infants in car seats…causes breathing problems and should be discouraged."<ref name=howitsmade>{{cite web|url=http://www.madehow.com/Volume-5/Child-Safety-Seat.html|title=How child safety seat is made—manufacture, history, used, parts, components, product, industry, History, Raw Materials|website=Madehow.com|access-date=16 May 2018}}</ref> His warning came after the death of a two-month-old boy who was left to nap in a child safety seat positioned inside his crib rather than the crib itself. The death was linked to positional [[asphyxiation]].<ref name=howitsmade/> This means that the child was in a position causing him to slowly lose his supply of oxygen. Coroner Jacques Robinson said it is common for a baby's head to slump forward while in a car seat that is not properly installed in a car and that can diminish a baby's ability to take in oxygen. "The car seat is for the car," he said. "It's not for a bed or sleeping."  Robinson added, however, he has nothing against car seats when they are properly used. The coroner said that it is common for a baby's head to "slump forward while in a car seat and that it diminishes oxygen".<ref name=howitsmade/>

The [[American Academy of Pediatrics]] says to "make sure the seat is at the correct angle so your infant’s head does not flop forward. Many seats have angle indicators or adjusters that can help prevent this. If your seat does not have an angle adjuster, tilt the car safety seat back by putting a rolled towel or other firm padding (such as a [[pool noodle]]) under the base near the point where the back and bottom of the vehicle seat meet." Safety seats come with an instruction booklet with additional information on the appropriate angle for the seat.

==Criticism==
Child safety seats offer a considerable safety advantage over seat belts alone, when looking at data about collisions in newer cars with newer child safety seats, without restricting the data to include only fatalities.<ref>{{cite web|last1=Elliot|first1=Michael R. |display-authors=etal |title=Effectiveness of Child Safety Seats vs Seat Belts in Reducing Risk for Death in Children in Passenger Vehicle Crashes|url=http://archpedi.jamanetwork.com/article.aspx?articleid=205063|website=JAMA Pediatrics|access-date=9 June 2015}}</ref>

Child safety seats can be expensive and bulky, and if the family has multiple children who need to use child safety seats at the same time, it may even be necessary to buy a larger vehicle or specialized, narrower safety seats to comply with legal requirements about transporting children safely. One economic study found that these requirements affected families' decisions about [[birth spacing]], and estimated that, in 2017, the requirements for fitting multiple child safety seats into a vehicle at the same time had likely discouraged several thousand families from having an additional child at that time.<ref>{{Cite web|last1=Nickerson|first1=Jordan|last2=Solomon|first2=David H.|date=2020-07-31|title=Car Seats as Contraception|url=https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3665046|language=en|location=Rochester, NY|doi=10.2139/ssrn.3665046|ssrn=3665046|s2cid=229069738|url-access=subscription}}</ref> A major challenge to child restraint use is that it is considered as a mechanism to discipline children instead of being seen as a safety device by parents and as children became older they actively seek opportunities to negotiate the non-usage of restraints.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Bhaumik|first1=Soumyadeep|last2=Hunter|first2=Kate|last3=Matzopoulos|first3=Richard|last4=Prinsloo|first4=Megan|last5=Ivers|first5=Rebecca Q.|last6=Peden|first6=Margaret|date=October 2020|title=Facilitators and barriers to child restraint use in motor vehicles: a qualitative evidence synthesis|journal=Injury Prevention|volume=26|issue=5|pages=478–493|doi=10.1136/injuryprev-2020-043655|issn=1475-5785|pmid=32447304|s2cid=218874010}}</ref>
