An '''aquarium''' ({{plural form}}: '''aquariums''' or '''aquaria''') is a [[vivarium]] of any size having at least one transparent side in which aquatic plants or animals are kept and displayed. [[fishkeeping|Fishkeepers]] use aquaria to keep [[fish]], [[invertebrate]]s, [[amphibian]]s, aquatic [[reptile]]s, such as [[turtle]]s, and [[aquatic plant]]s. The term ''aquarium'', coined by English naturalist [[Philip Henry Gosse]], combines the [[Latin]] root {{wikt-lang|la|aqua}}, meaning "water," with the suffix {{wikt-lang|la|-arium}}, meaning "a place for relating to or connected with."<ref>{{cite Merriam-Webster |aquarium |access-date=2007-04-03}} {{Cite web |url=http://www.m-w.com/dictionary/aquarium |title=Definition of aquarium - Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary |access-date=2007-04-03 |archive-date=2007-04-04 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070404103239/http://www.m-w.com/dictionary/aquarium |url-status=live}}</ref>

The aquarium principle was fully developed in 1850 by the chemist [[Robert Warington]], who explained that aquatic plants placed in a container of water could produce enough [[oxygen]] to support animals, so long as the numbers of animals did not grow too large.<ref name="Grier"/> The aquarium craze was launched in early [[Victorian era|Victorian England]] by Gosse, who created and stocked the first [[public aquarium]] at the [[London Zoo]] in 1853, and published the first manual, ''The Aquarium: An Unveiling of the Wonders of the Deep Sea'' in 1854.<ref name="Grier">{{cite book |first=Katherine C. |last=Grier |year=2008 |title=Pets in America: A History |page=53 |publisher=[[University of North Carolina Press]]}}</ref> Small aquariums are kept in the home by [[hobby]]ists. There are large public aquariums in many cities. Public aquariums keep fish and other [[aquatic animal]]s in large tanks. A large aquarium may have [[otter]]s,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Sea Otters |url=https://www.montereybayaquarium.org/visit/exhibits/sea-otters |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=[[Monterey Bay Aquarium]]}}</ref> [[dolphin]]s,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Dolphins |url=https://www.cmaquarium.org/animals/dolphins/ |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=[[Clearwater Marine Aquarium]] |language=en-US}}</ref> [[shark]]s,<ref>{{Cite web |last=Kaplan |first=Mike |title=10 Largest Aquariums in the World |url=https://www.touropia.com/largest-aquariums-in-the-world/ |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=Touropia |date=16 February 2020 |language=en-US}}</ref> [[penguin]]s,<ref>{{Cite web |last=Baker |first=Lev |date=2023-02-28 |title=Discover 5 Incredible Aquariums and Zoos with Penguins |url=https://a-z-animals.com/blog/discover-5-incredible-aquariums-and-zoos-with-penguins/ |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=AZ Animals |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=Penguins |url=https://www.montereybayaquarium.org/animals/live-cams/penguin-cam |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=www.montereybayaquarium.org}}</ref> [[Pinniped|seal]]s,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Pinnipeds in captivity |url=https://www.pinnipeds.org/seal-information/rehabilitation-and-captivity/pinnipeds-in-captivity |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=www.pinnipeds.org}}</ref> and [[whale]]s.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Marine mammals in captivity |url=https://www.humanesociety.org/resources/marine-mammals-captivity |access-date=2023-05-01 |website=The Humane Society of the United States |language=en}}</ref> Many aquarium tanks also have plants.

An [[aquarist]] owns fish or maintains an aquarium, typically constructed of glass or high-strength [[Acrylate polymer|acrylic]]. Aquaria with flat walls are known as '''fish tanks''' or simply tanks, while those with rounded walls are known as '''fish bowls'''. Size can range from a small glass bowl, a few liters in volume, to immense public aquaria of thousands of liters. Specialized equipment maintains appropriate water quality and other characteristics suitable for the aquarium's residents.

==History and popularization==
[[File:Cat by Koryusai.jpg|thumb|Cat and fishbowl, by [[Koryūsai|Isoda Koryusai]]. Original {{circa|1775}}.]]

===Antiquity===
In 1369, the [[Hongwu Emperor]] of China established a [[porcelain]] company that produced large porcelain tubs for maintaining [[goldfish]]; over time, people produced tubs that approached the shape of modern fish bowls.<ref>{{cite book |last = Brunner | first = Bernd | title = The Ocean at Home | publisher = Princeton Architectural Press | year = 2003 | location = New York | isbn = 1-56898-502-9 | pages = [https://archive.org/details/oceanathomeil00brun/page/21 21–22] | url = https://archive.org/details/oceanathomeil00brun/page/21}}</ref> [[Leonhard Baldner]], who wrote ''Vogel-, Fisch- und Tierbuch'' (Bird, Fish, and Animal Book) in 1666, maintained [[weather loach]]es and [[newt]]s.<ref name = "duqmbv">Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 25</ref> It is sometimes held that the aquarium was invented by the Romans, who are said to have kept [[Mullus barbatus|sea barbels]] in marble-and-glass tanks, but scholars doubt the veracity of this.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.wetwebmedia.com/MythRomanAqKlee.htm|title=Myth Roman Aq Klee|website=www.wetwebmedia.com|access-date=2018-10-25}}</ref>

===Nineteenth century===
[[File:Waldmüller - Die Schauspielerin Theres Krones.jpeg|thumb|left|upright|Goldfish in a glass: portrait of [[Therese Krones]], 1824]]
[[File:1856aquariumfresh.png|thumb|An aquarium of the 1850s containing ''[[Vallisneria spiralis]]'' and [[coldwater fish]] from ''The Book of the Aquarium and Water Cabinet'' (London 1856), by [[Shirley Hibberd]].]]

In 1832, [[Jeanne Villepreux-Power]], a pioneering French marine biologist, became the first person to create aquaria for experimenting with aquatic organisms.<ref>{{cite book |last=Wertheimer|first=Douglas|title=Philip Henry Gosse: A Biography|url=https://search.worldcat.org/title/1495001167?oclcNum=1495001167|year=2024|publisher=Brethren Archivists and Historians Network, pp.191-5, gives an overview of the various individuals claimed to be "the" inventor of the aquarium |isbn=978-1739128326}}</ref> This experimentation led to several discoveries, including the first direct evidence that [[Argonaut (animal)|argonauts]], a marine [[cephalopod]], create their own shells.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Scales |first=Helen |title=Spirals in time: the secret life and curious afterlife of seashells |date=2015 |publisher=Bloomsbury Sigma |isbn=978-1-4729-1136-0 |series=Bloomsbury Sigma |location=London}}</ref> In 1836, soon after his invention of the [[Wardian case]], Dr. [[Nathaniel Bagshaw Ward]] proposed to use his tanks for tropical animals. In 1841 he did so, though only with aquatic plants and toy fish. However, he soon housed real animals. In 1838, [[Félix Dujardin]] noted owning a [[saltwater aquarium]], though he did not use the term.<ref name=Brunner35>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 35</ref> In 1846, [[Anne Thynne]] maintained [[Scleractinia|stony corals]] and [[seaweed]] for almost three years, and was credited as the creator of the first balanced marine aquarium in London.<ref>{{cite journal | url= https://books.google.com/books?id=CrBMAAAAYAAJ&q=anne%20thynne%20zoology&pg=PA449| journal= Annals and Magazine of Natural History | title= On the increase of ''Madrepores'' | pages=449–461 | year=1859 | volume=3 | number=29 | publisher=Taylor and Francis | location=London }}</ref><ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', pages 35–36</ref> English chemist [[Robert Warington]] experimented with a 13-gallon container, which contained [[goldfish]], [[Vallisneria|eelgrass]], and [[snail]]s, creating one of the first stable aquaria. The aquarium principle was fully developed by Warington, explaining that plants added to water in a container would give off enough oxygen to support animals, so long as their numbers do not grow too large.<ref name="Grier"/> He published his findings in 1850 in the [[Chemical Society]]'s [[scientific journal|journal]].<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 36</ref>

[[File:L'aquarium; vue intérieure.jpg|thumb|left|The ''Jardin zoologique'' at the [[Bois de Boulogne]] included an aquarium that housed both fresh and saltwater animals, 1860 in Paris.]]
The keeping of fish in an aquarium became a popular [[hobby]] and spread quickly. In the United Kingdom, it became popular after ornate aquaria in cast-iron frames were featured at the [[The Great Exhibition|Great Exhibition]] of 1851. In 1853, the aquarium craze was launched in England, spreading from there to Germany, the United States and France as the result of the publications and activity of [[Philip Henry Gosse]], the marine zoologist known as the "Father of the Aquarium".<ref>Wertheimer, D.: ''Philip Henry Gosse: A Biography'', pp.194, 208–212.</ref> He created and stocked the first public aquarium at the [[London Zoo]] in Regent's Park, which came to be known as the Fish House.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Oceans at Home'', pages 99</ref> The Regent's Park aquarium, initially indiscriminately referred to as the "Fish House", "Vivarium", "Aquavivarium" or "Marine vivarium", soon yielded to the word "aquarium", a term coined by Gosse<ref>Wertheimer, D.: ''Philip Henry Gosse: A Biography'', page 208 fn.264.</ref> used as the title of his 1854 book ''The Aquarium: An Unveiling of the Wonders of the Deep Water''.<ref name="Grier"/> In this book, Gosse primarily discussed saltwater aquaria.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 38</ref> The high-water mark of the popular aquarium movement in Britain lasted from 1853 to 1860.<ref>Wertheimer, D.: ''Philip Henry Gosse: A Biography'', page 241 fn.472</ref>
Tank designs and techniques for maintaining water quality were developed by Warington, later cooperating with Gosse until his critical review of the tank water composition. [[Edward Edwards (zoologist)|Edward Edwards]] developed these glass-fronted aquaria in his 1858 patent for a "dark-water-chamber slope-back tank", with water slowly circulating to a reservoir beneath.<ref name="Alexander">{{cite web|url=http://www.parlouraquariums.org.uk/Papers/b%20AQUARIUM%20HISTORY/History%20Papers/Parlour%20aquarum.htm |title=The first Parlour Aquariums and the Victorian Aquarium Craze |last=Alexander |first=Bob |date=November 2005 |work=History of parlour aquarium |publisher=parlouraquariums.org |access-date=24 December 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110722221227/http://www.parlouraquariums.org.uk/Papers/b%20AQUARIUM%20HISTORY/History%20Papers/Parlour%20aquarum.htm |archive-date=22 July 2011 }}</ref>
[[File:What an Aquarium Should Be.png|thumb|upright|"What an Aquarium Should Be" – a humorous 1876 British engraving, apparently showing [[Thomas Huxley]] dreaming about sea creatures]]

Influenced by Gosse, the German [[Emil Adolf Rossmässler]] promoted the value of the aquarium movement in the educational field. Rossmässler wrote of its use in an 1855 article in ''Die Gartenlaube'' (''The Gazebo'') and in his 1857 book ''Das Susswasser-Aquarium'' (''The Freshwater Aquarium''),<ref>Wertheimer, D.: ''Philip Henry Gosse: A Biography'', page 209–210</ref> the freshwater aquarium being much easier to maintain in landlocked areas.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', pages 60–61</ref> In 1862 [[William Alford Lloyd]], then bankrupt because of the craze in England being over, moved to Grindel Dammthor, Hamburg, to supervise the installation of the circulating system and tanks at the [[Tierpark Hagenbeck|Hamburg Aquarium]].{{Citation needed|date=January 2014}} During the 1870s, some of the first [[Aquarium fish clubs|aquarist societies]] were appearing in Germany.<ref name=Brunner75>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 75</ref> The United States soon followed. Published in 1858, Henry D. Butler's ''The Family Aquarium'' was one of the first books written in the United States solely about the aquarium.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 69</ref> According to the July issue of ''The North American Review'' of the same year, William Stimson may have owned some of the first functional aquaria, and had as many as seven or eight.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 71</ref> [[Henry Bishop (bird man and goldfish king)|Henry Bishop]], a bird and fish dealer in Baltimore ("Goldfish King"), is credited with revolutionizing the aquarium business in the US, selling a wide range of tanks and supplies beginning in the 1870s-1880s.<ref>{{cite news |last=Kelly |first=John |title=Breeding ponds in College Park, Md., once kept the U.S. awash in goldfish |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=December 2, 2017 |access-date=2025-08-01 |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/local/breeding-ponds-in-college-park-md-once-kept-the-us-awash-in-goldfish/2017/12/01/b4ea8568-d6e4-11e7-a986-d0a9770d9a3e_story.html }}</ref> The first aquarist society in the United States was founded in [[New York City]] in 1893, followed by others.<ref name=Brunner75/> The ''New York Aquarium Journal'', first published in October 1876, is considered to be the world's first aquarium magazine.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', pages 76–77</ref>
[[File:AntiqueFiskeAquarium.jpg|thumb|upright|left|An antique cast-iron aquarium made by [[J. W. Fiske & Company]] in the 1880s, New York City<ref>{{Cite journal|url = https://people.uncw.edu/pawlikj/2007TFHPawlik.pdf|title = Cast iron beauty: an antique J.W. Fiske Aquarium|last = Pawlik|first = JR|date = 2007|journal = Tropical Fish Hobbyist|volume = 55|pages = 111–114}}</ref>]]

In the [[Victorian era]] in the United Kingdom, a common design for the home aquarium was a glass front with the other sides made of wood (made watertight with a [[pitch (resin)|pitch]] coating). The bottom would be made of [[slate]] and heated from below.<ref name=Sanford>{{cite book | title = Aquarium Owner's Guide | last = Sanford | first = Gina | publisher = [[DK Publishing]] | location = New York | year = 1999 | pages = [https://archive.org/details/aquariumownersgu00sand/page/9 9–13] | isbn = 0-7894-4614-6 | url = https://archive.org/details/aquariumownersgu00sand/page/9 }}</ref> More advanced systems soon began to be introduced, along with tanks of glass in metal frames.<ref name=Sanford/> During the latter half of the 19th century, a variety of aquarium designs were explored, such as hanging the aquarium on a wall, mounting it as part of a [[window]], or even combining it with a [[birdcage]].<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', pages 86–89</ref>

===Twentieth century===
{{Update|section|date=June 2019}}
Around 1908, the first mechanical aquarium air pump was invented, powered by running water, instead of electricity.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://reefkeeping.com/issues/2004-09/rv/feature/index.php |title=A History of the Hobby by Roger Vitko |publisher=Reefkeeping.com |access-date=2012-06-28}}</ref> The introduction of the air pump into the hobby is considered by several historians of the hobby to be a pivotal moment in its development.<ref>Whittall, Robin. "How Did It All Begin? The Roots Of Captive Fishkeeping From The Victorians' Perspective." 1999</ref>
[[File:Pike aquarium c1908.jpg|thumb|[[Esox|Pike]] in an aquarium {{circa|1908}}, at the [[Belle Isle Aquarium]], [[Belle Isle Park]], Detroit, Michigan]]

Aquaria became more widely popular as houses had an electricity supply after [[World War I]]. [[Electricity]] allowed artificial lighting, as well as [[aeration]], [[filtration]], and heating of the water.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 93</ref> Initially, amateur [[Aquarist|aquarists]] kept native fish (with the exception of [[goldfish]]); the availability of exotic species from overseas further increased the popularity of the aquarium.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 78</ref> Jugs made from a variety of materials were used to import fish from overseas, with a [[bicycle pump|bicycle foot pump]] for aeration.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', pages 82–83</ref> Plastic shipping bags were introduced in the 1950s, making it easier to ship fish.<ref>Brunner, B: ''The Ocean at Home'', page 82</ref> The eventual availability of air freight allowed fish to be successfully imported from distant regions.<ref name = "duqmbv"/> Popular publications started by [[Herbert R. Axelrod]] influenced many more hobbyists to start keeping fish.<ref name=Fromson>{{cite news|first=Daniel|last=Fromson|title=Herbert R. Axelrod, a Hustler who Built a Fortune on a Fish Tale|journal=The New York Times Magazine|date=31 December 2017|pages=43–44}}</ref> In the 1960s, metal frames made [[marine aquarium|marine aquaria]] almost impossible due to corrosion, but the development of [[tar]] and [[silicone]] sealant allowed the first all-glass aquaria made by Martin Horowitz in Los Angeles, CA. The frames remained, however, though purely for aesthetic reasons.<ref name=Sanford/>

Japan played an increasingly important role in shaping aquarium design in the latter part of the twentieth century, with the [[aquascaping]] designs of [[Takashi Amano]] influencing fishkeepers to treat home aquariums as aesthetically pleasing compositions, rather than simply as a way of displaying fish specimens.<ref>[[Herbert R. Axelrod|Axelrod, Herbert R.]], Warren E. Burgess, Neal Pronek, Glen S. Axelrod and David E. Boruchowitz (1998), ''Aquarium Fishes of the World'', Neptune City, N.J.: T.F.H. Publications, p. 718, {{ISBN|0-7938-0493-0}}.</ref>

In the United States, as of 1996, [[Fishkeeping|aquarium keeping]] is the second-most popular hobby after [[stamp collecting]].<ref name="baensch">{{cite book |last1=Riehl |first1=Rüdiger |last2=Baensch |first2=Hans A. |title=Aquarium Atlas |year=1996 |edition=5th |publisher=Tetra Press |location=Germany |isbn=3-88244-050-3}}</ref> In 1999, an estimated 9.6 million US households owned an aquarium.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://directmag.com/mag/marketing_aquarium_hobbyists/|title=Aquarium Hobbyists|last=Emerson|first=Jim|date=1999-08-01 |url-status =dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20041012082340/http://directmag.com/mag/marketing_aquarium_hobbyists/ |archive-date = 12 October 2004|website = directmag.com}}</ref> Figures from the 2005/2006 APPMA National Pet Owners Survey report that Americans own approximately 139 million freshwater fish and 9.6 million [[saltwater fish]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.appma.org/newsletter/may2005/npos.html|title=National Pet Owners Survey|publisher=American Pet Products Manufacturers Association|year=2005|access-date=2007-05-02|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070406080935/http://www.appma.org/newsletter/may2005/npos.html|archive-date=6 April 2007|url-status=usurped}}</ref> Estimates of the numbers of fish kept in aquaria in Germany suggest at least 36 million.<ref name="baensch"/> The hobby has the strongest following in Europe, Asia, and North America. In the United States, 40% of [[Aquarist|aquarists]] maintain two or more tanks.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://unity.edu/careers/what-is-an-aquarist-and-how-to-become-one/ |title=What is an Aquarist And How to Become One |publisher=aquarist}}</ref>

Over time, there has been an increasing appreciation of the usefulness of access to an aquarium to provide potential [[stress (biology)|stress]] reduction and improvement of [[mood (psychology)|mood]] in people observing aquatic life.<ref name="bbc">{{cite web |author=Mark Kinver |title=Aquariums 'deliver significant health benefits' |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/science-environment-33716589 |work=BBC News |access-date=28 July 2018 |date=30 July 2015}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.1177/0013916515597512|pmid=27818525|title=Marine Biota and Psychological Well-Being|journal=Environment and Behavior|volume=48|issue=10|pages=1242–1269|year=2016|last1=Cracknell|first1=Deborah|last2=White|first2=Mathew P|last3=Pahl|first3=Sabine|last4=Nichols|first4=Wallace J|last5=Depledge|first5=Michael H|pmc=5081108|bibcode=2016EnvBe..48.1242C }}</ref> According to the research of having an aquarium is many health benefits like reduce stress, [[blood pressure]] and [[heart rate]] improvement, better quality [[sleep]], reduce [[anxiety]] and [[pain]], therapy of excited children, [[Alzheimer's]] therapy and improve [[productivity]].

===Materials===
====Glass====
The first modern aquarium made of glass was developed in the 19th century by Robert Warrington.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.ratemyfishtank.com/articles/74| title=History of Fish Tank Materials|access-date= 2010-05-28| archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20100514073351/http://www.ratemyfishtank.com/articles/74| archive-date= 14 May 2010 | url-status= live}}</ref> During the [[Victorian age]], glass aquariums commonly had slate or [[steel]] bottoms, which allowed them to be heated underneath by an open-flame heat source. These aquariums had the glass panels attached with metal frames and sealed with putty. Metal-framed aquariums were still available until the mid-1960s, when the modern, silicone-sealed style replaced them. [[Poly (methyl methacrylate)|Acrylic]] aquariums first became available to the public in the 1970s. [[Laminated glass]] is sometimes used, which combines the advantages of both glass and acrylic.<ref name=Adey/> Today, most aquaria consist of [[glass]] panes bonded together by 100% [[silicone]] sealant,<ref>{{cite web|title=Aquarium Silicone, Tank Repair, Applications, DIY, How To Use|url=https://www.aquarium-pond-answers.com/2007/03/aquarium-silicone.html|first = Carl|last= Strohmeyer |date = 1 January 2019|website = Aquarium Answers}}</ref> with plastic frames attached to the upper and lower edges for decoration. The glass aquarium is standard for sizes up to about {{convert|1000|L|USgal Impgal}}. However, glass is brittle and has very little give before fracturing, though generally the sealant fails first.<ref name="Adey">{{Cite book|title=Dynamic Aquaria|last1 = Adey|first1 = Walter H.|last2 = Loveland|first2 = Karen|publisher=Academic Press|location=San Diego|year=1991|isbn= 0-12-043792-9}}</ref> Aquaria are made in a variety of shapes, such as [[cuboid]], [[hexagonal prism|hexagonal]], angled to fit in a corner (L-shaped), and bow-front (the front side curves outwards).<ref name="Sanford2">{{cite book | title = Aquarium Owner's Guide | last = Sanford | first = Gina | publisher = [[DK Publishing]] | location = New York | year = 1999 | pages = [https://archive.org/details/aquariumownersgu00sand/page/162 162–169] | isbn = 0-7894-4614-6 | url = https://archive.org/details/aquariumownersgu00sand/page/162 }}</ref> Fish bowls are generally either made of plastic or glass, and are either spherical or some other round configuration in shape.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

Glass aquaria have been a popular choice for many home and hobbyist [[Aquarist|aquarists]] for many years. Once silicone sealant became strong enough to ensure a long-term water-tight seal, it eliminated the need for a structural frame. In addition to lower cost, glass aquaria are more scratch resistant than acrylic. Although the price is one of the main considerations for aquarists when deciding which of these two types of aquaria to purchase, for very large tanks, the price difference tends to disappear.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}
[[File:Al Mahara Burj al Araba Dubai March 2008pano.jpg|thumb|An aquarium in the [[Burj Al Arab]] in [[Dubai]]]]

====Acrylic====
Acrylic aquaria are now the primary competitor with glass. Prior to the invention of [[UV stabilizers in plastics|UV stabilization]], early acrylic aquaria discolored over time with exposure to light; this is no longer the case. Acrylic is generally stronger than glass, weighs less, and provides a certain amount of temperature insulation. In colder climates or environments, it is easier to achieve and maintain a tropical temperature and requires less capacity from an aquarium heater.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.reef-one.com/technology/benefits-of-acrylic.html |title=Benefits of Acrylic for Home Aquariums |publisher=reef-one.com |last=Crosswell |first=Tom |access-date=2009-05-10 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090309175603/http://www.reef-one.com/technology/benefits-of-acrylic.html |archive-date=2009-03-09 }}</ref> Acrylic-soluble cements are used to directly fuse acrylic together.<ref name="Adey" /> Acrylic allows for the formation of unusual shapes, such as the hexagonal tank.<ref name="Sanford" /> Acrylics are easier to scratch than glass, but unlike glass, scratches in acrylic can be polished out.<ref name="Adey" />

====Other materials====
Large aquaria might instead use stronger materials such as [[fiberglass]]-reinforced [[plastic]]s. However, this material is not transparent.<ref name="Adey" /> Reinforced [[concrete]] is used for aquaria where weight and space are not factors. Concrete must be coated with a waterproof layer to prevent the water from breaking down the concrete, as well as preventing contamination of the water by the concrete.<ref name="Adey" />

Plywood can also be used when building aquaria. The benefits of using plywood include: lower construction costs, less weight, and better insulation. A popular positioning choice for plywood aquaria is keeping them in a wall. Here the use of plywood is hidden by sinking the aquarium inside the wall. Putting insulation between the two helps with the insulation of a heated tank.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.advancedaquarist.com/2011/12/aafeature|title=Feature Article: Building a Plywood Aquarium|access-date=2017-12-10|language=en-US|archive-date=2017-12-24|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171224133456/http://www.advancedaquarist.com/2011/12/aafeature|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Styles===
[[File:Macquarium.jpg|thumb|right|upright|A [[Macquarium]]]]
Objects used for aquariums include: coffee tables, sinks, and even toilets.<ref name=NYTimes>{{Cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2010/08/19/garden/19aqua.html|title=The Six-Figure Fish Tank Catches On|work=[[The New York Times]]|last=Kingson|first=Jennifer A.|page=D1|date=August 19, 2010|access-date=August 22, 2010| archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20110906143240/http://www.nytimes.com/2010/08/19/garden/19aqua.html| archive-date=September 6, 2011| url-status= live}}</ref> Another such example is the [[Macquarium]], an aquarium made from the shell of an [[Apple Macintosh]] [[computer]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.cs.tut.fi/~ace/macquarium.html |title=The Original MacQuarium |first=Andy |last=Ihnatko |year=1992 |access-date=2007-04-04 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080812031730/http://www.cs.tut.fi/~ace/macquarium.html |archive-date=2008-08-12 }}</ref> In recent years, elaborate custom-designed home aquariums costing hundreds of thousands of dollars have become status symbols—according to ''The New York Times'', "among people of means, a dazzling aquarium is one of the last surefire ways to impress their peers."<ref name=NYTimes/>

====Kreisel<!-- Wikilink directs here from Monterey Bay Aquarium -->====
[[File:Jellyfish Art Tank.jpg|thumb|alt=3 jellyfish in water in a cylindrical tank (the wall behind the tank has a floral design)|A typical kreisel tank housing several [[Aurelia aurita|moon jellyfish]]]]

A kreisel tank (''kreisel'' being German for "[[spinning top]]" or "[[gyroscope]]") is an aquarium shaped like a horizontal cylinder that is designed to hold delicate animals such as [[jellyfish]] and newborn seahorses. These aquariums provide slow, circular water flow with a bare minimum of interior hardware to prevent the inhabitants from becoming injured by pumps or the tank itself.<ref>{{cite magazine |url=http://www.advancedaquarist.com/issues/dec2004/lines.htm|title=Delicatessen Part I: Creating a system for rare and delicate animals|last=Blundell|first=Adam|publisher=Pomacanthus Publications, LLC |magazine=Advanced Aquarist |volume=3 |date=December 2004 |access-date=2007-04-04 |url-status=live |archive-date=26 May 2007 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070526201532/http://www.advancedaquarist.com/issues/dec2004/lines.htm }}</ref> The tank has no sharp angles around its sides and keeps the housed animals away from plumbing. Water moving into the tank gives a gentle flow that keeps the inhabitants suspended. Water leaves the tank through a screen which prevents animals from being drawn into the pump intake or overflow line.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

There are several types of kreisel tanks. In a true kreisel, a circular tank has a circular, submerged lid. ''Pseudokreisels'' are "U" or semicircle shaped, usually without a lid.<ref>{{cite web |last=Wrobel |first=Dave |url=http://jellieszone.com/captivejellies.htm|title=Captive Jellies: Keeping Jellies in an Aquarium | publisher = The Jellies Zone |access-date=2007-04-04 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070410061052/http://jellieszone.com/captivejellies.htm| archive-date=10 April 2007 |url-status=dead }}</ref> ''Stretch kreisels'' are a "double gyre" kreisel design, where the tank length is at least twice the height. Using two downwelling inlets on both sides of the tank lets gravity create two gyres in the tank. A single downwelling inlet may be used in the middle as well. The top of a stretch kreisel may be open or closed with a lid. There may also be screens about midway down the sides of the tank, or at the top on the sides.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Raskoff |first1=Kevin A. |last2=Sommer |first2=Freya A. |last3=Hamner |first3=William M. |last4=Cross |first4=Katrina M. |date=February 2003 |title=Collection and culture techniques for gelatinous zooplankton |journal=Biological Bulletin |volume=204 |issue=1 |pages=68–80 |jstor=1543497 |doi=10.2307/1543497 |pmid=12588746 |s2cid=22389317 |url=https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/9222 }}</ref> It is possible to combine these designs; a circular shaped tank is used without a lid or cover, and the surface of the water acts as the continuation of circular flow.

====Biotope====
Another popular setup is the [[Aquascaping#Biotopes|biotope]] aquarium. A biotope aquarium is a recreation of a specific natural environment. Some of the most popular biotopes are the freshwater habitats of the [[Amazon River]], the [[Rio Negro (Amazon)|Rio Negro]] River, the African [[rift lake]] environments of [[Lake Malawi]] and [[Lake Tanganyika]], and saltwater [[coral reef]]s of [[Great Barrier Reef|Australia]], the [[Red Sea]], and the [[Caribbean Sea]]. The fish, plants, substrate, rocks, wood, coral, and any other component of the display should completely match that of the local natural environment. It can be a challenge to recreate such environments, and most "true" biotopes will only have a few (if not only one) species of fish and invertebrates.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

Finally, an emerging concept for the home is that of a wall mounted aquarium.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://wallmountedaquarium.net/|title=Wall Mounted Aquarium|access-date=July 13, 2014|archive-date=July 14, 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140714185909/http://wallmountedaquarium.net/|url-status=dead}}</ref>

===Aquarium size and volume===
[[File:Kelp Forest exhibit full main viewing window at Monterey Bay Aquarium.jpg|thumb|left|A {{convert|1200000|L|USgal Impgal|adj=on}} aquarium at [[Monterey Bay Aquarium]] in California, displaying a [[kelp forest]] ecosystem|alt=Photo of {{convert|50|ft|m|adj=mid|-tall}} yellow plants in water behind glass wall divided into sections.]]

An aquarium can range from a small glass bowl containing less than {{convert|1|L|U.S.gal}} of water to immense public aquaria that house entire ecosystems such as [[kelp forest]]s. Relatively large home aquaria resist rapid fluctuations of temperature and [[pH]], allowing for greater system stability.<ref name=Sanford2/> Beginner [[Aquarist|aquarists]] are advised to consider larger tanks to begin with, as controlling water parameters in smaller tanks can prove difficult.<ref>{{Cite web|date=2018-10-05|title=Beginner's Guide to Set Up a Freshwater Aquarium|url=https://smartaquariumguide.com/freshwater-fish-tank-setup/|access-date=2022-01-31|website=SmartAquariumGuide|language=en-US}}</ref>

Small, unfiltered bowl-shaped aquaria are now widely regarded as unsuitable for most fish. In order to keep water conditions at suitable levels, aquariums should contain at least two forms of [[Filter (aquarium)|filtration]]: biological and mechanical. Chemical filtration should also be considered under some circumstances for optimum water quality. Chemical filtration is frequently achieved via [[activated carbon]], to filter medications, [[tannin]]s, and/or other known impurities from the water.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

Reef aquaria under {{convert|100|L|USgal Impgal}} have a special place in the aquarium hobby; these aquaria, termed [[nano reef]]s (when used in reefkeeping), have a small water volume, under {{convert|40|L|USgal Impgal|0}}.<ref>Reef Hobbyist Magazine, pp. 42–46, Q2 2013</ref>

[[Public aquarium]]s and [[oceanarium]]s designed for exhibition of large species or environments can be dramatically larger than any home aquarium. ==Components==

The typical hobbyist aquarium includes a filtration system, an artificial lighting system, an air diffuser and pump, and a heater or chiller depending on the aquarium's inhabitants. Many aquaria incorporate a hood, containing the lights, to decrease evaporation and prevent fish from leaving the aquarium (and anything else from entering the aquarium).<ref name=Sanford2/>

Combined biological and mechanical aquarium [[Filter (aquarium)|filtration systems]] are common. These either convert ammonia to nitrate (removing nitrogen at the expense of aquatic plants), or to sometimes remove [[phosphate]]. Filter media can house [[microbes]] that mediate [[nitrification]]. Filtration systems are sometimes the most complex component of home aquaria.<ref name=dakin>{{cite book

[[Aquarium heater]]s combine a heating element with a [[thermostat]], allowing the [[aquarist]] to regulate water temperature at a level above that of the surrounding air, whereas coolers and chillers (refrigeration devices) are for use anywhere, such as cold water aquaria, where the ambient room temperature is above the desired tank temperature.<ref name=Sanford2/> [[Thermometer]]s used include glass alcohol thermometers, adhesive external [[Liquid crystal thermometer|plastic strip thermometers]], and battery-powered LCD thermometers.<ref name=Sanford2/> In addition, some aquarists use air pumps attached to [[airstone]]s or water pumps to increase water circulation and supply adequate gas exchange at the water surface. Wave-making devices have also been constructed to provide wave action.<ref name=Adey/>

[[File:Fishes, multicolored pebbles and artificial plants within an aquarium.jpg|thumb|Fishes, multicolored pebbles and artificial plants within a home aquarium.]]
An aquarium's physical characteristics form another aspect of aquarium design. Size, lighting conditions, density of floating and rooted plants, placement of [[bog-wood]], creation of caves or overhangs, type of [[substrate (aquarium)|substrate]], and other factors (including an aquarium's positioning within a room) can all affect the behavior and survival of tank inhabitants.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

An aquarium can be placed on an aquarium stand. Because of the weight of the aquarium, a stand must be strong as well as level. A tank that is not level may distort, leak, or crack.<ref name=Sanford2/> These are often built with cabinets to allow storage, available in many styles to match room [[decor]]. Simple metal tank stands are also available.<ref name=Sanford2/> Most aquaria should be placed on [[polystyrene]] to cushion any irregularities on the underlying surface or the bottom of the tank itself that may cause cracks.<ref name=Sanford2/> However, some tanks have an underframe making this unnecessary.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

Another important consideration for aquariums is their electrical usage. Water is expensive to keep heated,<ref>{{Cite web|title=The Cost of Running a Fish Tank – The Mandarin Garden|date=13 January 2020 |url=https://www.themandaringarden.com/cost-to-run-fish-tank.html|access-date=2020-12-11|language=en-US}}</ref> along with the lights that many aquariums, especially those with live plants have. New [[Aquarist|aquarists]] should also pay close attention to their electrical setup for their aquarium, taking care to set up power connections with drip loops to prevent water from getting to outlets.<ref>{{Cite web|date=2020-12-11|title=How To Pick Supplies For Your 40 Gallon Aquarium|url=https://freshwatercentral.com/pick-supplies-for-your-40-gallon-aquarium|access-date=2020-12-11|website=Freshwater Central|language=en-US|archive-date=2021-01-18|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210118204258/https://freshwatercentral.com/pick-supplies-for-your-40-gallon-aquarium|url-status=dead}}</ref>

==Aquarium maintenance==
{{More citations needed section|date=February 2021}}
Large volumes of water enable more stability in a tank by diluting effects from death or contamination events that push an aquarium away from equilibrium. The bigger the tank, the easier such a [[systemic shock]] is to absorb, because the effects of that event are diluted. For example, the death of the only fish in an {{convert|11|L|USgal Impgal|0|adj=on}} tank causes dramatic changes in the system, while the death of that same fish in a {{convert|400|L|USgal Impgal|adj=on}} tank with many other fish in it represents only a minor change. For this reason, hobbyists often favor larger tanks, as they require less attention.

Several [[nutrient cycle]]s are important in the aquarium. Dissolved oxygen enters the system at the surface water-air interface. Similarly, carbon dioxide escapes the system into the air. The phosphate cycle is an important, although often overlooked, nutrient cycle. Sulfur, iron, and micronutrients also cycle through the system, entering as food and exiting as waste. Appropriate handling of the [[nitrogen cycle]], along with supplying an adequately balanced food supply and considered biological loading, is enough to keep these other nutrient cycles in approximate equilibrium.

An aquarium must be maintained regularly to ensure that the fish are kept healthy. Daily maintenance consists of checking the fish for signs of stress and [[disease]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.aquariumfish.net/information/maintenance_schedule.htm| title=A Preventative Maintenance Schedule|access-date= 2010-05-28| archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20100525222821/http://www.aquariumfish.net/information/maintenance_schedule.htm| archive-date= 25 May 2010 | url-status= live}}</ref> Also, [[Aquarist|aquarists]] must make sure that the water has a good quality and it is not cloudy or [[foamy]] and the [[temperature]] of the water is appropriate for the particular species of fish that live in the aquarium.

Typical weekly maintenance includes changing around 10–30% or more of the water while cleaning the [[gravel]], or other substrate if the aquarium has one; however some manage to avoid this entirely by keeping it somewhat self-sufficient. A good habit is to remove the water being replaced by "vacuuming" the gravel with suitable implements, as this will eliminate uneaten foods and other residues that settle on the [[Substrate (aquarium)|substrate]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.algone.com/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=52:aquarium-maintenance-tips&catid=35:tank-maintenance&Itemid=34| title=Aquarium Maintenance Tips|access-date= 2010-05-28}}</ref> In many areas [[tap water]] is not considered to be safe for fish to live in because it contains chemicals that harm the fish.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Beginner: Water |url=https://users.cs.duke.edu/~narten/faq/water-treatment.html |access-date=2023-10-12 |website=users.cs.duke.edu}}</ref> Tap water from those areas must be treated with a suitable water conditioner, such as a product which removes chlorine and [[monochloramine|chloramine]] and neutralizes any heavy metals present. The water conditions must be checked both in the tank and in the replacement water, to make sure they are suitable for the species.

===Water conditions===
The [[solute]] content of water is perhaps the most important aspect of water conditions, as [[total dissolved solids]] and other constituents dramatically impact basic water chemistry, and therefore how organisms interact with their environment. Salt content, or [[salinity]], is the most basic measure of water conditions. An aquarium may have [[freshwater]] (salinity below 500&nbsp;parts per million), simulating a lake or river environment; [[brackish water]] (a salt level of 500 to 30,000&nbsp;PPM), simulating environments lying between fresh and salt, such as [[estuary|estuaries]]; and salt water or [[seawater]] (a salt level of 30,000 to 40,000&nbsp;PPM), simulating an ocean environment. Rarely, higher salt concentrations are maintained in specialized tanks for raising brine organisms.

Saltwater is usually alkaline, while the [[pH]] ([[alkali]]nity or [[acid]]ity) of fresh water varies more. Hardness measures overall dissolved mineral content; [[hard water|hard or soft water]] may be preferred. Hard water is usually alkaline, while soft water is usually neutral to acidic.<ref name=Axelrod/> [[Chemical oxygen demand|Dissolved organic content]] and dissolved gases content are also important factors.

Home [[Aquarist|aquarists]] typically use tap water supplied through their local [[water supply network]] to fill their tanks. Straight tap water cannot be used in localities that pipe chlorinated water. In the past, it was possible to "condition" the water by simply letting the water stand for a day or two, which allows the [[chlorine]] time to dissipate.<ref name=Axelrod/> However, [[monochloramine|chloramine]] is now used more often and does not leave the water as readily. [[Water conditioner]]s formulated to remove chlorine or chloramine are often all that is needed to make the water ready for aquarium use. Brackish or saltwater aquaria require the addition of a commercially available [[artificial seawater|mixture of salts]] and other minerals.

[[File:Outdoor heated fish tank enclosed by glass plus reflections.jpg|thumb|250px|This aquarium features a heated tank and a glass-enclosed top for warmth during winter.]]

Some aquarists modify water's alkalinity, hardness, or dissolved content of organics and gases, before adding it to their aquaria. This can be accomplished by additives, such as sodium bicarbonate, to raise pH.<ref name=Axelrod/> Some aquarists [[Water filter|filter]] or purify their water through [[Purified water|deionization]] or [[reverse osmosis]] prior to using it. In contrast, public aquaria with large water needs often locate themselves near a natural water source (such as a river, lake, or ocean) to reduce the level of treatment. Some hobbyists use an [[algae scrubber]] to filter the water naturally.{{Citation needed|date=July 2022}}

Water [[temperature]] determines the two most basic aquarium classifications: [[tropics|tropical]] versus [[Coldwater fish|cold water]]. Most fish and plant species tolerate only a limited temperature range; tropical aquaria, with an average temperature of about {{convert|25|C|F}}, are much more common. Temperate or coldwater aquaria are for fish that are better suited to a cooler environment. Temperature consistency is more important than range. Most organisms are not accustomed to sudden changes in temperatures, which can cause shock and lead to disease.<ref name=Axelrod/> Water temperature can be regulated with a [[thermostat]] and heater (or cooler).

Water movement can also be important in simulating a natural ecosystem. [[Aquarist|Aquarists]] may prefer anything from still water up to swift [[wikt:current|currents]], depending on the aquarium's inhabitants. Water movement can be controlled via aeration from air pumps, powerheads, and careful design of internal water flow (such as location of filtration system points of inflow and outflow).

===Nitrogen cycle===
{{main|Nitrogen cycle}}
[[File:Aquarium Nitrogen Cycle.svg|thumb|The [[nitrogen cycle]] in an aquarium|alt=Drawing showing cross-section of the ocean. The bottom is labeled "Decomposing plant & animal matter". A two-headed arrow is labeled ''Nitrosomonas'' and points back to the seaweed and also to another rectangle labeled Nitrites ({{chem|NO|-|2}}). Another arrow labeled ''Nitrospira'' points to another rectangle labeled Nitrates ({{chem|NO|-|3}}). Another arrow points back to the seaweed. Another arrow points to the air above the ocean surface and is labeled ''Water change''. Another arrow, labeled ''Food'' points from the air to a fish below the surface. A final arrow points to the rectangle labeled Ammonium ({{chem|NH|+|4}}).]]

Of primary concern to the [[aquarist]] is management of the [[waste]] produced by an aquarium's inhabitants. Fish, invertebrates, [[fungus|fungi]], and some bacteria excrete [[nitrogen]] waste in the form of [[ammonia]] (which converts to [[ammonium]], in water) and must then either pass through the [[nitrogen cycle]] or be removed by passing through [[zeolite]].<ref>{{cite web |last=Monks |first=Neale |title=Aquarium Filter Maintenance |website=Fish Channel |publisher=BowTie |url=http://www.fishchannel.com/fish-health/healthy-aquariums/aquarium-filter-maintenance.aspx |url-status=dead |archive-date=23 October 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121023133331/http://www.fishchannel.com/fish-health/healthy-aquariums/aquarium-filter-maintenance.aspx }}</ref> Ammonia is also produced through the [[decomposition]] of plant and animal matter, including [[feces|fecal]] matter and other [[detritus]]. Nitrogen waste products become toxic to fish and other aquarium inhabitants at high concentrations.<ref name=Axelrod>{{cite book | title = Exotic Tropical Fishes | last = Axelrod | first = Herbert R. | publisher = T.F.H. Publications. | year = 1996 | isbn = 0-87666-543-1}}</ref>{{Page needed|date=March 2017}} In the wild, the vast amount of water surrounding the fish dilutes ammonia and other waste materials. When fish are put into an aquarium, waste can quickly reach toxic concentrations in the enclosed environment unless the tank is cycled to remove waste.<ref name=Axelrod/>{{Page needed|date=March 2017}}<ref>{{cite web|last1=Fransisca |first1=Erica |title=Understanding Nitrogen Cycle In Aquarium |work=Tankquarium |date=11 June 2021|url=https://tankquarium.com/nitrogen-cycle-in-aquarium/}}</ref>

====The process====
A well-balanced tank contains organisms that are able to [[metabolism|metabolize]] the waste products of other aquarium residents, recreating a portion of the [[nitrogen cycle]]. [[Bacteria]] known as [[nitrification|nitrifiers]] (genus ''[[Nitrosomonas]]'') metabolize nitrogen waste. [[Nitrifying bacteria]] capture ammonia from the water and metabolize it to produce [[nitrite]]. Nitrite is toxic to fish in high concentrations. Another type of bacteria (genus ''[[Nitrospira]]'') converts nitrite into [[nitrate]], a less toxic substance. (''[[Nitrobacter]]'' bacteria were previously believed to fill this role. While biologically they could theoretically fill the same niche as ''Nitrospira'', it has recently been found that ''Nitrobacter'' are not present in detectable levels in established aquaria, while ''Nitrospira'' are plentiful.)<ref>{{Cite journal|title=Nitrospira -Like Bacteria Associated with Nitrite Oxidation in Freshwater Aquaria|url=https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/AEM.64.1.258-264.1998|journal=Applied and Environmental Microbiology|date=January 1998|issn=0099-2240|pmc=124703|pmid=16349486|pages=258–264|volume=64|issue=1|doi=10.1128/AEM.64.1.258-264.1998|language=en|first=Timothy A.|last=Hovanec|first2=Lance T.|last2=Taylor|first3=Andrew|last3=Blakis|first4=Edward F.|last4=Delong}}</ref> However, commercial products sold as kits to "jump start" the nitrogen cycle often still contain ''Nitrobacter''.{{Citation needed|date=November 2009}}

Aquatic plants also eliminate nitrogen waste by metabolizing ammonia and nitrate. When plants metabolize nitrogen compounds, they remove nitrogen from the water by using it to build [[biomass]] that decays more slowly than ammonia-driven [[plankton]] already dissolved in the water. Some hobbyists also use "anoxic filtration", which relies on bacteria that live in low-oxygen environments.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.mankysanke.co.uk/html/anoxic_filtration.html|title = Anoxic Filtration}}</ref>

====Maintaining the nitrogen cycle====
[[File:Echinotitti.JPG|thumb|Live plants in an aquarium utilize the final product in the [[nitrogen cycle]] of [[nitrate]] as fertilizer, helping the nitrate levels stay minimal. This {{convert|60|L|adj=on}} aquarium contains ''[[Anubias barteri]]'' and ''[[Echinodorus bleheri]]''. A [[heater (aquarium)|heater]] and small [[filter (aquarium)|filter]] are in the background.]]
The nitrogen cycle in an aquarium is only a portion of the complete cycle: nitrogen must be added to the system (usually through food provided to the tank inhabitants), and nitrates accumulate in the water at the end of the process, or become bound in the biomass of plants. The aquarium keeper must remove water once nitrate concentrations grow, or remove plants which have grown from the nitrates.

Hobbyist aquaria often do not have sufficient bacteria populations to adequately denitrify waste. This problem is most often addressed through different [[filtration]] solutions: [[Activated carbon]] filters absorb nitrogen compounds and other [[toxin]]s, while biological filters provide a medium designed to enhance [[colony (biology)|bacterial colonization]]. Activated carbon and other substances, such as ammonia absorbing resins, stop working when their pores fill, so these components have to be replaced regularly.  Mechanical solutions, often referred to as [[Protein skimmer|protein skimmers]].  These devices use a combination of high throughput pumps, mechanical agitation (impellers), and air stones to circulate the water column through the system while removing fish and/or coral waste products.  These proteins typically form a high density foam that is the then captured in a trap that needs to be periodically cleaned by the [[aquarist]].  These systems can also enhance the dissolved oxygen levels in tanks.    

New aquaria often have problems associated with the nitrogen cycle due to insufficient beneficial bacteria.<ref>{{cite web |first=James M. |last=Kostich |date=2005
|url=http://www.bestfish.com/breakin.html
|title=New Tank Syndrome |website=Aquatics Unlimited |url-status=dead |archive-date=10 May 2008 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080510140511/http://www.bestfish.com/breakin.html
}}</ref> Therefore, both fresh water and saltwater systems have to be matured before stocking them with fish or coral. There are three basic approaches to this: the "fishless cycle", the "silent cycle" and "slow growth".  This is a common mistake made by beginner hobbyists who are excited to put livestock in their tanks on day one.  When a tank is overstocked it can result in excess ammonia build-up, "nitrogen burn" , potentially leading to livestock death. 

In a [[fishless cycling|fishless cycle]], small amounts of ammonia are added to an unpopulated tank to feed the bacteria. During this process, [[ammonia]], [[nitrite]], and [[nitrate]] levels are tested to monitor progress. The "silent" cycle is basically nothing more than densely stocking the aquarium with fast-growing aquatic [[plant]]s and relying on them to consume the [[nitrogen]], allowing the necessary bacterial populations time to develop. According to anecdotal reports, the plants can consume nitrogenous waste so efficiently that ammonia and nitrite level spikes seen in more traditional cycling methods are greatly reduced or disappear. "Slow growth" entails slowly increasing the population of fish over a period of 6 to 8 weeks, giving bacteria colonies time to grow and stabilize with the increase in fish waste. This method is usually done with a small starter population of hardier fish which can survive the ammonia and nitrite spikes, whether they are intended to be permanent residents or to be traded out later for the desired occupants.

The largest bacterial populations are found in the filter, where there is high water flow and plentiful surface available for their growth, so effective and efficient filtration is vital. Sometimes, a vigorous cleaning of the filter is enough to seriously disturb the biological balance of an aquarium. Therefore, it is recommended to rinse mechanical filters in an outside bucket of aquarium water to dislodge organic materials that contribute to nitrate problems, while preserving bacteria populations. Another safe practice consists of cleaning only half of the filter media during each service, or using two filters, only one of which is cleaned at a time.

===Biological load===
[[File:Small aquarium.jpg|thumb|A very heavily stocked 19-liter aquarium containing ''[[Paracheirodon innesi]]'', ''[[Trigonostigma heteromorpha]]'', and ''[[Hemigrammus erythrozonus]]''|alt=Photo displaying plants, small fish, and tipped-over clay pots]]
The biological load, or bioload is a measure of the burden placed on the aquarium ecosystem by its inhabitants. High biological loading presents a more complicated tank ecology, which in turn means that equilibrium is easier to upset. Several fundamental constraints on biological loading depend on aquarium size. The water's [[surface area]] limits [[Oxygen saturation|oxygen]] intake. The bacteria population depends on the physical space they have available to colonize. Physically, only a limited size and number of plants and animals can fit into an aquarium while still providing room for movement. Biologically, biological loading refers to the rate of biological decay in proportion to tank volume. Adding plants to an aquarium will sometimes help greatly with taking up fish waste as plant nutrients. Although an aquarium can be overloaded with fish, an excess of plants is unlikely to cause harm. Decaying plant material, such as decaying plant leaves, can add these nutrients back into the aquarium if not promptly removed. The bioload is processed by the aquarium's [[biofilter]] filtration system.

====Calculating capacity====
[[Limiting factor]]s include the oxygen availability and filtration processing. [[Aquarist|Aquarists]] have [[rule of thumb|rules of thumb]] to [[Approximation|estimate]] the number of fish that can be kept in an aquarium. The examples below are for small freshwater fish; larger freshwater fishes and most marine fishes need much more generous allowances.
* 3&nbsp;cm of ''adult'' fish length per 4&nbsp;litres of water (i.e., a 6&nbsp;cm-long fish would need about 8&nbsp;litres of water).<ref name="Ulrich Baensch">{{cite book
|first=Ulrich
|last=Baensch
|title=Tropical Aquarium Fish
|publisher=Tetra
|year=1983
|isbn=3-89356-131-5
|url=https://archive.org/details/tropicalaquarium00andr
}}</ref>
* 1&nbsp;cm of ''adult'' fish length per 30&nbsp;square centimetres of surface area.<ref name="Scott">{{cite book
|first=Peter
|last=Scott
|title=The Complete Aquarium
|publisher=Dorling Kindersley
|year=1996
|isbn=0-7513-0427-1
|url-access=registration
|url=https://archive.org/details/completeaquarium00pete
}}</ref>
* 1&nbsp;inch of ''adult'' fish length per US gallon of water.<ref name="Ulrich Baensch"/>
* 1&nbsp;inch of ''adult'' fish length per 12&nbsp;square inches of surface area.<ref name="Scott"/>

Experienced [[Aquarist|aquarists]] warn against applying these rules too strictly because they do not consider other important issues such as growth rate, activity level, social behaviour, filtration capacity, total biomass of plant life, and so on.<ref>{{cite book
|title=The Interpet Manual of Fish Health
|publisher=Salamander Books
|year=1988
|isbn=0-86101-368-9
|author1=Chris Andrews |author2=Adrian Exell |author3=Neville Carrington }}</ref> It is better to apply the overall mass and size of a fish per gallon of water, than simply the length. This is because fish of different sizes produce quite differing amounts of waste. Establishing maximum capacity is often a matter of slowly adding fish and monitoring water quality over time, following a [[trial and error]] approach.

====Other factors affecting capacity====
[[File:BGSU Aquarium.jpg|thumb|An academic aquarium at a university, using a variety of tank sizes and styles to care for different fish.]]
One variable is differences between fish. Smaller fish consume more oxygen per gram of body weight than larger fish. [[Labyrinth fish]] can breathe atmospheric oxygen and do not need as much surface area (however, some of these fish are territorial, and do not appreciate crowding). [[Barb (fish)|Barb]]s also require more surface area than [[tetra]]s of comparable size.<ref name=Axelrod/>

Oxygen exchange at the surface is an important constraint, and thus the surface area of the aquarium matters. Some [[Aquarist|aquarists]] claim that a deeper aquarium holds no more fish than a shallower aquarium with the same surface area. The capacity can be improved by surface movement and water circulation such as through [[aeration]], which not only improves oxygen exchange, but also waste decomposition rates.<ref name=Axelrod/>

Waste density is another variable. Decomposition in solution consumes oxygen. Oxygen dissolves less readily in warmer water; this is a double-edged sword since warmer temperatures make fish more active, so they consume more oxygen.<ref name=Axelrod/>

In addition to bioload/chemical considerations, [[Aquarist|aquarists]] also consider the mutual compatibility of the fish. For instance, predatory fish are usually not kept with small, passive species, and territorial fish are often unsuitable tankmates for shoaling species. Furthermore, fish tend to fare better if given tanks conducive to their size. That is, large fish need large tanks and small fish can do well in smaller tanks. Lastly, the tank can become overcrowded without being overstocked. In other words, the aquarium can be suitable with regard to filtration capacity, oxygen load, and water, yet still be so crowded that the inhabitants are uncomfortable.{{Citation needed|date=July 2022}}

For planted freshwater aquariums, it is also important to maintain a balance between the duration and quality of light, the amount of plants, CO<sub>2</sub> levels and nutrients. Light exposure within the tank environment can also influence nutrient concentrations. For a given amount of light, if there is insufficient number of plants or insufficient CO<sub>2</sub> to support the growth of those plants, so as to consume all the nutrients in the tank, the result would be algae growth. While there are fishes and invertebrates that could be introduced in the tank to clean up this algae, the ideal solution would be to find the optimal balance between the above-mentioned factors. Supplemental CO<sub>2</sub> can be provided,<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://aquaplantscare.uk/co2-and-the-role-of-carbon-and-liquid-carbon-in-planted-aquariums/|title = CO2 carbon and liquid carbon in planted aquariums|date = 12 December 2018}}</ref> whose quantity has to be carefully regulated, as too much CO<sub>2</sub> may harm the fishes.{{Original research inline|date=December 2017}}

==Aquarium classifications==
[[File:58g aquascape.jpg|thumb|An [[Aquascaping|aquascaped]] freshwater aquarium|alt=Photo showing a tank filled with water and multiple aquatic plants.]]
From the outdoor ponds and glass jars of antiquity, modern aquaria have evolved into a wide range of specialized systems. Individual aquaria can vary in size from a small bowl large enough for only a single small fish, to the huge public aquaria that can simulate entire marine [[ecosystem]]s.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

One way to classify aquaria is by salinity. [[Freshwater aquarium|Freshwater aquaria]] are the most popular due to their lower cost.<ref name=Sanford3>{{cite book
| title = Aquarium Owner's Guide
| last = Sanford
| first = Gina
| publisher = [[DK Publishing]]
| location = New York
| year = 1999
| pages = [https://archive.org/details/aquariumownersgu00sand/page/180 180–199]
| isbn = 0-7894-4614-6
| url = https://archive.org/details/aquariumownersgu00sand/page/180
}}</ref> More expensive and complex equipment is required to set up and maintain [[marine aquarium|marine aquaria]]. Marine aquaria frequently feature a diverse range of [[invertebrate]]s in addition to species of fish.<ref name=dakin/><ref name=Sanford3/> [[Brackish-water aquarium|Brackish water aquaria]] combine elements of both marine and freshwater fishkeeping.<ref name=Sanford3/> Fish kept in brackish water aquaria generally come from habitats with varying salinity, such as [[mangrove|mangrove swamp]]s and [[estuaries]]. Subtypes exist within these types, such as the [[reef aquarium]], a typically smaller marine aquarium that houses [[coral]].<ref name=Sanford3/>

Another classification is by [[temperature]] range. Many [[Aquarist|aquarists]] choose a [[tropical fish|tropical aquarium]] because tropical fish tend to be more colorful.<ref name=Sanford3/> However, the [[coldwater fish|coldwater aquarium]] is also popular, which includes fish from temperate areas worldwide.<ref name=Sanford3/>
[[File:Aquarium fg01.jpg|thumb|A [[Seawater|saltwater]] aquarium|alt=Photo of water, coral, and fish behind a glass wall.]]

Aquaria may be grouped by their species selection. In a [[community tank]], several non-aggressive species live peacefully. In these aquaria, the fish, [[invertebrate]]s, and [[aquatic plant|plants]] probably do not originate from the same geographic region, but tolerate similar water conditions and each other. Aggressive tanks, by contrast, house a limited number of species that can be aggressive toward other fish, or are able to withstand aggression well. Most aquarists maintaining marine tanks and tanks housing [[cichlids]] have to take species aggressiveness into account when stocking. Specimen tanks usually only house one fish species, along with plants—sometimes those found in the fish species' natural environment—and decorations simulating a natural ecosystem. This type is useful for fish that cannot coexist with other fish, such as the [[electric eel]], as an extreme example. Some tanks of this sort are used simply to house adults for breeding.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

[[Biotope]] aquaria is another type based on species selection. In it, an [[aquarist]] attempts to simulate a specific natural ecosystem, assembling fish, invertebrate species, plants, decorations and water conditions all found in that ecosystem. Public aquaria often use this approach. Biotope aquaria simulates the experience of observing in the wild. It typically serves as the healthiest possible artificial environment for the tank's occupants.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

==Public aquaria==
[[File:Tunnelaquarium 14-05-2009 15-54-09.JPG|thumb|The 80-meter (260 ft) underwater tunnel in [[Aquarium Barcelona]]|alt=Photo – silhouettes of people in foreground. One large fish with many smaller fish in background.]]
[[File:Lisbon Oceanarium.jpg|thumb|[[Lisbon Oceanarium]] designed by architect [[Peter Chermayeff]]]]
[[File:Kotka maretarium inside.jpg|thumb|The Baltic Sea Aquarium at the [[Maretarium]] in [[Kotka]], Finland]]
{{Main|Public aquarium}}
Most [[public aquarium]] facilities feature a number of smaller aquaria, as well as those too large for home [[Aquarist|aquarists]]. The largest tanks hold millions of gallons of water and can house large species, including [[shark]]s or [[Beluga (whale)|beluga whales]], which typically could not be housed properly in the home aquarium. [[Dolphinarium|Dolphinaria]] are specifically for [[dolphin]]s. Aquatic and semiaquatic animals, including [[otter]]s and [[penguin]]s, may also be kept by public aquaria. Public aquaria may also be included in larger establishments such as a [[marine mammal park]] or a [[marine park]]. These are very popular around the world, especially with a new emergence in the [[Middle East]].

==Virtual aquariums==
{{Unreferenced section|date=February 2019}}
A virtual aquarium is a [[computer program]] which uses [[3D graphics]] to reproduce an aquarium on a [[personal computer]]. The swimming fish are [[rendering (computer graphics)|rendered]] in [[Real-time computer graphics|real time]], while the background of the tank is usually static. Objects on the floor of the tank may be mapped in simple [[plane (geometry)|plane]]s so that the fish may appear to swim both in front and behind them, but a relatively simple 3D map of the general shape of such objects may be used to allow the light and ripples on the surface of the water to cast realistic [[shadow]]s.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Park |first1=Jinho |last2=Choi |first2=Jin |last3=Seo |first3=Yong-Ho |date=2013-06-22 |title=Development of a virtual aquarium system interacting with a smart device |url=http://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s11554-013-0364-z |journal=Journal of Real-Time Image Processing |volume=9 |issue=3 |pages=531–539 |doi=10.1007/s11554-013-0364-z |s2cid=255314743 |issn=1861-8200|url-access=subscription }}</ref> Bubbles and water noises are common for virtual aquariums, which are often used as [[screensaver]]s.

The number of each type of fish can usually be selected, often including other animals like [[starfish]], [[jellyfish]], [[seahorse]]s, and even [[sea turtle]]s. Most companies that produce virtual aquarium software also offer other types of fish for sale via Internet [[download]]. Other objects found in an aquarium can also be added and rearranged on some software, like [[treasure chest]]s and giant [[clam]]s that open and close with air bubbles, or a bobbing [[Scuba diving|diver]]. There are also usually features that allow the user to tap on the glass or put food in the top, both of which the fish will react to. Some also have the ability to allow the user to edit fish and other objects to create new varieties.
