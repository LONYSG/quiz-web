The '''ondes Martenot''' ({{IPAc-en|ˈ|oʊ|n|d|_|m|ɑːr|t|ə|ˈ|n|oʊ}} {{respell|OHND|_|mar|tə|NOH}}; {{IPA|fr|ɔ̃d maʁtəno|lang}}, {{lit|Martenot waves}}) or '''ondes musicales''' ({{lit|musical waves}}) is an early [[electronic musical instrument]]. Early models were played only by moving a ring tied to a wire, creating "wavering" sounds similar to a [[theremin]], but a lateral-vibrato [[Keyboard instrument|keyboard]] was soon added. Dynamics and timbre are adjusted using controls in a drawer on the instrument's left side. A player of the ondes Martenot is called an '''ondist'''.

The ondes Martenot was invented in 1928 by the French inventor [[Maurice Martenot]]. Martenot was inspired by the accidental overlaps of tones between military radio oscillators, and wanted to create an instrument with the expressiveness of the [[cello]].

The ondes Martenot is used in more than 100 orchestral compositions. The French composer [[Olivier Messiaen]] used it in pieces such as his 1949 ''[[Turangalîla-Symphonie|Turangalîla-symphonie]]'', and his sister-in-law [[Jeanne Loriod]] was a celebrated player of the instrument. It appears in numerous film and television soundtracks, particularly [[Science fiction film|science fiction]] and [[horror film]]s. It has also been used by contemporary acts such as [[Daft Punk]], [[Damon Albarn]], and [[Radiohead]] guitarist and keyboardist [[Jonny Greenwood]].

== History ==
The ondes Martenot (French for "Martenot waves") is one of the earliest [[Electronic musical instrument|electronic instruments]],<ref name="McNamee-2009" /><ref name="Reid-2002">{{Cite web|url=https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/analogue-systems-french-connection|title=Analogue Systems French Connection|last=Reid|first=Gordon|date=February 2002|website=[[Sound on Sound]]|language=en-gb|access-date=24 July 2018}}</ref><ref name=Ross /> patented in the same year as another early electronic instrument, the [[theremin]].<ref name="McNamee-2009" /> It was invented in 1928 by the French cellist [[Maurice Martenot]].<ref name="McNamee-2009">{{Cite news|url=https://www.theguardian.com/music/2009/oct/12/ondes-martenot|title=Hey, What's That Sound: Ondes Martenot|last=McNamee|first=David|date=12 October 2009|newspaper=[[The Guardian]]|access-date=7 September 2018}}</ref> Martenot had been a [[radio operator]] during [[World War I]], and developed the ondes Martenot in an attempt to replicate the accidental overlaps of tones between military radio oscillators.<ref name="McNamee-2009" /> He hoped to bring the musical expressivity of the [[cello]] to his new instrument.<ref>Jean Laurendeau: ''Maurice Martenot: Luthier de l'Electronique'' (Dervy Livres, 1996)</ref> According to ''[[The Guardian]]'', the ondes Martenot visually resembles a cross between an organ and a theremin.<ref name="McNamee-2009" />

Martenot first demonstrated the ondes Martenot on April 20, 1928,<ref name="Martin-2001">{{Cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2001/08/19/arts/jeanne-loriod-who-transformed-electronic-wails-into-heartfelt-music-dies-at-73.html?exprod=permalink&partner=permalink|title=Jeanne Loriod, Who Transformed Electronic Wails Into Heartfelt Music, Dies at 73|last=Martin|first=Douglas|date=19 August 2001|newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|access-date=25 July 2018}}</ref> performing [[Dimitrios Levidis]]'s ''Poème symphonique'' at the [[Paris Opera]].<ref>{{Cite magazine|url=http://www.musicaltimes.co.uk/archive/0104/loriod.html|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20091226034731/http://www.musicaltimes.co.uk/archive/0104/loriod.html|url-status=dead|archive-date=26 December 2009|magazine=[[The Musical Times]]|title=Jeanne Loriod 1928–2001|date=26 December 2009|access-date=2 August 2018}}</ref> He embarked on a number of performance tours to promote it, beginning in Europe before going to New York.<ref name="Chadabe-1997">{{Cite book |last=Chadabe |first=Joel |title=Electric Sound |publisher=Prentice Hall |year=1997 |isbn=0133032310 |location=Upper Saddle River, New Jersey |pages=12–13}}</ref> In 1930, he performed with the [[Philadelphia Orchestra]], after which he embarked on a world tour.<ref name="Chadabe-1997" /> In 1937, the ondes Martenot was displayed at the [[Exposition Internationale des Arts et Techniques dans la Vie Moderne|Exposition Internationale de Paris]] with concerts and demonstrations in an ensemble setting with up to twelve ondists performing together at a time.<ref name="Chadabe-1997" /> Beginning in 1947, the ondes Martenot was taught at the Paris Conservatory, with Martenot as the first teacher.<ref name="Ratsimandresy-2014" />

The earliest model of the ondes Martenot was similar to the Theremin. The second model of the instrument, demonstrated in 1928, lacked most of the features that are now associated with the instrument. It was played in a standing position by pulling on a wire connected to a wooden box. Over the following years, Martenot produced several new models, introducing a keyboard with the ability to produce [[vibrato]] by moving the keys laterally.<ref name="Reid-2002" />
According to Cynthia Millar, "[b]y the 1940s, the instrument had settled into what was to be, with
minor variations, its final form."<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.theguardian.com/news/2001/sep/06/guardianobituaries|title=Jeanne Loriod|last=Cynthia|first=Millar|date=6 September 2001|newspaper=[[The Guardian]]|access-date=7 April 2025}}</ref> Instruments were manufactured to order,<ref name="Martin-2001" /> and "often
differed according to the wishes of the player."<ref name="Schampaert" /> Martenot was uninterested in mass-producing the ondes Martenot, which may have contributed to its decline in popularity following initial interest.<ref name="Chadabe-1997" /> Jean-Louis Martenot, Maurice Martenot's son, created new ondes Martenot models.<ref name="Reid-2002" /> In 2009, ''[[The Guardian]]'' reported that the last ondes Martenot was manufactured in 1988, but that a new model was being manufactured.<ref name="McNamee-2009" />

== Sounds and technique ==
[[File:Ondomo audio recording.ogg|thumb|right|A recording of the Ondomo, an instrument based on the ondes Martenot]]
The ondes Martenot is unique among electronic musical instruments in its methods of control.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|p={{page needed|date=January 2024}}}} The ondes Martenot can be played with a metal ring worn on the right index finger. Sliding the ring along a wire produces "theremin-like" tones, generated by [[electronic oscillator|oscillator]] circuits using [[vacuum tube]]s,<ref name="McNamee-2009" /> or [[transistor]]s in the seventh model.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, "Preface", p. III}}<ref>{{cite web|title=Ondes Musicales by Dierstein|date=15 July 2011|url=http://www.thomasbloch.net/new_ondes_martenot|access-date=27 January 2024|author=[[Thomas Bloch]]|website=thomasbloch.net}}</ref> A drawer allows manipulation of volume and timbre by the left hand.{{sfn|Martenot|1931|pp=14–15}} Volume is controlled with a touch sensitive glass "lozenge".<ref name="McNamee-2009" />

The third model, unveiled in 1929, had a non-functioning [[simulacrum]] of a keyboard below the wire to indicate pitch.<ref name="Battaglia-2014">{{harvnb|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I p. VI}}; {{Cite web|url=http://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2014/03/ondes-martenot-introduction|title=Ondes Martenot: An Introduction|last=Battaglia|first=Andy|date=6 March 2014|website=[[Red Bull Music Academy]]|access-date=25 July 2018}}</ref> This model also had a "black fingerguard" on a wire which could be used instead of the ring. It was held between the right thumb and index finger, which was played standing at a distance from the instrument. When played in this way, the drawer is removed from the instrument and placed on a bench next to the player. Maurice Martenot's pedagogical manual for the ondes Martenot, written in 1931, offers instruction on both methods of playing.{{sfn|Martenot|1931|pp=39–40}}[[File:Ondes-ruban.jpg|thumb|''Au ruban'' playing technique]][[File:Ondes-diffuseurs.jpg|thumb|''Diffuseurs'' from left: ''Métallique'', ''Palme'', and cabinet containing both ''Principal'' and ''Résonance''{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. XV}}|alt=]]Later versions added a real functioning [[Keyboard instrument|keyboard]];<ref name="Battaglia-2014" /> the keys produce [[vibrato]] when moved from side to side. This was introduced in the 1930s with the 84-key fourth version of the instrument.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. VII}}<ref>{{Cite web |date= 2018 |title= Détail du document Ondes Marenot |website= cité de la musique philharmonie de paris |url= http://collectionsdumusee.philharmoniedeparis.fr/doc/MUSEE/1077984 |access-date= 6 September 2018 |archive-date= 18 August 2022 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20220818005104/https://collectionsdumusee.philharmoniedeparis.fr/doc/MUSEE/1077984 |url-status= dead }}</ref> Subsequent versions had 72 keys. Combined with a switch that transposes the pitch by one octave, these instruments have a range from C<sub>1</sub> to C<sub>8</sub>.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. XIII}}

Early models can produce only a few [[waveform]]s.{{sfn|Martenot|1931|pp=14–15}} Later models can simultaneously generate [[Sine wave|sine]], peak-limited [[Triangle wave|triangle]], [[Square wave (waveform)|square]], [[Pulse wave|pulse]], and [[Rectifier#Full-wave rectification|full-wave rectified]] sine waves, in addition to [[pink noise]], all controlled by switches in the drawer.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", pp. XVI–XX}} The square wave and full-wave rectified sine wave can be further adjusted by sliders in the drawer. On the Seventh model, a dial at the top of the drawer adjusts the balance between white noise and the other waveforms. A second dial adjusts the balance between the three speakers. A switch chooses between the keyboard and ribbon.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. XX}}

Further adjustments can be made using controls in the body of the instrument. These include several dials for tuning the pitch, a dial for adjusting the overall volume, a switch to transpose the pitch by one octave, and a switch to activate a filter.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. XX}} The drawer of the seventh model also includes six ''transposition'' buttons, which change the pitch by different intervals.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. XX–XXI}} These can be combined to immediately raise the pitch by up to a minor ninth.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. II, p. 72}}

Martenot produced four speakers, called ''diffuseurs'', for the instrument.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", pp. VI–XVII}} The ''Métallique'' features a [[gong]] instead of a [[Diaphragm (acoustics)|speaker cone]], producing a metallic timbre. It was used by the first ondes Martenot quartets in 1932.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. VII}} Another, the ''Palme'' speaker, has a [[resonance chamber]] laced with strings tuned to all 12 [[semitone]]s of an [[octave]]; when a note is played in tune, it resonates a particular string, producing chiming tones.<ref name="McNamee-2009" /><ref name="REYNOLDS2">{{cite web|url=http://www.followmearound.com/presscuttings.php?year=2001&cutting=131|title=Walking on Thin Ice|last=Reynolds|first=Simon|date=July 2001|work=The Wire|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120204233756/http://www.followmearound.com/presscuttings.php?year=2001&cutting=131|archive-date=4 February 2012|url-status=dead|access-date=17 March 2007}}</ref> It was first presented alongside the sixth version of the ondes Martenot in 1950.{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. VIII}}

According to ''[[The Guardian]]'', the ondes Martenot "can be as soothing and moving as a [[string quartet]], but nerve-jangling when gleefully abused".<ref name="McNamee-2009" /> Greenwood described it as "a very accurate theremin that you have far more control of ... When it's played well, you can really emulate the voice."<ref name="REYNOLDS2" /> ''[[The New York Times]]'' described its sound as a "haunting wail".<ref name="Martin-2001" /> Messiaen, discussing its sonic capabilities, describes “an extraterrestrial, enchanted voice", “a cracked bell”, and "a crumbling pile of sand.”{{sfn|Loriod|1987|loc=vol. I, ch. "Preface", p. III}}

== Use ==
=== Classical music ===
The ondes Martenot's popularity was originally owed to its usage in various classical compositions,<ref name="Reid-2002" /> most notably by the French composer [[Olivier Messiaen]]. Although he did not employ it very often, he is the composer most associated with the instrument. Messiaen first used it (six of them, in fact) in ''[[Fête des belles eaux]], a'' 1936-1937 suite of incidental music for the [[Exposition Internationale des Arts et Techniques dans la Vie Moderne]].<ref>{{cite book | last1 = Hill | first1 = Peter |last2=Simeone|first2=Nigel| title = Messiaen | publisher = Yale | year = 2005 | pages = 74–75 | isbn = 978-0-300-10907-8}}</ref> He went on to use it in several more works: ''Deux monodies en quarts de ton'' (1938), ''Musique de scène pour un Œdipe'' (1942; these two were written for solo onde Martenot), ''[[Trois Petites Liturgies de la présence divine]]'' (1943-1944; solo part) and ''[[Saint François d'Assise]]'' (1975-1983; three ondes as part of the orchestra). The most famous of the works to use one, however, was his ''[[Turangalîla-Symphonie]]''. In ''Turangalìla'', Messiaen used it to create "shimmering, swooping musical effects".<ref name="Martin-2001" /> This symphony featured the ondes Martenot and piano as soloists against the backdrop of a large orchestra. It is widely renowned as a masterpiece, and its fame associated the instrument with Messiaen.<ref name="Ratsimandresy-2014">{{Cite journal |last= Ratsimandresy |first= Nadia |date= December 2014 |title= Interaction, onde Martenot and Répertoire |journal=[[Leonardo Music Journal]]|volume= 24 |pages =35–36 |doi= 10.1162/LMJ_a_00195 |s2cid= 57567194|jstor=43832700}}</ref> Messiaen's widow, [[Yvonne Loriod]], arranged and edited four unpublished ''[[Feuillets inédits]]'' for ondes Martenot and piano (originally for voice and piano) which were published in 2001.{{sfn|Bloch|2004|p=7}}

Other composers who used the instrument include [[Arthur Honegger]], [[Claude Vivier]], [[Darius Milhaud]], [[Edgard Varèse]], [[Marcel Landowski]], [[Charles Koechlin]], [[Florent Schmitt]], [[Matyas Seiber]], and [[Jacques Ibert]].<ref name="Martin-2001" /> Honegger's most notable work including the ondes Martenot was his dramatic oratorio, ''[[Jeanne d'Arc au bûcher]]'' in 1935, in which the ondes Martenot's unique sonority was used to augment the string section.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Honegger, Arthur|url=https://www.oxfordmusiconline.com/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-0000013298|access-date=2021-09-12|website=Grove Music Online|year=2001|language=en|doi=10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.13298|isbn=978-1-56159-263-0|last1=Spratt|first1=Geoffrey K.}}</ref> Milhaud, who also enjoyed the unusual nature of the ondes Martenot, used it several times in the 1930s for incidental music.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Milhaud, Darius|url=https://www.oxfordmusiconline.com/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-0000018674|access-date=2021-09-12|website=Grove Music Online|year=2001|language=en|doi=10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.18674|isbn=978-1-56159-263-0|last1=Drake|first1=Jeremy}}</ref> Varèse did not use the ondes Martenot often, but it did appear in the premiere of ''[[Amériques]]'' in Paris; he also replaced the [[theremin]] parts of his ''Ecuatorial'' with ondes Martenot.<ref>{{Cite Grove|last=Griffiths|first=Paul|author-link=Paul Griffiths (writer)|date=2001|title=Varèse, Edgard [Edgar] (Victor Achille Charles)|id=29042}}</ref>

According to ''The New York Times'', the ondes' most celebrated performer was the French musician [[Jeanne Loriod]] (1928–2001), who studied under Martenot at the [[Conservatoire de Paris|Paris Conservatory]]. She performed internationally in more than 500 works, created 85 works for a [[sextet]] of ondes she formed in 1974, and wrote a three-volume book on the instrument, ''Technique de l'Onde Electronique Type Martenot''.<ref name="Martin-2001" /> A British pupil of Loriod, John Morton (1931-2014) from Darlington, performed his own ondes instrument in works by Messiaen, Milhaud, Honegger and Bartok amongst others at the [[Royal Albert Hall]] and elsewhere in the 1970s, as well as on television and radio.<ref>{{Cite news|last=Keeler |first=Stephen |date=2014-12-07 |title=John Morton obituary |url=http://www.theguardian.com/theguardian/2014/dec/07/john-morton-obituary |access-date=2022-09-09 |newspaper=[[The Guardian]]}}</ref>

English composer [[Hugh Davies (composer)|Hugh Davies]] estimated that more than 1,000 works had been composed for the ondes.<ref name="Martin-2001" /> Loriod estimated that there were 15 [[concerto|concerti]] and 300 pieces of chamber music.<ref name="Martin-2001" /> The instrument was also popular in French theatres such as the [[Comédie-Française]], the [[Théâtre National Populaire]] and the [[Folies Bergère]].<ref>{{Cite web|title=Ondes martenot|url=https://www.oxfordmusiconline.com/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-0000020343|access-date=2021-09-12|website=Grove Music Online|year=2001|language=en|doi=10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.20343|isbn=978-1-56159-263-0|last1=Orton|first1=Richard|last2=Davies|first2=Hugh|editor-first1=Hugh |editor-last1=Davies }}</ref>

[[Thomas Adès]]'s opera ''[[The Exterminating Angel (opera)|The Exterminating Angel]]'' makes extensive use of the ondes Martenot, which Adès says "could be considered the voice of the exterminating angel".<ref>{{cite news |title=Hear the Surreal Instruments of the Met's New Opera |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2017/10/24/arts/music/hear-the-surreal-instruments-of-the-mets-new-opera.html |newspaper=[[The New York Times]]|date=24 October 2017 |last1=Cooper |first1=Michael }}</ref>

=== Film and television ===
The ondes Martenot has featured in many films, particularly [[Science fiction film|science fiction]] and [[horror film]]s.<ref name="Battaglia-2014" /> In 1934 Arthur Honegger used the ondes Martenot in his soundtrack for the 1932 French animated film ''[[The Idea (1932 film)|The Idea]]'' ({{langx|fr|'L'Idée'|link=no}}) by Austro-Hungarian filmmaker [[Berthold Bartosch]], believed to be the first use of electronic music in film. In 1936 [[Adolphe Borchard]] used it in [[Sacha Guitry]]'s ''Le roman d'un tricheur'', played by Martenot's sister, [[Ginette Martenot|Ginette]].<ref>{{cite book|title=Les Cloches d'Atlantis. Musique électroacoustique et cinéma. Archéologie et histoire d'un art sonore|author=Philippe Langlois|date=2012|publisher=Éditions MF|location=Paris|page=119}}</ref> It was used by composer [[Brian Easdale]] in the ballet music for ''[[The Red Shoes (1948 film)|The Red Shoes]]''.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.powell-pressburger.org/Reviews/Easdale/Proms.html|title=Brian Easdale at the Proms|website=www.powell-pressburger.org|access-date=2018-09-07}}</ref> 

French composer [[Maurice Jarre]] introduced the ondes Martenot to American cinema in his [[Lawrence of Arabia (soundtrack)|score]] for ''[[Lawrence of Arabia (film)|Lawrence of Arabia]]'' (1962).<ref>{{cite book|title=A History of Film Music|author=Mervyn Cooke|date=2008|publisher=Cambridge University Press|location=Cambridge and New York|page=333}}</ref> Composer [[Harry Lubin]] created cues for ''[[The Loretta Young Show]]'', ''[[Alcoa Presents: One Step Beyond|One Step Beyond]]'' and ''[[The Outer Limits (1963 TV series)|The Outer Limits]]'' featured the instrument, as did the first-season ''[[Lost in Space]]'' (1965) theme by [[John Williams]]. The composer [[Danny Elfman]] used the ondes Martenot in the soundtrack to the comedy science fiction film ''[[Mars Attacks!]]'' He originally intended to use a theremin, but was unable to find a musician who could play one.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.inverse.com/entertainment/mars-attacks-oral-history|work=[[Inverse (website)|Inverse]]|title=The Oral History of ''Mars Attacks'', Tim Burton's Misunderstood Sci-Fi Masterpiece|first=Ralph|last=Jones|date=13 December 2021|access-date=17 December 2021}}</ref> The director [[Lucile Hadžihalilović|Lucille Hadžihalilović]] used the ondes Martenot in her film [[Evolution (2015 film)|''Evolution'']] (2015) as it "brings a certain melancholy, almost a human voice, and it instantly creates a particular atmosphere".<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.electricsheepmagazine.co.uk/features/2016/05/06/evolution-interview-with-lucile-hadzihalilovicz/|title=Evolution: Interview with Lucile Hadzihalilovicz|date=2016-05-06|work=Electric Sheep|access-date=2017-10-03|language=en-US}}</ref> Other film scores that use the ondes Martenot include ''[[A Passage to India (film)|A Passage to India]]'', ''[[Amélie|Amelie]]'', ''[[Bodysong]]'',<ref name="McNamee-2009" /> ''[[There Will Be Blood]]'' ([[2007 in film|2007]]), ''[[Hugo (film)|Hugo]]'' ([[2011 in film|2011]])<ref>Howard Shore, Hugo – Original Score, CD booklet, published by Paramount Pictures in the year 2011</ref> and ''[[Manta Ray (film)|Manta Ray]]''.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.lfetranger.fr/single-post/2017/09/07/the-french-composer-christine-ott-on-the-waves-of-manta-ray|title=The french composer Christine Ott on the waves of Manta Ray|date=7 September 2017 |publisher=LFE}}</ref>

The ondes Martenot is the subject of the 2013 Quebec documentary ''[[Wavemakers]]''.<ref name="Dunlevy">{{cite news|url=https://montrealgazette.com/entertainment/movie-guide/Review+chant+Ondes/8098931/story.html|title=Review: Le chant des Ondes|last=Dunlevy|first=T'cha|date=13 March 2013|work=[[Montreal Gazette]]|access-date=28 November 2014}}</ref> It is used in a performance of the fourth movement of Messiaen's ''Fête des belles eaux'' in an episode from the third season of the [[Amazon Studios|Amazon]] series ''[[Mozart in the Jungle]]'', where a musician plays the ondes Martenot to inmates on [[Rikers Island]] (with piano accompaniment substituting the three other ondes).<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.wqxr.org/story/new-season-mozart-jungle-features-real-world-performance/|title=New Season of ''Mozart in the Jungle'' Features a Unique Performance|work=[[WQXR-FM|WQXR]]|access-date=2017-10-27|language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=http://flavorwire.com/595680/how-mozart-in-the-jungle-brought-an-obscure-avant-garde-piece-of-classical-music-to-rikers-island|title=How ''Mozart in the Jungle'' Brought an Obscure, Avant-Garde Piece of Classical Music to Rikers Island|date=2016-12-13|work=Flavorwire|access-date=2017-10-27}}</ref> The English composer [[Barry Gray]] studied the ondes Martenot with Martenot in Paris, and used it in his soundtracks for 1960s films ''including [[Fahrenheit 451 (1966 film)|Fahrenheit 451]]'', ''[[Dr. Who and the Daleks]]'' and [[Journey to the Far Side of the Sun|''Journey'' ''to the Far Side of the Sun'']].<ref name="Reid-2002" /> The ondes Martenot is sometimes said to have been used in the original [[Theme from Star Trek|''Star Trek'' theme]]; the part was in fact performed by a singer.<ref name="McNamee-2009" />

== Legacy ==
In 2001, the ''New York Times'' described the ondes, along with other early electronic instruments such as the theremin, [[telharmonium]], [[trautonium]], and [[Electric organ|orgatron]], as part of a "futuristic electric music movement that never went remotely as far as its pioneers dreamed ... proponents of the new wired music delighted in making previously unimaginable noises".<ref name="Martin-2001" /> The French classical musician [[Thomas Bloch]] said: "The ondes martenot is probably the most musical of all electric instruments ... Martenot was not only interested in sounds. He wanted to use electricity to increase and control the expression, the musicality. Everything is made by the musician in real time, including the control of the vibrato, the intensity, and the attack. It is an important step in our electronic instrument lineage."<ref name="Battaglia-2014" />

According to music journalist [[Alex Ross (music critic)|Alex Ross]], fewer than 100 people had mastered the ondes Martenot as of 2001.<ref name=Ross>{{cite magazine|url=http://www.therestisnoise.com/2004/04/mahler_1.html|title=The Searchers: Radiohead's unquiet revolution|last=Ross|first=Alex|author-link=Alex Ross (music critic)|date=21 August 2001|magazine=[[The New Yorker]]|access-date=14 June 2007|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070525102645/http://www.therestisnoise.com/2004/04/mahler_1.html <!-- Bot retrieved archive -->|archive-date=25 May 2007}}</ref> In 1997, [[Mark Singer (journalist)|Mark Singer]] wrote for ''[[The Wire (magazine)|The Wire]]'' that it would likely remain obscure: "The fact is that any instrument with no institutional grounding of second- and third-raters, no spectral army of amateurs, will wither and vanish: how can it not? Specialist virtuosos may arrive to tackle the one-off novelty ... but there's no meaningful level of entry at the ground floor, and, what's worse, no fallback possibility of rank careerism if things don't turn out."<ref name="Martin-2001" />

The ondes Martenot's electronics are fragile, and it includes a powder which transfers electric currents, which Martenot would mix in different quantities according to musicians' specifications; the precise proportions are unknown. Attempts to construct new ondes Martenot models using Martenot's original specifications have had variable results.<ref name="Battaglia-2014" />

Several instruments have taken inspiration from elements of the ondes Martenot. In the 1930s, Frenchman Georges Jenny took inspiration from the Martenot's vibrato keyboard to create the [[Ondioline]] as a low-cost alternative to the Martenot.<ref name="ondioline.com">{{cite web | title=Ondioline | website=Ondioline | url=https://ondioline.com/ | access-date=2025-08-20}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |last1=Brend |first1=Mark |title=Synth French Connection: Ondioline and Ondes Martenot |url=https://reverb.com/news/synth-french-connection-ondioline-and-ondes-martenot |access-date=14 March 2025 |publisher=Reverb.com |date=October 14, 2020}}</ref> The [[Yamaha GX-1]] synthesiser, released in the 1970s, featured two lateral-vibrato keyboard manuals inspired by the Martenot.<ref name="Reid-2002" /><ref>{{cite book|title = Yamaha GX-1 Owner's Manual|year = 1974|url=http://www.synthfool.com/docs/Yamaha/Yamaha%20GX-1%20Owners%20Manual.pdf}}</ref> In 2000, [[Jonny Greenwood]] of the English rock band [[Radiohead]] commissioned the synthesiser company Analogue Systems to develop a replica of the ondes Martenot, as he was nervous about damaging his instrument on tour. The replica, called the French Connection, imitates the ondes Martenot's control mechanism, but does not generate sound; instead, it controls an external [[Voltage-controlled oscillator|oscillator]].<ref name="Reid-2002" /> In 2012, the Canadian company [[Therevox]] began selling a synthesizer with an interface based on the ondes Martenot pitch ring and intensity key.<ref>{{cite book |last=Laurendeau |first=Jean |date=2017 |page=367 |title=Maurice Martenot: Luthier de l'électronique |publisher=Beauchesne éditeur}}</ref>

There have also been more thorough replicas of the ondes Martenot. A replica called Ondéa was created by Ambro Oliva in the early-2000s. It went out of production in 2011 due to bankruptcy, but was still in use by professional Ondists in 2018.<ref name="Schampaert">{{cite thesis |last=Schampaert |first=Dorien |date=December 2018 |title=The Ondes Martenot Network in the Twenty-First Century: The Co-Construction of the Ondes Martenot and its Users |url=http://www.ondes.net/publications/The%20Ondes%20Martenot%20Network%20in%20the%20Twenty-First%20Century%20-%20The%20Co-Construction%20of%20the%20Ondes%20Martenot%20and%20its%20Users%20-%20Dorien%20Schampaert.pdf |location=The University of Leeds |access-date=14 Mar 2025}}</ref> In 2011 ''[[Sound on Sound]]'' wrote that original ondes Martenot models were "all but impossible to obtain or afford, and unless you can stump up 12,000 Euros for one of Jean‑Loup Dierstein's new reproduction instruments, the dream of owning a real Ondes is likely to remain such".<ref name="Reid-2002" /> In 2016, David Kean of Audities Studios began producing a new version of the Ondéa, after obtaining the original design from Ambro Oliva in circa-2012.<ref>{{cite web | title=Home | website=Ondes Martenot | date=1970-01-01 | url=https://www.ondesmartenot.com/ | access-date=2025-08-20}}</ref>{{Better source needed|reason=Citation is the Ondea' company website, which may not be entirely objective.|date=March 2025}} In 2017, the Japanese company Asaden manufactured 100 Ondomo instruments, a portable version of the ondes Martenot.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.soundonsound.com/news/ondomo-revitalising-90-year-old-electronic-instrument|title=Ondomo is revitalising a 90-year-old electronic instrument|date=22 April 2017|website=[[Sound on Sound]]|language=en-gb|access-date=5 September 2018}}</ref>

In 2025, American musician Joe Edelmann announced GreenWaves, an inspired replica of the Ondes Martenot developed under the IdioTech Team. The instrument features several modern capabilities, including MIDI control, both to control the device with an external controller or to use it as a controller for another device, waveform manipulation, built-in effects, intelligent harmonization, and preset storage. Edelmann also announced that GreenWaves would receive regular updates to its I/O system.
