The '''serpent''' is a [[bass (sound)|low-pitched]] early [[wind instrument]] in the [[brass instrument|lip-reed family]], developed in the [[Renaissance]] era. It has a [[trombone]]-like [[mouthpiece (brass)|mouthpiece]], with six [[tone hole]]s arranged in two groups of three [[fingering (music)#Woodwind instruments|fingered]] by each hand. It is named for its long, [[conical bore]] bent into a snakelike shape, and unlike most brass instruments is made from wood with an outer covering of leather or [[parchment]]. A distant ancestor of the [[tuba]], the serpent is related to the [[cornett]] and was used for [[bassline|bass parts]] from the 17th to the early 19th centuries.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|page=128–31|loc="serpent"}}

In the early 19th century, [[Key (instrument)|keys]] were added to improve intonation, and several upright variants were developed and used, until they were superseded first by the [[ophicleide]] and ultimately by the [[brass instrument valve|valved]] tuba. After almost entirely disappearing from orchestras, the serpent experienced a renewed interest in [[historically informed performance]] practice in the mid-20th century. Several contemporary works have been commissioned and composed, and serpents are again made by a small number of contemporary manufacturers.
The sound or ''timbre'' of a serpent is somewhere between a [[bassoon]] and a [[euphonium]], and it is typically played in a seated position, with the instrument resting upright between the player's knees.

== Construction ==

Although closely related to the [[cornett]], the serpent has thinner walls, a more conical bore, and no thumb-hole.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=64}} The original serpent was typically built from [[hardwood]], usually [[Juglans|walnut]] or other [[tonewood]]s like [[Maple#Tonewood|maple]], [[cherry]], or [[Pear#Timber|pear]], or sometimes softer woods like [[Populus|poplar]]. In France, the instrument was made from bonding two double-S-shaped halves, each carved from a single large piece of wood. In England, it was usually made from several smaller curved tubular wooden segments, each made by gluing two hollowed halves together. The whole instrument was then glued and bound with an outer covering of leather.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=66}} A small number were made instead from copper or brass; one brass serpent in the [[Museo internazionale e biblioteca della musica]] in Bologna was built in 1773 with an added outer layer of leather.<ref>{{cite journal |title=More Exciting News |journal=Serpent Newsletter |date=2022 |first=Michel |last=Godard |author-link=Michel Godard (musician) |issue=April 2022 |page=8 |url=http://www.serpentwebsite.com/sn_apr22.pdf#page=8 |access-date=15 August 2024 |archive-date=15 August 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240815070945/http://www.serpentwebsite.com/sn_apr22.pdf#page=8 |url-status=live }}</ref>

The instrument uses a [[mouthpiece (brass)|mouthpiece]] about the same size as a [[trombone]] mouthpiece, originally made from ivory, horn or hardwood. The cup profiles of most historical serpent and bass horn mouthpieces were a distinctly hemispherical or ovate bowl shape, with a sharper-edged and narrower "throat" at the bottom of the cup than modern trombone mouthpieces, before expanding more-or-less conically through the shank.<ref name="Kridel-2023">{{Cite journal |title=Russian Bassoons and Bass Horns: A Comfortable Uncertainty |first=Craig |last=Kridel |journal=ITEA Journal |date=2023 |volume=50 |number=3 |page=28-29 |publisher=[[International Tuba Euphonium Association]] |url= https://berliozhistoricalbrass.org/50-3_Spr23.pdf |access-date=6 November 2024 |via=Berlioz Historical Brass }}</ref> The mouthpiece fits into the ''[[bocal]]'' or ''crook'', a small length of brass tubing that emerges from the top wooden segment.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=66}}

The serpent has six [[tone hole]]s, in two groups of three, [[fingering (music)|fingered]] by each hand.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=66}} It is initially challenging to play the instrument with good [[intonation (music)|intonation]], due in large part to the positions of the tone holes.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=74–75}} They are arranged mainly to be accessible to the player's fingers, rather than in acoustically correct positions, which for some of them would be out of reach.{{sfn|Herbert|Myers|Wallace|2019|p=373 |loc="Serpent"}}{{sfn|Yeo|2019|p=9}} The lower tone holes are too small to act effectively to shorten the air column, but by dampening resonances they nevertheless aid note selection and contribute to the serpent's characteristic soft {{lang|fr|[[timbre]]}}.{{sfn|Campbell|Gilbert|Myers|2021|p=382-385}} While early serpents were keyless, later instruments added [[Key (instrument)|keys]] for additional holes out of reach of the fingers to improve intonation, and extend range.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=66}} A mid-19th century model by London instrument maker [[Thomas Key (instrument maker)|Thomas Key]] has 14 keys, and survives in [[St Fagans National Museum of History]] in Cardiff, Wales.<ref name="stfagans-14keys">{{Cite web |title=Serpent (early-mid 19th century) |last1=Key |last2=Thomas |publisher=[[St Fagans National Museum of History]] |publication-place=Cardiff |work=Collections Online |id=Accession: 91.6 |url=https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/d80ab911-0b78-3ade-a598-79aed2771ee9/Serpent/ |access-date=8 August 2024 |archive-date=8 August 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240808041246/https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/d80ab911-0b78-3ade-a598-79aed2771ee9/Serpent/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

Modern replicas are made by several specialist instrument makers, employing acoustic analysis and modern fabrication materials and techniques to further improve the serpent's intonation. === Sizes ===

[[File:Doug Yeo with serpent family.jpg|thumb|[[Douglas Yeo]] with serpents in four sizes, from largest to smallest: contrabass, bass, tenor, and soprano]]
The majority of surviving specimens in museums and private collections are nominally '''{{Visible anchor|Bass serpent|text=bass serpents}}''' built in [[Eight-foot pitch|eight-foot (8′)]] C, thus having a total tubing length of about {{convert|8|feet}}. A few slightly smaller specimens were built in D, and military serpents could sometimes vary in pitch between D♭ and B♭.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=74–75}}

The '''{{Visible anchor|Tenor serpent|text=tenor serpent}}''' (or '''{{Visible anchor|Serpet|text=serpet}}'''; {{langx|fr|'''serpenteau'''}}) arose in the 1960s when English early music specialist and instrument maker [[Christopher Monk]] began his efforts to produce modern reproduction serpents by first building a half-sized pattern, which equated to a tenor size in 4′ C. Through a [[pantograph]]ic [[Milling (machining)|milling process]], he scaled up the pattern by 2× to carve a bass serpent. Since he could just as easily carve at 1×, he was also able to produce tenor serpents popular in serpent ensembles, and usable by players with smaller fingers. The '''{{Visible anchor|Soprano serpent|text=soprano serpent}}''', or '''{{Visible anchor|Worm|text=worm}}''', was built by scaling the tenor serpent by 0.5× to produce an instrument in 2′ C, two octaves higher than the standard serpent. It first appeared in the 1980s, made as a novelty instrument by Monk. There is no repertoire or other evidence of the historical existence of these sizes.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|p=170|loc="worm"}}{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=79}}

[[File:Contrabass Serpent - 'The Anaconda'.webm|thumb|start=8|thumbtime=12|Contrabass serpent, {{circa|1840}}, being played at [[St Cecilia's Hall]], [[University of Edinburgh]]<ref name="mimed-anaconda"/>]]
The '''{{Visible anchor|Contrabass serpent|text=contrabass serpent}}''', nicknamed the '''{{Visible anchor|Anaconda|text=anaconda}}''' and built in 16′ C one octave below the serpent, was an English invention of the mid-19th century with no historical repertoire.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=77–79}} The prototype instrument was built {{circa|1840}} by Joseph and Richard Wood in [[Huddersfield]] as a double-sized English military serpent, and survives in the [[University of Edinburgh]] museum collection.<ref name="anaconda">{{cite journal |title=The 'Anaconda' |first=R. Morley |last=Pegge |journal=The Galpin Society Journal |volume=12 |date=May 1959 |pages=53–56 |publisher=Galpin Society |doi=10.2307/841945 |jstor=841945 |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/841945 |access-date=18 May 2023 |archive-date=18 May 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230518141631/https://www.jstor.org/stable/841945 |url-status=live |url-access=subscription }}</ref><ref name="mimed-anaconda">{{Cite web |title="Contrabass serpent, nominal pitch: 16-ft C" |work=Musical Instruments Museums Edinburgh |publisher=University of Edinburgh |publication-place=[[St Cecilia's Hall]] |url=https://collections.ed.ac.uk/mimed/record/18242 |access-date=30 May 2023 |id=Accession: L 2929 |archive-date=5 April 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230405232321/https://collections.ed.ac.uk/mimed/record/18242 |url-status=live }}</ref> Its use of keys, progressively larger tone holes, and an open top tone hole make it essentially a wooden, serpent-shaped contrabass ophicleide.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|p=10|loc="anaconda"}} During the serpent's modern revival, two more contrabass serpents were built in the original ''serpent ordinaire'' form in the 1990s by Christopher Monk's workshop, by doubling the pattern for a bass serpent. They were called "George" and "George II".{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=79}} The first, commissioned by musicologist and serpent player Philip Palmer, was owned by American trombonist and serpent player [[Douglas Yeo]] for a time and features in some of his serpent recordings.<ref name="monde-cd-notes">{{cite AV media notes |first=Douglas |last=Yeo |author-link=Douglas Yeo |title=Le Monde du Serpent |type=CD booklet |date=2003 |publisher=Berlioz Historic Brass |id=BHB CD101 |url=https://www.yeodoug.com/publications/le_monde_du_serpent/le_monde_du_serpent_notes.html |access-date=20 June 2023 |archive-date=3 October 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231003033116/https://www.yeodoug.com/publications/le_monde_du_serpent/le_monde_du_serpent_notes.html |url-status=live }}</ref> At least four other contrabass serpents have also been built: one from [[Plastic pipework|PVC piping]] in 1986, two from box plywood based on a "squarpent" design by American serpent player and curator Paul Schmidt, and one in 2014 from spare tuba and sousaphone parts.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Contrabass Serpent Photo Gallery |last=Schmidt |first=Paul |work=The Serpent Website |date=2014<!-- last updated --> |url=http://www.serpentwebsite.com/CBS_gallery.htm |access-date=5 September 2024 |archive-date=20 November 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231120062056/https://www.serpentwebsite.com/CBS_gallery.htm |url-status=live }}</ref>

== History ==

There is little direct material or documentary evidence for the exact origin of the serpent. French historian [[Jean Lebeuf]] claimed in his 1743 work ''Mémoires Concernant l'Histoire Ecclésiastique et Civile d’Auxerre'' that the serpent was invented in 1590 by Edmé Guillaume, a clergyman in [[Auxerre]], France, which is generally accepted.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=65}} Some scholars propose that the serpent may have evolved from large, curved bass [[cornett]]s that were in use in Italy in the 16th century,<ref>{{cite Q|Q113004406|first=Herbert |last=Heyde |chapter=Zoomorphic and Theatrical Musical Instruments in the Late Italian Renaissance and Baroque Eras |editor1-first=Renato |editor1-last=Meucci |editor2-first=Franca |editor2-last=Falletti |editor3-first=Gabriele |editor3-last=Rossi Rognoni }}</ref> but the lack of knowledge of the serpent in early 17th century Italy, or surviving early serpents outside of France, counts against this idea.{{sfn|Hostiou|2021|p=55–60}} The serpent was certainly used in France since the early 17th century, to strengthen the [[cantus firmus]] and bass voices of [[choir]]s in [[plainchant]].<ref>{{cite journal |author=Christopher Holman |title=Rhythm and metre in French Classical plainchant |journal=Early Music |volume=45 |issue=4 |date=November 2017 |page=657–64 |doi=10.1093/em/cax087 }}</ref> This original traditional serpent was known as the {{lang|fr|serpent ordinaire}} or {{lang|fr|serpent d'église}} ({{lit.|ordinary serpent|church serpent}}).

Around the middle of the 18th century, the serpent began to appear in [[military band]]s, [[Chamber music|chamber ensembles]], and later in [[orchestra]]s. In England, particularly in the south, the serpent was used in [[west gallery music]] played in [[Church of England parish church]]es and village bands until the mid 19th century.<ref name="Grove-Gallery-music">{{Cite Grove|id=43466 |last=Drage |first=Sally |title=Gallery music |url= https://www.oxfordmusiconline.com/grovemusic/display/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-0000043466 |access-date=15 November 2024 |url-access=subscription }}</ref>

Towards the end of the 18th century, the increased popularity of the serpent in [[military band]]s drove the subsequent development of the instrument to accommodate [[marching band|marching]] or [[mounted band|mounted]] players. In England, a distinct ''military serpent'' was developed which had a more compact shape with tighter curves, added extra keys to improve its intonation, and metal braces between the bends to increase its rigidity and durability.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|page=128–31|loc="serpent"}}
In France around the same time several makers produced a {{lang|fr|serpent militaire}} initially developed by Piffault (by whose name they are also known) that arranges the tubing vertically with an upward turned bell, reminiscent of a [[tenor saxophone]].{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=79}}

Several vertical configurations of the serpent, generally known as ''upright serpents'' ({{langx|fr|serpent droit}}) or ''bass horns'', were developed from the late 18th century. Retaining the same six tone holes and fingering of the original serpent, these instruments resemble the [[bassoon]], with jointed straight tubes that fit into a short U-shaped butt joint, and an upward-pointing bell.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|page=128–31|loc="serpent"}}

==== Basson russe<span class="anchor" id="Russian bassoon"></span> ====
Among the first of the upright serpents to appear around the turn of the 19th century was the {{lang|fr|basson russe}}, {{lit.|Russian bassoon}}, although it was neither Russian nor a bassoon. The name is possibly a corruption of {{lang|fr|basson prusse}} since they were taken up by the [[Prussia]]n army bands of the time.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=81}} Many of these instruments were built in [[Lyon]] and often had the [[buccin]]-style decorative [[protome|zoomorphic]] bells popular in France at the time, shaped and painted like a dragon or serpent head.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|page=128–31|loc="serpent"}} Appearing around the same time in military bands was the {{lang|fr|serpent à pavillon}} ({{lit.|bell serpent}}) which had a normal brass instrument bell, similar in flare to the later [[ophicleide]].<ref name="kridel-itea-2003">{{cite journal |title=Questions and Answers: Bass Horns and Russian Bassoons |first=Craig |last=Kridel |journal=ITEA Journal |publisher=[[International Tuba Euphonium Association]] |date=2003 |volume=30 |issue=4 |page=73–5 |url=http://www.berliozhistoricalbrass.org/THE%20FINAL%20bass%20horn.pdf |access-date=12 July 2022 |archive-date=26 March 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230326104031/http://www.berliozhistoricalbrass.org/THE%20FINAL%20bass%20horn.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref>

==== English bass horn<span class="anchor" id="Bass horn"></span> ====
The ''English bass horn'', developed by London-based French musician and inventor Louis Alexandre Frichot in 1799, had an all-metal V-shaped construction, described by German composer [[Felix Mendelssohn]] as resembling a watering can. He admired its sound however, and wrote for the instrument in several of his works, including the overture to ''[[A Midsummer Night's Dream (Mendelssohn)|A Midsummer Night's Dream]]'' (1826) and his [[Symphony No. 5 (Mendelssohn)|fifth "Reformation" symphony]] (1830),{{sfn|Yeo|2021|page=128–31|loc="serpent"}} although when the ''Overture'' was first published, the part was changed to ophicleide for unknown reasons.<ref name="Hogwood-2006">{{Cite book|editor-last=Hogwood |editor-first=Christopher |editor-link=Christopher Hogwood |date=2006 |title=Mendelssohn Bartholdy: Sommernachtstraum Konzert-Ouvertüre: Introduction |publisher=[[Bärenreiter]] |publication-place=Kassel |page=VII–VIII |ismn=979-0-0065-2313-9 |url= https://www.barenreiter.co.uk/a-midsummer-night-s-dream-op-21-concert-overture-full-score.html |access-date=5 September 2024}}</ref> The bass horn was popular in civic and military bands in Britain and Ireland, and also spread back into orchestras in Europe, where it influenced the inventors of both the ophicleide and later the {{lang|de|Baß-Tuba}}.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=86–89}}

==== Early cimbasso ====
The serpent appears as {{lang|it|serpentone}} in early 19th century [[Italian opera]]tic scores by composers such as [[Spontini]], [[Gioachino Rossini|Rossini]], and [[Vincenzo Bellini|Bellini]].<ref name="touroude-2011"/> In Italy it was replaced by the ''[[cimbasso]]'', a loose term that referred to several instruments; initially an upright serpent similar to the ''basson russe'', then the ophicleide, early forms of valved [[tuba]] ({{lang|it|pelittone}}, {{lang|it|bombardone}}), and finally by the time of [[Giuseppe Verdi|Verdi]]'s opera ''[[Otello]]'' (1887), a valve [[contrabass trombone]].{{sfn|Meucci|1996|p=158}}

==== Other upright serpents<span class="anchor" id="Ophimonocleide"></span> ====
In Paris in 1823, Forveille invented the {{lang|fr|serpent Forveille}}, an upright serpent with an enlarged bell section influenced by the (then newly invented) ophicleide. It is distinguished by being made from wood, brass tubing being used only for the leadpipe and first bend.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=82}} It became popular in bands for its improved intonation and sound quality.{{sfn|Yeo|2021|page=128–31|loc="serpent"}} In 1828 Jean-Baptiste Coëffet patented his ''ophimonocleide'' ("snake with one key"), one of the last forms of the upright serpent.<ref name="touroude-2011">{{Cite web |title=Compte rendu du colloque "le serpent sans sornettes" du 6 et 7 septembre 2011 aux Invalides à Paris |language=fr |last=Touroude |first=José-Daniel |work=Archives Musique, Facteurs, Marchands, Luthiers |date=9 November 2011 |url=https://rp-archivesmusiquefacteurs.blogspot.com/2011/11/compte-rendu-du-colloque-le-serpent.html |access-date=12 July 2022 |archive-date=12 July 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220712034947/https://rp-archivesmusiquefacteurs.blogspot.com/2011/11/compte-rendu-du-colloque-le-serpent.html |url-status=live }}</ref> It solved a perennial problem of the serpent, its difficult and indistinct B♮ notes. The instrument is built a semitone lower in B♮ and adds a large open tone hole that keeps the instrument in C until its key is pressed, closing the tone hole and producing a clear and resonant B♮. The instrument also has a unique {{lang|fr|pompe}}, a double [[tuning slide]] that (combined with adjusting the bocal) could change the pitch by up to a major second and allow the player to switch between the different [[History of pitch standards in Western music|pitch standards]] of the time ({{lang|fr|diapason de l'opéra}} and {{lang|fr|diapason de la cathédrale}}).<ref name="kridel-itea-2019">{{cite journal |title=The Ophimonocleide: Folly or Genius? |first=Craig |last=Kridel |journal=ITEA Journal |publisher=[[International Tuba Euphonium Association]] |date=2019 |volume=46 |issue=2 |page=30–3 |url=http://www.berliozhistoricalbrass.org/46-2_Winter_2019.pdf |access-date=12 July 2022 |archive-date=8 August 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240808030209/http://www.berliozhistoricalbrass.org/46-2_Winter_2019.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref>

=== Contemporary revival ===
[[File:London Serpent Trio 1989.jpg|thumb|The London Serpent Trio, 1989: Andrew van der Beek (''left''), Christopher Monk (''center''), Alan Lumsden (''right'')]]
The era of upright serpents was brief, spanning the first half of the 19th century from their invention to their replacement by the ophicleide and subsequent [[brass instrument valve|valved]] brass instruments.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=83, 89}} German opera composer [[Richard Wagner]], by the 1869 première of his ''[[Der Ring des Nibelungen]]'' cycle, was writing his lowest brass parts for tuba and contrabass trombone.{{sfn|Herbert|Wallace|1997|p=150 |loc=The low brass }} Consequently, the serpent had all but disappeared from ensembles by 1900.

The serpent has enjoyed a modern revival of interest and manufacture since the mid-20th century. Christopher Monk began building his own replica cornetts and serpents and playing them in [[historically informed performance]]s. In 1968 he and instrument maker Keith Rogers devised a method of constructing cornetts inexpensively from a composite wood-resin material, which helped to raise interest in these instruments and increase their availability. In 1976 he established the London Serpent Trio with English [[Early Music Consort]] players Andrew van der Beek and Alan Lumsden, performing new works and historical arrangements, both serious and whimsical, throughout Europe and North America.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=122}}<ref name="monk-obit">{{cite news |title=Obituary: Christopher Monk |newspaper=The Independent |date=20 July 1991 |first=Andrew |last=van der Beek |url=https://www.lacock.org/people/christopher-monk |access-date=29 May 2023 |via=Lacock |archive-date=8 August 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240808030214/https://www.lacock.org/people/christopher-monk |url-status=live }}</ref> At the same time in France, historical instrument specialist Bernard Fourtet and jazz musician [[Michel Godard (musician)|Michel Godard]] began promoting use of the serpent, and reintroduced serpent teaching at the [[Conservatoire de Paris]].<ref name="ribo-historie">{{Cite web |title=Historie |language=fr |first=Pierre |last=Ribo |publisher=Serpent Ribo |publication-place=Brussels |url= https://www.serpent-ribo.com/index.php/histoire/ |access-date=29 May 2023 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20181221174932/http://www.serpent-ribo.com/index.php/histoire/ |archive-date=21 December 2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> Among the graduates are Volny Hostiou, who has recorded a significant serpent discography, and Patrick Wibart, also an accomplished ophicleide player.<ref name="Hostiou-profile"/><ref name="Wibart-review">{{Cite web |title=Trio Aenea: The Virtuoso Ophicleide |type=review |last=Schmidt |first=Paul |work=Historic Brass Society |date=26 April 2016 |url= https://historicbrass.org/features/reviews/recording-reviews/trio-aenea-the-virtuoso-ophicleide |access-date=14 August 2024 }}</ref> Wibart succeeded Godard as the serpent teacher at the Conservatoire de Paris, and also teaches serpent at the {{ill|Conservatoire de Versailles Grand Parc|fr|Conservatoire à rayonnement régional de Versailles|lt=Conservatoire de Versailles Grand Parc}}.<ref name="SN-2024-09-Wibart">{{cite journal |title=More Exciting News |journal=Serpent Newsletter |date=2024 |editor-first=Paul |editor-last=Schmidt |issue=September 2024 |page=12 }}</ref>

The ''Serpent Newsletter'', first published in 1986 as the ''Newsletter for United Serpents'', is published twice a year and edited by Paul Schmidt. It reviews new recordings and publications, covers contemporary performances and events, as well as new discoveries, appearances in media and film, and other developments.<ref name="newsletter">{{Cite web |title=Serpent Newsletter Backissues |last=Schmidt |first=Paul |work=The Serpent Website |url= https://www.serpentwebsite.com/backissues.htm |access-date=18 January 2025 }}</ref>

== Range and performance ==

{{Image frame | caption = Range of the serpent; notes below C₂ are obtained with the embouchure{{sfn |Herbert|Myers|Wallace|2019|p=490|loc=Appendix 2: The Ranges of Labrosones}}

The serpent's range typically covers the two and a half [[octave]]s from C{{sub|2}} two [[octave]]s below [[middle C]] to G{{sub|4}}. The range can easily extend downwards to A<sub>1</sub> or even F<sub>1</sub> by fingering the low C note with all holes covered, and producing [[Falset (music)|falset]] tones, by slackening the [[embouchure]] ("lipping down"). Proficient players can play upwards to C{{sub|5}}.{{sfn|Herbert|Myers|Wallace|2019|p=371|loc="Serpent"}}

Monk writes that due to its inherent mechanical and acoustical defects, the serpent is one of the more difficult wind instruments to play well.{{sfn|Monk|1986|loc=Introduction}} Although played with an embouchure similar to that used with other brass instruments such as the trombone, the instrument is easily over-blown so the player must use a more gentle air stream. The tone holes do not always serve to shorten the air column as they do in [[woodwind instrument]]s. The lower tone holes in particular are too small and behave more like air leaks, working largely by dampening resonances in the air column, thus altering the note selection and influencing the timbre.{{sfn|Campbell|Gilbert|Myers|2021|p=382-385}} Consequently, the player must rely much more on a strong, controlled embouchure to produce the correct pitch than on other brass instruments. The serpent's natural tones with all holes covered (the [[Harmonic series (music)|harmonic series]] on C{{sub|2}}) are fuller and richer than those produced with fingering, so the player must also focus on consistency of timbre and a ''[[Singing|cantabile]]'' approach throughout the serpent's range.{{sfn|Monk|1986|loc=Introduction}}

[[File:Carte postale - En Normandie - Le serpent du village (crop).png|thumb|upright|Serpentist holding the instrument horizontally, with reversed right-hand fingering]]
The serpent was originally played vertically, with each hand covering one of the two sets of tone holes from above. In 1772 Abbé Lunel, serpentist at [[Notre-Dame de Paris]], devised a way to hold the serpent horizontally, a method subsequently used in military bands.{{sfn|Roze|attrib. Métoyen|1814|loc="Manières de tenir le serpent"}} Holding the serpent this way involves supporting the lower curve with the right hand under, rather than over, the lower three tone holes, thus reversing the order of the fingering.{{sfn|Yeo|2019|p=11}}

Production of pitch can be lowered by a semitone or more by slackening the embouchure given the same fingering. This is due to the serpent's coupling of a "strong" acoustical system of embouchure and mouthpiece, with the relatively "weak" system of the air column in the serpent body. This results in the mouthpiece having a stronger influence on selecting the instrument pitch than the air column.{{sfn|Hostiou|2021|p=61–62}} The mismatch of embouchure and air column length also contributes to its timbre.{{sfn|Campbell|Gilbert|Myers|2021|p=382-385}}

== Repertoire ==

[[File:Adrabesa Quartet 12.jpg|thumb|[[Michel Godard (musician)|Michel Godard]] performs on a serpent in the Adrabesa Quartet, 2020. Note, Godard is using the vertical hold.]]

Serpents were originally used as an instrument to accompany church choral music, particularly in France. For this purpose, very little was specifically written for the serpent ''per se''; the serpent player would simply play the ''[[cantus firmus]]'', or bass line.{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=65}} The serpent began to be called for in orchestras by opera composers in the mid-to-late 18th century, and their subsequent adoption in military bands prompted the publication of several [[method (music)|method books]], fingering charts and etudes, including duets for student and teacher.{{sfn|Yeo|2019|p=10}}

Among the serpent's first appearances in orchestral scores is [[George Frideric Handel]]'s ''[[Music for the Royal Fireworks]]'' (1749), although he later removed it.<!-- Mozart's "serpentini" in Ascanio in Alba (1771) were curved cor anglais (Bevan 2000, p.69). --> Italian composers in the early 19th century often called for ''serpentone'', in operas by [[Gioachino Rossini]] and [[Vincenzo Bellini]]. Mendelssohn paired serpent with contrabassoon in his overture ''[[Calm Sea and Prosperous Voyage (Mendelssohn)|Calm Sea and Prosperous Voyage]]'' (1828) and [[Symphony No. 5 (Mendelssohn)|fifth "Reformation" symphony]] (1830), and [[Hector Berlioz]] included serpent and ophicleide in his early revisions of ''[[Symphonie fantastique]]'' (1830).

After disappearing almost entirely by the late 19th century, the serpent began to reappear in the mid-20th century in [[film score]]s and new [[List of period instruments|period instrument]] chamber ensembles. American film composer [[Bernard Herrmann]] used a serpent in the scores of ''[[White Witch Doctor (film)|White Witch Doctor]]'' (1953) and ''[[Journey to the Center of the Earth (1959 film)|Journey to the Center of the Earth]]'' (1959), as did [[Jerry Goldsmith]] in his score for ''[[Alien (film)|Alien]]'' (1979).{{sfn|Bevan|2000|p=125}}

French [[tuba|tubist]] and serpent specialist at [[Paris Conservatoire]] [[Michel Godard (musician)|Michel Godard]] often uses serpent in jazz and contemporary music, and has frequently collaborated with [[Lebanon|Lebanese]] composer and [[oud]] player [[Rabih Abou-Khalil]].<ref name="RFI-Godard">{{Cite web |title=Serpentist Michel Godard meets Alim Qasimov: spellbinding |last=Hird |first=Alison |work=World Music Matters |publisher=[[Radio France Internationale]] |publication-place=Paris |date=6 June 2019 |url=https://www.rfi.fr/en/culture/20190606-michel-godard |access-date=3 September 2024 |archive-date=28 December 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221228120910/https://www.rfi.fr/en/culture/20190606-michel-godard |url-status=live }}</ref>
