'''Eating your own dog food''' or "'''dogfooding'''" is the practice of using one's own products or services.<ref>{{cite news |first=Miguel|last= Helft |title=Google Appears Closer to Releasing Its Own Phone |url=http://bits.blogs.nytimes.com/2009/12/12/google-appears-closer-to-releasing-its-own-phone/ |work=[[The New York Times]] |year=2009 |access-date=12 December 2009 }}</ref> This can be a way for an organization to test its products in real-world usage using [[product management]] techniques. Hence dogfooding can act as [[quality control]], and eventually a kind of [[testimonial|testimonial advertising]]. Once in the market, dogfooding can demonstrate developers' confidence in their own products.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://news.zdnet.com/2100-3513_22-5047467.html |title=Microsoft tests its own 'dog food' |work=Tech News on ZDNet |access-date=14 November 2009 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080621080007/http://news.zdnet.com/2100-3513_22-5047467.html |archive-date=21 June 2008 }}</ref><ref name="dvorak">{{cite news|url=https://www.pcmag.com/article2/0,2817,2217007,00.asp |title=The Problem with Eating Your Own Dog Food |last=Dvorak |first=John C. |author-link=John C. Dvorak|date=15 November 2007 |work=PC Magazine |access-date=17 May 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090628161803/http://www.pcmag.com/article2/0,2817,2217007,00.asp |archive-date=28 June 2009 }}</ref>

== Origin of the term ==
In 2006, Warren Harrison, the Editor-in-Chief of ''[[IEEE Software]]'' recounted that in the 1970s television advertisements for [[Alpo (pet food)|Alpo dog food]], spokesperson and actor [[Lorne Greene]] pointed out that he fed Alpo to his own dogs. Another possible origin he remembered was that the president of [[Whiskas|Kal Kan Pet Food]] was said to eat a can of his dog food at annual shareholders' meetings.<ref name= ieee>{{cite journal|last=Harrison|first=Warren|date=May–June 2006|title=From The Editor: Eating Your Own Dog Food|journal=IEEE Software|publisher=IEEE|volume=23|issue=3|pages=5–7|doi=10.1109/MS.2006.72|url= http://www.computer.org/csdl/mags/so/2006/03/s3005.html|url-access=subscription}}</ref><ref>{{Citation | url = https://www.nytimes.com/2022/11/14/business/dogfooding.html | date = 14 November 2022 |title = What Is 'Dogfooding'? | work = The New York Times}}</ref>

In 1988, [[Microsoft]] manager [[Paul Maritz]] sent [[Brian Valentine]], test manager for [[Microsoft LAN Manager]], an email titled "Eating our own Dogfood", challenging him to increase internal usage of the company's product. From there, the usage of the term spread through the company.<ref>{{Cite book |url= https://books.google.com/books?id=Y09tQgAACAAJ |title=Inside Out: Microsoft—in Our Own Words |date=2000 |publisher=Warner Books |isbn= 978-0-446-52739-2}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|url=http://www.arnnet.com.au/article/317315/vmworld_2009_virtualization_controversy_eating_your_own_dog_food/?fp=39&fpid=25874&rid=1|title=VMworld 2009: Virtualization, controversy and eating your own dog food|last=Brodkin|first=John|date=4 September 2009|work=Network World|access-date=17 May 2010|quote=[Paul] Maritz also poked fun at himself by claiming that one of his only contributions to the IT world is coining the commonly used "eat your own dog food" phrase. "You can read about it on Wikipedia, so it must be true," Maritz said.|archive-date= 2012-04-06 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20120406142929/http://www.arnnet.com.au/article/317315/vmworld_2009_virtualization_controversy_eating_your_own_dog_food/?fp=39&fpid=25874&rid=1|url-status=dead}}</ref>

== Real world usage ==
''[[InfoWorld]]'' commented that this needs to be a transparent and honest process: "watered-down examples, such as auto dealers' policy of making salespeople drive the brands they sell, or Coca-Cola allowing no Pepsi products in corporate offices… are irrelevant."<ref name="yager">{{cite news | url= http://www.infoworld.com/d/developer-world/if-its-good-enough-fido-%E2%80%A6-990 | title=If it's good enough for Fido… | work=[[InfoWorld]] | date=30 May 2003 | access-date=15 June 2013 | last=Yager | first=Tom}}</ref> In this sense, a corporate culture of not supporting the competitor is not the same as a philosophy of "eating your own dog food". The latter focuses on the functional aspects of the company's own product.

Dogfooding allows employees to test their company's products in real-life situations; a perceived, but still controversial, advantage beyond marketing,<ref name=dvorak/><ref name= testing/> which gives management a sense of how the product might be used—all before launch to consumers.<ref name= testing>{{cite book|last=Ash|first=Lydia|title=The Web testing companion: the insider's guide to efficient and effective tests|url= https://archive.org/details/webtestingcompan00lydi|url-access=registration |series=ITPro collection|year=2003|publisher= Wiley|isbn=978-0-471-43021-6 |page= [https://archive.org/details/webtestingcompan00lydi/page/17 17]}}</ref> In software development, dogfooding can occur in multiple stages: first, a stable version of the software is used with just a single new feature added. Then, multiple new features can be combined into a single version of the software and tested together. This allows several validations before the software is released. The practice enables proactive resolution of potential inconsistency and dependency issues, especially when several developers or teams work on the same product.{{citation needed|date=May 2022}}

The risks of public dogfooding, specifically that a company may have difficulties using its own products, may reduce the frequency of publicized dogfooding.{{r|yager}}

== Examples ==
In February 1980, [[Apple Computer]] president [[Michael Scott (Apple)|Michael Scott]] wrote a [[Memorandum|memo]] announcing "Effective Immediately!! No more typewriters are to be purchased, leased etc., etc. […] We believe the typewriter is obsolete. Let's prove it inside before we try and convince our customers."<ref>{{cite web|title=Apple Memo – No Typewriters |url=https://archive.org/details/Apple_Memo_No_Typewriters | website=archive.org|access-date=2 January 2015}}</ref> He set a goal to remove all typewriters from the company by 1 January 1981.<ref>{{cite news | url=http://www.inc.com/magazine/19811001/2033.html | title=An Apple On Every Desk | access-date=March 6, 2011 | author=Ditlea, Steve | date=1981-10-01 | publisher=Inc.}}</ref>

By 1987, [[Atari Corp.]] was in the process of using the [[Atari ST]] throughout the company.<ref name="friedland198703">{{cite news | url=https://archive.org/stream/1987-03-anticmagazine/Antic_Vol_5-11_1987-03_Inside_Atari_Corp#page/n29/mode/2up | title=Today's Atari Corp. {{!}} A close-up look inside | work=Antic | date=March 1987 | access-date=4 July 2014 | pages=30 | author=Friedland, Nat}}</ref>

The development of [[Windows NT]] at Microsoft involved over 200 developers in small teams, and it was held together by [[Dave Cutler]]'s February 1991 insistence on dogfooding. Microsoft developed the operating system on computers running NT daily [[software build|builds]]. The software was initially crash prone, but the immediate feedback of code breaking the build, the loss of pride, and the knowledge of impeding the work of others were all powerful motivators.<ref>{{cite book|last1=Bolman|first1=Lee G.|last2=Deal|first2=Terrence E.|title=Reframing organizations: artistry, choice, and leadership|url=https://archive.org/details/reframingorganiz00bolm_1|url-access=registration|edition=3|series=Jossey-Bass business & management series; Jossey-Bass higher and adult education series|year=2003|publisher=[[John Wiley and Sons]]|isbn=978-0-7879-6426-9|page=[https://archive.org/details/reframingorganiz00bolm_1/page/64 64]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Zachary |first=G. Pascal |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=k8ISAQAAMAAJ |title=Show-stopper!: The Breakneck Race to Create Windows NT and the Next Generation at Microsoft |date=1994 |publisher=Free Press |isbn=978-0-02-935671-5 |page=135 |language=en}}</ref> Windows developers would typically dogfood or [[Self-hosting (compilers)|self-host]] Windows starting from the early (alpha) builds, while the rest of the employees would start from the more stable beta builds that were also available to [[MSDN]] subscribers.{{Citation needed|reason=What timeframe? When did MSDN subscriptions start and what versions of the OS are we talking about (Win 3.1, WinNT, did they ever work with DOS?) |date=June 2018}} In 2005, ''InfoWorld'' reported that a tour of Microsoft's [[network operations center]] "showed pretty much beyond a reasonable doubt that Microsoft does run its 20,000-plus node, international network on 99 percent Windows technology, including servers, workstations, and edge security".<ref>{{cite news|url=http://www.infoworld.com/t/platforms/microsoft-machine-churns-911|title=The Microsoft machine churns on|last=Rist|first=Oliver|date=29 December 2005|work=[[InfoWorld]]|access-date=17 May 2010}}</ref> ''InfoWorld'' argued that "Microsoft's use of Windows for its high-traffic operations tipped many doubters over to Windows' side of the fence."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.infoworld.com/article/2681851/if-it-s-good-enough-for-fido--.html|title=If it's good enough for Fido... |first=Tom |last=Yager |work=InfoWorld |date=31 May 2003 |access-date=2 August 2022}}</ref>

{{quote box|align=right | width=15% | quote="Microsoft's use of Windows and [[.NET Framework|.NET]] would be irrelevant except for one thing: Its software project leads and on-line services managers do have the freedom to choose."|source=Tom Yager, ''InfoWorld''<ref name=yager/>}}

Microsoft's internal email system initially ran on [[Xenix]].<ref name="flynn19870223">{{Cite magazine |last=Flynn |first=Laurie |date=1987-02-23 |title=Companywide Network A Necessity at Microsoft |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=1DAEAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA17 |access-date=2025-05-21 |magazine=InfoWorld |page=17 |volume=9 |issue=8}}</ref> The only employees using [[Microsoft Mail]] worked on the software itself, and used it as a client for Xenix mail servers.{{r|xenix1}} When asked why Microsoft used [[Unix]], it publicly moved to using [[Microsoft Exchange Server|Microsoft Exchange]].<ref>{{cite news|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=oD0EAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA101|title=Microsoft forgoes eating dog food in favor of Unix as Bobby sneaks a burger|last=Cringely|first=Robert X.|date=14 August 2000|work=[[InfoWorld]]|access-date=17 May 2010}}</ref> During the 1993-1996 [[Software modernization|migration]], the internal test environment was codenamed "Dogfood".<ref name=xenix1>{{cite web|title=Microsoft's Migration to Microsoft Exchange Server|url=https://www.microsoft.com/technet/archive/itsolutions/intranet/build/exchgdep.mspx|work=[[Microsoft TechNet|TechNet]]|publisher=[[Microsoft]]|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20050507010335/http://www.microsoft.com/technet/archive/itsolutions/intranet/build/exchgdep.mspx|archivedate=May 7, 2005}}</ref> In 1997, an [[email storm]] known as the Bedlam DL3<ref name="Me Too">{{cite web | title=Me Too! | author-first=Larry | author-last=Osterman | work=Microsoft Exchange Team weblog | date=8 April 2004 | url=http://msexchangeteam.com/archive/2004/04/08/109626.aspx | publisher=Microsoft | access-date=21 February 2012 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101218161351/http://msexchangeteam.com/archive/2004/04/08/109626.aspx | archive-date=18 December 2010 | url-status=dead }}</ref> incident made Microsoft build more robust features into Microsoft Exchange Server to avoid lost and duplicate emails and network and server down-time. A second email storm in 2006<ref name="Bedlam all over again">"It's Bedlam all over again..." Larry Osterman's WebLog. 18 September 2006 in [http://blogs.msdn.com/b/larryosterman/archive/2006/09/18/760981.aspx blogs.msdn.com]</ref> was handled perfectly by the system.

In 1999, [[Hewlett-Packard]] staff referred to a project using HP's own products as "Project [[Alpo (dog food)|Alpo]]" (referring to a brand of dog food).<ref>{{cite news|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=8ggAAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA120|title=Unleash innovation|last=Field|first=Tom|date=15 August 1999|work=CIO|access-date=17 May 2010}}</ref> Around the same time, [[Mozilla]] also practised dogfooding under that exact name.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=15927|title=15927 – [Beta][Dogfood]No proxy authentication|website=bugzilla.mozilla.org|language=en|access-date=8 February 2017}}</ref>

Government [[Procurement#Green public procurement|green public procurement]] that allows testing of proposed environmental policies has been compared to dogfooding.<ref>{{cite book|last=Johnstone|first=Nick|title=The environmental performance of public procurement: issues of policy coherence|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=lUcup_W_HI0C&pg=PA37|year=2003|publisher=Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development|isbn=978-92-64-10155-5}}</ref>

On 1 June 2011, [[YouTube]] added a license feature to its video uploading service allowing users to choose between a standard or [[Creative Commons]] license.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://youtube-global.blogspot.com/2011/06/youtube-and-creative-commons-raising.html|title=YouTube and Creative Commons: raising the bar on user creativity|last=Peterson|first=Stace|date=2 June 2011|work=The Official YouTube Blog}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|url=http://gagagadget.com/wordpress/2011/06/shh-internal-dogfood-youtube-introduces-creative-commons-attribution-license/|title=(Shh! – Internal Dogfood) – YouTube introduces Creative Commons Attribution license|last=LaPine|first=John|date=2 June 2011|work=GaGaGadget.com|access-date=2 June 2011}}</ref> The license label was followed by the message "(Shh! – Internal Dogfood)" that appeared on all YouTube videos lacking commercial licensing.<ref>{{cite web|title=YouTube – Copyright Education – Creative Commons|website=[[YouTube]] |url=https://www.youtube.com/t/creative_commons|access-date=1 June 2011}}</ref>{{failed verification|reason=Does not contain the word "dogfood"|date=August 2022}} A YouTube employee confirmed that this referred to products that are tested internally.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.google.com/support/forum/p/youtube/thread?tid=50a8d56a6ccbb3c1|title=YouTube License: (Shh! – Internal Dogfood)|date=June 2, 2011|website=Youtube Help Forum|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225084034/https://support.google.com/youtube/forum/AAAAiuErobUlDgBLgFaqdA/?hl=en&gpf=d/category-topic/youtube/watching-videos/lDgBLgFaqdA|archive-date=February 25, 2021|url-status=dead|access-date=September 8, 2022}}</ref>

[[Oracle Corporation]] stated that {{as of|2016|10|lc=y}} it "runs [[Oracle Linux]] with more than 42,000 servers [to] support more than 4 million external users and 84,000 internal users. More than 20,000 developers at Oracle use Oracle Linux".<ref name="oracle201610">{{Cite web |date=October 2016 |title=Frequently Asked Questions – Oracle Linux |url=http://www.oracle.com/us/technologies/027617.pdf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161213065920/http://www.oracle.com/us/technologies/027617.pdf |archive-date=2016-12-13 |publisher=Oracle Corporation}}</ref>

After the [[2024 CrowdStrike-related IT outages|CrowdStrike outages]] in July 2024, CEO Adam Meyers testified before the US Congress that "dogfooding" (increasing its internal testing before deployment) was one measure the company had put in place to prevent future problems.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Kleinman |first=Zoe |date=September 24, 2024 |title='Deeply sorry,' CrowdStrike boss apologises for global IT outage |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/live/ckgnxd7d26gt |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241002184012/https://www.bbc.com/news/live/ckgnxd7d26gt |archive-date=October 2, 2024 |access-date=October 2, 2024 |website=BBC News}}</ref>

== Criticisms and support ==
Forcing those who design products to actually use and rely on them is sometimes thought to improve quality and [[usability]], but software developers may be blind to usability and may have knowledge to make software work that an end user will lack.<ref name=testing/> Microsoft's [[chief information officer]] noted in 2008 that, previously, "We tended not to go through the actual customer experience. We were always upgrading from a beta, not from production disk to production disk."<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.forbes.com/2008/12/12/cio-microsoft-scott-tech-cio-cx_es_1215microsoft.html|title=Eating Their Own Dog Food|last=Sperling|first=Ed|date=15 December 2008|work=Forbes|access-date=17 May 2010}}</ref> Dogfooding may happen too early to be viable, and those forced to use the products may get used to applying workarounds or may assume that someone else has reported the problem. Dogfooding may be unrealistic, as customers will always have a choice of different companies' products to use together, and the product may not be used as intended. The process can lead to a loss of productivity and demoralisation,<ref name=testing/> or at its extreme to "[[Not Invented Here]]" syndrome, i.e. only using internal products.<ref name=ieee/>

In 1989, [[Donald Knuth]] published a paper recounting lessons from the development of his [[TeX]] Typesetting software, in which the benefits of the approach were mentioned:{{Blockquote|text=Thus, I came to the conclusion that the designer of a new system must not only be the implementor and the first large-scale user; the designer should also write the first user manual. The separation of any of these four components would have hurt TeX significantly. If I had not participated fully in all these activities, literally hundreds of improvements would never have been made, because I would never have thought of them or perceived why they were important.|sign=[[Donald E. Knuth]]|source="The Errors Of TeX"<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Knuth |first1=Donald E. |title=The errors of t<sub>e</sub>x |journal=Software: Practice and Experience |date=1989 |volume=19 |issue=7 |pages=607–685 |doi=10.1002/spe.4380190702|s2cid=20608669 }}</ref>}}

== Alternative terms ==
In 2007, Jo Hoppe, the CIO of [[Pegasystems]], said that she uses the alternative phrase "drinking our own champagne".<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.cio.com/article/122351/Pegasystems_CIO_Tells_Colleagues_Drink_Your_Own_Champagne|title=Pegasystems CIO Tells Colleagues: Drink Your Own Champagne|work=Trendline on CIO.com|date=5 July 2007|access-date=5 July 2007|archive-date=2007-10-11 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071011064359/http://cio.com/article/122351/Pegasystems_CIO_Tells_Colleagues_Drink_Your_Own_Champagne|url-status=dead}}</ref> [[Novell]]'s head of [[public relations]] Bruce Lowry, commenting on his company's use of [[Linux]] and [[OpenOffice.org]], said that he also prefers this phrase.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.desktoplinux.com/news/NS8190577585.html|title=Novell comments on its transition to Linux desktops|date=13 April 2006|work=DesktopLinux|publisher=Ziff Davis Enterprise|access-date=17 May 2010|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110616183048/http://www.desktoplinux.com/news/NS8190577585.html|archive-date=16 June 2011}}</ref> In 2009, the new CIO of Microsoft, Tony Scott, argued that the phrase "dogfooding" was unappealing and should be replaced by "icecreaming", with the aim of developing products as "ice cream that our customers want to consume".<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.techflash.com/seattle/2009/11/turning_dog_food_into_ice_cream_and_other_tidbits_from_microsofts_cio.html|title=Microsoft CIO on a mission to make ice cream out of dog food |publisher=TechFlash |date=10 November 2009 |access-date=2 May 2010}}</ref> A less controversial and common alternative term used in some contexts is [[Self-hosting (compilers)|self-hosting]], where developers' workstations would, for instance, get updated automatically overnight to the latest daily build of the software or operating system on which they work. Developers of IBM's [[mainframe]] operating systems have long used the term "eating our own cooking".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.vm.ibm.com/vm40hist.pdf|title=z/VM – A Brief Review of Its 40 Year History  |publisher=IBM |date=2012}}</ref>
