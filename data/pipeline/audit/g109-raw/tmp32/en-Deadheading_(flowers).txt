{{Short description|Removing spent flowers from ornamental plants}}
'''Deadheading''' is the [[horticultural]] practice of removing spent [[flowers]] from [[ornamental plants]]. 

Deadheading is a widespread form of [[pruning]],<ref name="Bob">{{Cite book|first=Bob |last=Flowerdew|author-link=Bob Flowerdew|title=Pruning, Training, and Tidying: Bob's Basics|publisher=	Skyhorse Publishing, Inc|year=2012|isbn=978-1620872666}}</ref> since fading flowers are not as appealing and direct a lot of energy into [[seed development]] if pollinated.<ref name="rhs"/> The goal of deadheading is thus to preserve the attractiveness of the plants in [[bedding (horticulture)|beds]], [[herbaceous border|borders]], [[container garden|containers]] and [[hanging baskets]], as well as to encourage further blooming. Deadheading flowers with many petals, such as [[rose]]s, [[peonies]], and [[camellia]]s prevents them from littering. Deadheading can be done with fingers and thumb or with [[pruning shears]], knife, or scissors.<ref name="rhs">{{cite web|title=Deadheading plants|url=https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=129|publisher=Royal Horticultural Society|access-date=8 April 2018}}</ref>

Ornamental plants that do not require deadheading are those that do not produce a lot of seed or tend to deadhead themselves. These include [[lobelia]]s, [[salvia]]s, and [[fuchsia]]s. Deadheading is undesirable if the plant's seed is [[wildlife garden|enjoyed by birds]], as is the case with many species from the family [[Asteraceae]]. Likewise, if the plant bears attractive seeds or fruits, deadheading is normally avoided.<ref name="rhs"/>
