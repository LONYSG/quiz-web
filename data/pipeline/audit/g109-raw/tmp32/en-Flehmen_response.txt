The '''flehmen response''' ({{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|l|eɪ|m|ən}}; from [[German language|German]] {{lang|de|flehmen}} {{gloss|to bare the upper teeth}}, and [[Upper Saxon German]] {{lang|sxu|flemmen}} {{gloss|to look [[Spite (sentiment)|spiteful]]}}), also called the '''flehmen position''', '''flehmen reaction''', '''flehmen grimace''', '''flehming''', or '''flehmening''', is a [[behavior]] in which an animal curls back its upper lip exposing its front teeth, inhales with the nostrils usually closed, and then often holds this position for several seconds.<ref name=":1" /> The behavior occurs as a response to relevant external stimuli, typically [[Pheromone|pheromones]], which may have been produced by animals of the same or different species.<ref>{{Cite journal|title=9.3 Taste and Smell|url=https://opentext.uoregon.edu/animalphysiology/chapter/9-3-taste-and-smell/|date=2025-01-03|language=en|first=Avinash|last=Singh|journal=University of Oregon Libraries|editor-first=Avinash|editor-last=Singh}}</ref> It may be performed with the neck stretched and the head held high in the air.

Flehmen is performed by a wide range of [[mammals]], including [[ungulate]]s and [[felid]]s.<ref name=":1" /> The behavior facilitates the transfer of pheromones and other scents into the [[vomeronasal organ]] (VNO, or Jacobson's organ) located above the roof of the mouth via a duct which exits just behind the front teeth of the animal.

==Etymology==
The word is loaned from the [[German language|German]] verb {{lang|de|flehmen}} {{gloss|to bare the upper teeth}}, which itself is derived from the [[Upper Saxon German]] {{lang|sxu|flemmen}} {{gloss|to look [[Spite (sentiment)|spiteful]]}}.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://dictionary.reference.com/browse/flehmen |title=flehmen |publisher=Dictionary.com |access-date=May 28, 2013|url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131203012115/http://dictionary.reference.com/browse/flehmen |archive-date=2013-12-03 }}</ref> The word was introduced in 1930 by [[Karl Max Schneider]], director of the Leipzig zoo and an authority on big cats in captivity.<ref name=":0">{{cite book |doi=10.1007/978-1-4757-9652-0_5 |chapter=Flehmen Behavior and Vomeronasal Organ Function |title=Chemical Signals in Vertebrates 3 |page=87 |year=1983 |last1=Hart |first1=Benjamin L |editor-first=Dietland |editor-last=Müller-Schwarze |editor-first2=Robert M. |editor-last2=Silverstein |isbn=978-1-4757-9654-4 |publisher=Springer}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.1016/S0003-3472(85)80005-1|title=The ontogeny of flehmen in horses|journal=Animal Behaviour|volume=33|issue=3|pages=739|year=1985|last1=Crowell-Davis|first1=Sharon|last2=Houpt|first2=Katherine A|s2cid=53160386}}</ref>

== Description ==

This response is characterized by the animal curling back its top lip exposing the front teeth and gums, then inhaling and holding the posture for several seconds.<ref name=":0" /> The behavior may be performed over particular locations, in which case the animal may also lick the site of interest, or may perform the flehmen with the neck stretched and head held high in the air for a more general [[Gustation|gustatory]] or taste-related investigation. The flehmen response can give the appearance that the animal is looking spiteful, grimacing, smirking, disgusted, or laughing. The relationship between emotions and expressions across species is [[facial expression#Evolutionary significance of universality|debated]].

==Mechanism==
The flehmen response draws air into the vomeronasal organ (VNO), an auxiliary [[olfactory]] [[sense organ]] that is found in many animals.  This organ plays a role in the perception of certain scents and pheromones. The vomeronasal organ is named for its closeness to the [[vomer]] and [[nasal bone]]s, and is particularly well developed in animals such as cats and horses. The VNO is found encompassed inside a bony or cartilaginous capsule which opens into the base of the [[nasal cavity]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.neuro.fsu.edu/~mmered/vomer/|title=The Vomeronasal Organ|work=fsu.edu|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130211023317/http://www.neuro.fsu.edu/~mmered/vomer/|archive-date=2013-02-11}}</ref> Animals that exhibit flehmen have [[incisive papilla|a papilla located behind the incisors]] and ducts which connect the oral cavity to the VNO, with horses being an exception. Horses exhibit flehmen but do not have an incisive duct communication between the nasal and oral cavity because they do not breathe through their mouths; instead, the VNOs connect to the nasal passages by the [[Incisive foramen|nasopalatine duct]].<ref name="Briggs smell">{{cite web|last=Briggs|first=Karen|title=Equine Sense of Smell|url=http://www.thehorse.com/articles/10055/equine-sense-of-smell|publisher=The Horse|access-date=December 15, 2013|date=December 11, 2013}}</ref>

===Chemical cues===
The chemical cue obtained by an animal exhibiting the flehmen response is the presence of a non-volatile [[organic compound]]. In contrast to [[volatile organic compounds]] (VOCs), non-volatile organic compounds are those carbon compounds that do not participate in atmospheric photochemical reactions or evaporate under normal atmospheric conditions.<ref>Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs): Technical Overview. United States Environmental Protection Agency. {{Deprecated archive|url=https://archive.today/20120729053634/http://www.epa.gov/iaq/voc2.html#2}}. Updated April 10, 2012. Accessed May 9, 2012.</ref> The VNO detects non-VOCs, which must have direct contact with the odor source. Sources of non-VOCs relevant to the flehmen response include pheromones and hormones excreted from the genital regions or urine of animals.

==Function==
An animal may perform the flehmen response when investigating sites of particular interest, or perhaps (more generally) odors or flavours. In horses, spontaneous flehmen response outside of the normal context can also indicate discomfort.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Torcivia |first1=Catherine |last2=McDonnell |first2=Sue |date=2021 |title=Equine Discomfort Ethogram |journal=Animals |language=en |volume=11 |issue=2 |pages=580 |doi=10.3390/ani11020580 |doi-access=free |issn=2076-2615 |pmc=7931104 |pmid=33672338}}</ref>
=== Intra-species communication ===
The primary function of the flehmen response is intra-species communication.  By transferring air containing pheromones and other scents to the VNO, animals can gather chemical "messages".<ref name=":2">{{Cite journal|last1=Hart|first1=Benjamin L.|last2=Hart|first2=Lynette A.|last3=Maina|first3=J. N.|date=1988-01-01|title=Alteration in vomeronasal system anatomy in alcelaphine antelopes: Correlation with alteration in chemosensory investigation|journal=Physiology & Behavior|volume=42|issue=2|pages=155–162|doi=10.1016/0031-9384(88)90291-0|pmid=3368534|s2cid=26589910|issn=0031-9384}}</ref> These scents tell an animal about other members of their species in some of the following ways:
* Identifying reproductive status – male individuals commonly use the flehmen response as an olfactory mechanism for identifying the reproductive state of females of the same species based on [[pheromones]] in the female's urine or genitals.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Doty |first=Richard |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=PWwCnplyxYYC&q=flehmen |title=Mammalian Olfaction, Reproductive Processes, and Behavior |date=2012-12-02 |publisher=Elsevier |isbn=978-0-323-15450-5 |language=en}}</ref> This is exhibited in the [[Sheep reproduction#Sexual behavior|reproductive behavior of sheep]]. The ram often exhibits flehmen after sniffing ewes' external genital regions, but this occurs most frequently on the day before [[estrus]] when the ewes are sexually receptive.<ref name=":3">{{Cite journal |last1=Bland |first1=K. P. |last2=Jubilan |first2=B. M. |date=1987-06-01 |title=Correlation of flehmen by male sheep with female behaviour and oestrus |url=https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003347287801094 |journal=Animal Behaviour |volume=35 |issue=3 |pages=735–738 |doi=10.1016/S0003-3472(87)80109-4 |issn=0003-3472|url-access=subscription }}</ref>
* Reproductive synchrony – flehmen behavior also plays a role in [[reproductive synchrony]] between females. In the [[sable antelope]], the frequency of flehmen changes seasonally, with the highest levels just prior to conception. Female antelopes associate closely with other females in the same reproductive state. Flehmen rates between females anticipated birth synchrony. Additionally, the level of synchrony was predicted by the frequency of female urine sampling during the previous year. Flehmen is a mechanism used by female sable antelopes to manipulate the timing of both conception and birth of offspring.<ref>Thompson, K. V. "[https://repository.si.edu/bitstream/handle/10088/8063/4e704f54-cf10-49ba-b245-24a911dabc95.pdf Flehmen and birth synchrony among female sable antelope, Hippotragus-niger]". 1995. ''Animal Behavior'' 50: 475–84.</ref> In the American bison, flehmen behavior in females has also been shown to stimulate the onset of [[estrus]] and [[Synchronization of menstrual cycles|copulation synchronization]].<ref>Wolff, J. O. [https://www.jstor.org/stable/3546680 "Breeding strategies, mate choice, and reproductive success in American bison"]. 1998. ''Oikos'' 83(3): 529–44.</ref>
* Post-parturition – in horses, mares commonly show a peak in flehmen response during the first few hours after giving birth. Smelling the newborn foal and the amniotic fluids associated with birth often produces the reaction.<ref name="Kentucky Equine Research">{{cite web|url=http://www.ker.com/library/EquineReview/2008/HorseWorld/HW41.pdf|title=Flehmen: 'What's My Horse Doing with His Lip?'|publisher=Kentucky Equine Research, Inc.|access-date=May 29, 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121016051111/http://ker.com/library/EquineReview/2008/HorseWorld/HW41.pdf|archive-date=October 16, 2012|url-status=dead}}</ref>
* Immature animals – in young horses, both colts (males) and fillies (females) exhibit flehmen behavior toward other [[Biological specificity#Conspecific|conspecifics]] with neither sex performing the behavior more than the other.<ref name=":1">Weeks, J. W.; Cromwell-Davis, S. L.; Heusner, G. "[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159102001107 Preliminary study of the development of the Flehmen response in ''Equus caballus'']. 2002. ''Applied Animal Behavior Science'' 78(2): 329–35.</ref> However, it has been reported that young colts flehmen up to five times more frequently than fillies, and fillies flehmen more frequently than mature mares.<ref name="Kentucky Equine Research"/>{{Contradictory inline|date=December 2023}} Young elephants also have a flehmen response to stimulants. The VNO of newborn elephants displays a structural maturity similar to adults, which supports the conclusion that flehmen at only six weeks of age is used to deliver chemical pheromones to a functional VNO.<ref>{{Cite journal | doi=10.1002/ar.10112|pmid = 12115276|title = Morphological characteristics of the vomeronasal organ of the newborn Asian elephant (Elephas maximus)| journal=The Anatomical Record| volume=267| issue=3| pages=252–259|year = 2002|last1 = Johnson|first1 = Edward W.| last2=Rasmussen| first2=Lel|s2cid = 30345793| doi-access=free}}</ref>

===Inter-species communication===
The flehmen response is not limited to intra-species communication. Goats have been tested for their flehmen response to urine from 20 different species, including several non-mammalian species. This study suggests there is a common element in the urine of all animals, an [[interspecific]] pheromone, which elicits flehmen behavior. Specifically, chemical pheromone levels of a modified form of [[androgen]], a [[sex hormone]], were associated with the response in goats.<ref>Sasada, H.; Kanomata, K.; Fukuoka, T. "[https://www.cabdirect.org/cabdirect/abstract/19910186523 Flehmen induction with goats by the urine of twenty animal species]". 1988. 11th International Congress on Animal Reproduction and Artificial Insemination, University College Dublin, Irelend, June 26–30, 1988. Volume 4. Brief Communications.</ref>

==Mammals exhibiting==
A wide range of mammals exhibit flehmen including both predatory and non-predatory species.

The response is perhaps most easily observed in domestic cats and horses; both exhibit a strong flehmen response to odors.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.catsguru.com/flehmening-in-cats |title=Flehmening in cats |publisher=Catsguru.com |date=April 9, 2011 |access-date=May 23, 2012}}</ref> [[Stallion (horse)|Stallions]] usually smell the urine of mares in estrus whereas the male giraffe's flehmen response includes [[urophagia|tasting the female's urine]].<ref name="bio.davidson.edu">{{cite web |url=http://www.bio.davidson.edu/people/vecase/Behavior/Spring2004/breedlove/matingsystem.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110817214008/http://www.bio.davidson.edu/people/vecase/Behavior/Spring2004/breedlove/matingsystem.html |archive-date=August 17, 2011 |title=Introduction to the social system of giraffe (''Giraffa camelopardalis'') |date=August 17, 2011 |access-date=January 17, 2013}}</ref> [[Elephant]]s perform a flehmen response but also transfer chemosensory stimuli to the vomeronasal opening in the roof of their mouths using the [[prehensile]] structure, sometimes called a "finger", at the tips of their trunks.{{citation needed|date=February 2020}}
