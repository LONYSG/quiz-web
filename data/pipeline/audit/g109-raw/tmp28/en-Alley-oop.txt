In [[basketball]], an '''alley-oop''', also known as a lob, is an offensive play in which one player passes the ball near the basket to a teammate who [[Jumping|jump]]s, catches the ball in mid-air and [[slam dunk|dunks]] or [[layup|lays it in]] before touching the ground.

The alley-oop combines elements of [[teamwork]], pinpoint passing, timing and finishing.

==Etymology==
The term "alley-oop" is derived from the French term {{lang|fr|allez hop!}}, the cry of a [[circus]] [[acrobatics|acrobat]] about to leap.<ref>[http://www.oed.com/viewdictionaryentry/Entry/263414 alley-oop, adv., int., adj., and n], [[Oxford English Dictionary]]</ref>

The term “[[Alley Oop]]” was first popularized in the United States in 1932 as the name of a syndicated comic strip created by cartoonist [[V. T. Hamlin]].

In [[Sport|sports]], the term "[[Alley-oop (American football)|alley-oop]]" first appeared in the 1950s by the San Francisco 49ers of the NFL to describe a high arcing pass from quarterback [[Y. A. Tittle]] to wide receiver [[R.C. Owens]], who would outleap smaller cornerbacks for touchdown receptions.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Martin |first=Douglas |date=2012-06-20 |title=R. C. Owens, Receiver Who Made the Alley-Oop Famous, Dies at 77 |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2012/06/20/sports/football/r-c-owens-wide-receiver-behind-the-alley-oop-dies-at-77.html |access-date=2025-01-05 |work=The New York Times |language=en-US |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> "[[The Catch (American football)|The Catch]]", the [[Dwight Clark]] touchdown reception from [[Joe Montana]] by which the [[San Francisco 49ers|49ers]] gained entry into their first [[Super Bowl]], was also an "alley-oop" pass. The term later became better known from its use in basketball.

==History==
In the 1950s, some players began grabbing balls in mid-air and then [[Slam dunk|dunking]].  [[K. C. Jones]] and [[Bill Russell]] teamed up to perform the alley-oop several times while at the [[San Francisco Dons men's basketball|University of San Francisco]] in the mid-1950s.<ref name=johnson>{{Cite book| last = Johnson | first = James W. | title = The Dandy Dons: Bill Russell, K. C. Jones, Phil Woolpert, and One of College Basketball's Greatest and Most Innovative Teams| publisher = Bison Books | year = 2009 | page = 85 | isbn = 9780803224445}}</ref><ref name=Paul>{{cite web | last = Paul | first = Alan | title=An Interview With Bill Russell|url=http://alanpaul.net/2018/04/an-interview-with-bill-russell/| year=2018}}</ref>

In addition, [[Bill Russell]], [[Wilt Chamberlain]] at the [[University of Kansas]] and 'Jumping' [[Johnny Green (basketball)|Johnny Green]] at [[Michigan State University]] would frequently grab errant shots by teammates and dunk them. This resulted in a tightening in the enforcement of [[Basket interference|offensive goaltending]] rules in [[NCAA]] and [[NBA]] basketball in the late 1950s.<ref name=johnson/>

[[Phillips 66ers|The Phillips 66ers]], an Oklahoma [[Amateur Athletic Union|AAU]] team,<ref name=":5">{{Cite news |date=April 2, 1962 |title=Phillips 66ers Capture Crown In AAU Tournament |work=[[The Odessa American]] |pages=Thirteen |publication-place=[[Odessa, Texas]] |agency=United Press International}}</ref> had an alley-oop play in its playbook where Charlie Bowerman would pass the ball to [[Don Kojis]].<ref name="lacis"/> Before his 12-year NBA career,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Don Kojis Stats, Height, Weight, Position, Draft Status and more |url=https://www.basketball-reference.com/players/k/kojisdo01.html |access-date=2026-08-12 |website=Basketball-Reference.com |language=en}}</ref> Kojis played two seasons for the 66ers from 1961 to 1963, making that the time period when the play was executed.<ref name="nasljerseys"/> At the time, the play between Bowerman and Kojis was known as the "Kangaroo Kram" and became a regular play they used in games.<ref name=":0">{{Cite news |date=December 29, 1961 |title=66ers Gig Kirk's 113–58 |work=Bartlesville Examiner-Enterprise |pages=9 |publication-place=Bartlesville, Oklahoma}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |date=January 28, 1962 |title=66ers Pop Regis Hoopsters 78–64 |work=[[Bartlesville Examiner-Enterprise]] |pages=8 |publication-place=[[Bartlesville, Oklahoma]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |last=Durham |first=George |date=February 22, 1962 |title=66ers Invade Akron Lair Again Tonight |work=Bartlesville Examiner-Enterprise |pages=7 |publication-place=[[Bartlesville, Oklahoma]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news |last=Durham |first=George |date=February 7, 1963 |title=Fort Sill Axed By 66ers 117–73 |work=News Review |pages=12 |publication-place=Bartlesville, Oklahoma}}</ref> The Kangaroo Kram, with Bowerman passing and Kojis catching and [[Slam dunk|slam dunking]], was described in the local Oklahoma press in late December 1961: "It's like trying to describe a spiral staircase without using your hands, but the pass is lobbed to the side of the basket, and with perfect timing Kojis dives in, grabs the ball with both hands and stuffs its down the net . . . at full speed".<ref name=":0" />

[[Al Tucker]] and his brother Gerald at [[Oklahoma Baptist University]] are sometimes mistakenly credited with being the first to use the alley-oop in the mid-60s.<ref name="andrieson"/> In [[Bill Walton]]'s record-setting 44-point, 21-for-22 shooting performance for [[UCLA]] in the 1973 NCAA championship game against [[Memphis State]], six of his baskets came on alley-oop plays.

Some others credit [[David Thompson (basketball)|David Thompson]] as the first player to execute the classic alley-oop play while at [[NC State Wolfpack men's basketball|North Carolina State University]], with his teammates [[Monte Towe]] and [[Tim Stoddard]] performing the necessary lob passes. NCSU's Thompson popularized the play during the early 1970s, exploiting his 44-inch vertical leap to make the above-the-rim play a recurring staple in the Wolfpack's offensive attack.<ref name="posnanski"/>  Because dunking was illegal in college basketball at that time, upon catching the pass, Thompson would simply drop the ball through the hoop – never dunking one until the final play of the final home game of his career.

After a decade of dunking prohibition ended in the NCAA in 1976, the alley-oop became associated in the late 1970s with [[1978–79 Michigan State Spartans men's basketball team|Michigan State's]] [[Magic Johnson|Earvin 'Magic' Johnson]] and [[Greg Kelser]]. The duo connected for many highlight alley-oops and would showcase the play in their [[1979 NCAA Division I basketball tournament|1979 national championship]] run, including the most watched game in the history of the sport, the famed Magic vs. [[Larry Bird|Bird]] [[1979 NCAA Division I Basketball Championship Game|championship game]].<ref>{{Citation|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=qjQTsmTJbX0C&q=alley+oop&pg=PA130 |title=When March Went Mad: The Game That Transformed Basketball |first= Seth|last= Davis|isbn=978-0805088106|year=2009|publisher=Times Books}}</ref> Three years later, unheralded [[Idaho Vandals men's basketball|Idaho]] made the alley-oop an integral part of their undersized offense in [[Don Monson#1981.E2.80.9382 season|1982]],<ref name=loolit>{{cite news|url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=mbBeAAAAIBAJ&pg=5186%2C2965068 |work=Lewiston Morning Tribune |location=Idaho|last=Killen |first=John |title=Lob it or leave it |date=January 23, 1982 |page=1B }}</ref> ended the regular season eighth in [[1981–82 NCAA Division I men's basketball rankings|both major polls]] {{nowrap|at 26–2,<ref name=scvnosx>{{cite news |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=7-pWAAAAIBAJ&pg=7290%2C276586 |work=Spokane Chronicle |location=Washington |agency=wire services |title=Vandals No. 6 |date=March 2, 1982 |page=13}}</ref><ref name=fltoeit>{{cite news |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=9epWAAAAIBAJ&pg=5323%2C2242029 |work=Spokane Chronicle |location=Washington |agency=wire services |title=UI wins but falls to No. 8 |date=March 9, 1982 |page=17}}</ref>}} and advanced to the [[1982 NCAA Division I men's basketball tournament#West region|Sweet Sixteen]].<ref name=sevset>{{cite news |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=-upWAAAAIBAJ&pg=6623%2C3844087|work=Spokane Chronicle |location=Washington |last=Van Sickel |first=Charlie |title=Vandals: Sweet 16 and... |date=March 15, 1982 |page=15}}</ref>

The following year, [[1982–83 NC State Wolfpack men's basketball team|North Carolina State]] also won the national championship on what could be considered the most famous alley-oop of all time against heavily favored [[1982–83 Houston Cougars men's basketball team|Houston]] in the [[1983 NCAA Division I men's basketball tournament|1983]] [[1983 NCAA Division I Men's Basketball Championship Game|championship game]] at [[The Pit (arena)|The Pit]] in [[Albuquerque, New Mexico]]. With time running out and the score tied, guard [[Dereck Whittenburg]] shot short of the rim, which effectively functioned as a pass to [[Lorenzo Charles]], who caught the ball and stuffed it through the net to win the title in a {{nowrap|huge upset.<ref name=sillckp>{{cite news |url=https://www.si.com/vault/1983/04/11/619339/state-had-the-stuff |magazine=Sports Illustrated |last=Kirkpatrick |first=Curry |title=State had the stuff|date=April 11, 1983 |page=18}}</ref><ref name=wpmrlnd>{{cite news |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=vcksAAAAIBAJ&pg=6570%2C1499512 |work=Wilmington Morning Star |location=North Carolina|agency=Associated Press |title=Wolfpack miracle rules land |date=April 5, 1983 |page=1D}}</ref><ref name=wpscft>{{cite news|url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=EapRAAAAIBAJ&pg=6614%2C787183|title=Wolfpack stuffs Cougars for title|newspaper=Milwaukee Sentinel|date=April 5, 1983|page=1-part 2}}</ref>}}

During the 1990s, [[National Basketball Association|NBA]] stars turned the alley-oop into the game's ultimate quick-strike weapon. In recent years,{{Clarify|date=February 2016}} teams have often run the alley-oop as a planned play. The 2008 National Champions [[Kansas Jayhawks]] had several designs for alley-oops, including some thrown from inbound sets, and could execute them interchangeably with almost all of the players being able to both lob and finish the play.

==In popular culture==
In the 2008 film ''[[Semi-Pro]]'', the protagonist invents the alley-oop after being knocked unconscious and speaking with his deceased mother in a depiction of Heaven.

In the song “Basketball” for the 2002 film ''[[Like Mike]]'', Lil Bow Wow raps “My favorite play is the alley-oop.”
