'''Noble rot''' ({{langx|fr|pourriture noble}}; {{langx|de|Edelfäule}}; {{langx|it|Muffa nobile}}; {{langx|hu|Aszúsodás}}) is the beneficial form of a grey [[fungus]], ''[[Botrytis cinerea]]'', affecting [[wine]] [[grape]]s.<ref name=OxfordAcademic>{{cite journal |last1=Blanco-Ulate |first1=B. |last2=Amrine |first2=K. |last3=Collins |first3=T. |date=December 2015 |title= Developmental and Metabolic Plasticity of White-Skinned Grape Berries in Response to ''Botrytis cinerea'' during Noble Rot |journal=Plant Physiology |volume=169 |issue=4 |pages=2422–2443 |bibcode=1985QuRes..23...87O |doi=10.1104/pp.15.00852|pmid=26450706 |pmc=4677888 |hdl=11336/10951 |hdl-access=free }}</ref> [[Infestation]] by ''Botrytis'' requires warm and humid conditions, typically around {{cvt|20|C}} and above 80% humidity.<ref name=sevenfiftydaily>{{cite web|title=The Science Behind Noble Rot Wines |url=https://daily.sevenfifty.com/the-science-behind-noble-rot-wines/ | publisher=SevenFiftyDaily |date=4 September 2023 |last=Clarke |first=Jim |access-date=30 June 2024}}</ref> If the weather stays wet, the undesirable form, "bunch rot" or "grey rot", adversely affects winemaking by disrupting fermentation and changing the taste, aroma, and appearance of the final wine.<ref name=OxfordAcademic/><ref name=sevenfiftydaily/><ref name=ScienceDirect/> Grapes typically become infected with ''cinerea'' when they are ripe. If they are then exposed to drier conditions and become partially [[raisin]]ed, this form of infection is known as noble rot. Grapes picked at a certain point during infestation can produce particularly fine and concentrated sweet wine. Wines produced by this method are known as '''botrytized wines''',<ref>{{cite book |last=Labensky |first=Steven|page=[https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780134757322/page/48 28] |title=Webster's new world dictionary of culinary arts |year=1997 |publisher=Prentice-Hall |isbn=9780134757322 |url=https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780134757322/page/48}}</ref> and are considered a distinct category of [[dessert wine]]s.<ref name=ScienceDirect/>

The primary distinction between botrytized wines and other naturally sweet, non-fortified sweet wines, such as late-harvest wines, [[ice wine]]s, or [[Straw wine|straw/raisin]] wines, lies in the range and richness of aroma compounds generated by ''Botrytis cinerea''.<ref name=ScienceDirect>{{cite journal |last1=Magyar |first1=I. |date=2011 |title= Chapter 6 - Botrytized Wines |journal=Advances in Food and Nutrition Research |volume=63 |pages=147–206 |doi=10.1016/B978-0-12-384927-4.00006-3|pmid=21867895 |isbn=978-0-12-384927-4 }}</ref> Significant differences are also evident in other components, like sugar alcohol and acid composition, due to microbial activity.<ref name=ScienceDirect/> [[Wine tasting descriptors|Descriptors]] frequently used for these wines highlight flavours of peach, apricot, pear, quince, raisin, and honey, along with unique "botrytis" or roti characteristics.<ref name=ScienceDirect/><ref name=vinoble>{{cite web |last= |first= | title=Botrytis Cinerea: The Noble Rot |work= Vinoble|url=https://www.vinoble.org/en/blog/botrytis-cinerea-noble-rot | publisher=vinoble.org |date=4 September 2023 |access-date=13 July 2024}}</ref> Additionally, a notable feature of botrytized wines is their high acid content, which prevents them from tasting cloying, even with sugar levels often exceeding 200 g/L.<ref name=ScienceDirect/>

== Effect on grapes ==
When ''Botrytis cinerea'' infects grapes, it initiates several physiological changes that are crucial for the development of noble rot wines. The fungus penetrates the grape skins while feeding on the grapes, causing microscopic wounds that lead to the evaporation of water from the berries.<ref name=vinoble /> This dehydration concentrates the remaining sugars, acids, and flavour compounds within the grapes, resulting in their higher density. The mass of the grapes can decrease by up to 60%, leaving behind a shrivelled, sugar-rich berry.<ref name=sevenfiftydaily/>

The fungus primarily consumes [[tartaric acid]], which is more prevalent than [[Malic acid|malic]] or [[citric acid]] in healthy grapes, with remaining tartaric acid transformed into [[gluconic acid]] and [[glycerol]].<ref name=vinoble /> As observed in Furmint grapes used for [[Tokaji Aszú]], the infection process changes the ratio of tartaric to malic acid, from 2:1 in healthy berries to 1:3 in botrytised berries. The increased levels of citric acid and the formation of higher levels of sugar alcohols (such as glycerol, [[arabitol]], [[mannitol]], [[sorbitol]], and [[inositol]]) contribute to the complexity and mouthfeel of botrytized wines.<ref name=OxfordAcademic/><ref name=sevenfiftydaily/> The grapes' metabolic stress response also leads to the production and concentration of various [[Aroma compound|aroma compounds]]. The berries concentrate precursors for citrus aromas like lemon and grapefruit, and also generate compounds for stone fruit aromas such as apricot and peach in a direct metabolic response to the fungal attack. Some of these aromatic characteristics, including the distinct honey aroma, are directly attributable to the botrytis fungus itself.<ref name=OxfordAcademic/><ref name=sevenfiftydaily/>

The stress response of grapes to botrytis infection is similar to their response to drought. The breakdown of grape skin cells by the fungus facilitates water evaporation, intensifying the dehydration process. This stress response allows the grapes to adapt metabolically, slowing down the infection rate compared to grey rot, which spreads too quickly for the plant to respond effectively.<ref name=sevenfiftydaily/>

==Origins==
According to Hungarian legend, the first [[Tokaji|aszú]] (a wine using botrytised grapes) was made by Laczkó Máté Szepsi in 1630. However, mention of wine made from botrytised grapes appears before this in the {{lang|hu|Nomenklatura}} of Fabricius Balázs Sziksai, which was completed in 1576.<ref>{{cite journal | last1=Thakur | first1=N. S. | title=Botrytized Wines: A Review | journal=International Journal of Food and Fermentation Technology | date=2018 | volume=8 | doi=10.30954/2277-9396.01.2018.1 | url=https://doi.org/10.30954/2277-9396.01.2018.1 | doi-access=free }}</ref> When vineyard classification began in 1730 in the [[Tokaj-Hegyalja|Tokaj]] region, one of the gradings given to the various {{lang|fr|[[terroir]]s}} centered on their potential to develop ''Botrytis cinerea''.

There is a popular story that the practice originated independently in [[Germany]], where the [[Riesling]] producers at [[Schloss Johannisberg]] ([[Geisenheim]], in the [[Rheingau]] region) traditionally awaited the say-so of the estate owner, [[Heinrich von Bibra]], Bishop of [[Fulda]], before cutting their grapes. In 1775, the story claims the abbey messenger was robbed en route to delivering the order, delaying cutting by three weeks, time enough for the ''Botrytis'' to take hold. The grapes were presumed worthless and given to local peasants,<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.uncork.biz/tidbits11.htm |title=A Short history of Riesling........ |publisher<!--Company name-->=Uncork |date=<!--not stated--> |access-date=6 September 2026}}</ref> who produced a surprisingly good, sweet wine which became known as {{lang|de|[[Spätlese]]}}, or [[late harvest wine]]. In the following few years, several different classes of increasing [[must weight]] were introduced, and the original {{lang|de|Spätlese}} was further elaborated, first into {{lang|de|[[Auslese]]}} in 1787<ref>{{cite book |first=Karen |last=MacNeil |title=The Wine Bible |publisher=Workman |year=2001 |page=540 |isbn=1-56305-434-5}}</ref> and later {{lang|de|[[Eiswein]]}} in 1858 (although {{lang|de|Eiswein}} is usually made from grapes not affected by ''Botrytis'').<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.schloss-johannisberg.de/en/history.htm |title=A History of Schloss Johannisberg |access-date=2010-03-25 |archive-date=2012-03-04 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120304152953/http://www.schloss-johannisberg.de/en/history.htm |url-status=dead }}</ref>

==Viticulture and uses==
[[File:SemillonBotrytisCinerea.JPG|thumb|''Botrytis cinerea'' on [[Sémillon]] grapes in [[Sauternes, Gironde|Sauternes]]]]
In some cases, inoculation occurs when [[spore]]s of the fungus are sprayed over the grapes, while some vineyards depend on natural inoculation from spores present in the environment.

Botrytized wines can be made using many varieties of grape. Due to [[anthocyanin]] oxidation, a majority of varieties are white. The ideal varieties mature late in the season in order for ripening to coincide with the best environmental conditions for noble rot to occur. Thick skinned cultivars can also be used to make harvesting infected grapes less difficult. [[Riesling]] and [[Sémillon]] are the primary cultivars used in the production of botrytized wines. The Hungarian variety, [[Furmint]], is the predominant variety used in the production of Tokaji [[Aszú]]. Other varieties, such as [[Picolit]], [[Gewürztraminer]], [[Chenin blanc]], and [[Pinot blanc]], are used, depending on tradition and adaptation to local conditions. {{cite book |last= Jackson|first=Ronald |date= 2008|title= Wine Science Principles and Applications|location= Oxford, UK|publisher= Elsevier Inc.|page=525 |isbn=978-0-12-373646-8 }}

Some of the finest botrytized wines are picked berry by berry in successive {{lang|fr|tris}} ([[French language|French]] for "selections").

Internationally renowned botrytized wines include the [[aszú]] of [[Tokaj-Hegyalja]] in [[Hungary]] (commonly called Tokaji or Tokay),<ref name=vinoble /> [[Sauternes (wine)|Sauternes]] from [[France]]<ref name=vinoble /> – where the process is known as {{lang|fr|pourriture}} or {{lang|fr|pourriture noble}}, and [[Beerenauslese]] or [[Trockenbeerenauslese]] wines from [[Germany]] and [[Austria]].<ref name=vinoble /> Other wines of this type include the [[Romania]]n [[Grasă de Cotnari]], French [[Coteaux du Layon]], French [[Monbazillac AOC|Monbazillac]], Austrian [[Ausbruch]],<ref name=vinoble /> South African Noble Late Harvest and Croatian wine maker Vinarija Mihalj from [[Kutjevo]] (producing Mačevo brdo-Graševina). Depending on conditions, the grapes may be only minimally botrytized. Botrytized wines are also produced by [[California wine|Californian]] and [[Australian wine|Australian]] winemakers.<ref name=vinoble />
