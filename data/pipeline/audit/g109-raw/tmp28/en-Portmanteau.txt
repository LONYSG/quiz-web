In [[literary theory|literary studies]], a '''portmanteau''',{{efn|The term ''portmanteau'' is prononunced {{IPAc-en|audio=en-us-portmanteau-1.ogg|p|ɔɹ|t|ˈ|m|æ|n|t|oʊ}} {{respell|port|MAN|toh}} or {{IPAc-en|ˌ|p|ɔɹ|(|t|)|m|æ|n|ˈ|t|oʊ}} {{respell|POR(T)|man|TOH}};<ref name="MW"/> the plural is ''portmanteaus'' or ''portmanteaux''.}} also known in [[linguistics]] and [[lexicography]] as a '''blend word''', a '''lexical blend''', or simply a '''blend''', is a word formed by combining the meanings and parts of the sounds of two or more words.<ref name="Garner">[https://books.google.com/books?id=FwmQpyibKkAC&q=portmanteau&pg=PA644 Garner's Modern American Usage], p.&nbsp;644.</ref><ref name="MW">{{cite encyclopedia|url=http://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/portmanteau|title=Portmanteau|access-date=21 June 2008|encyclopedia=Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary|df=dmy-all}}</ref><ref>{{cite encyclopedia|url=http://www.bartleby.com/61/91/P0459100.html|access-date=21 June 2008|title=Portmanteau|encyclopedia=The American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language|edition=4th|year=2000|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071126113733/http://www.bartleby.com/61/91/P0459100.html|archive-date=26 November 2007|df=dmy-all}}</ref> English examples include ''[[smog]]'', coined by blending ''smoke'' and ''fog'',<ref name="MW"/><ref name="Websters college">{{cite encyclopedia|year=2010|title=portmanteau word|encyclopedia=Webster's New World College Dictionary|publisher=Wiley|location=Cleveland|isbn=978-0-7645-7125-1}}</ref> and ''[[motel]]'', blending ''motor'' (or ''[[motorist]]'') and ''hotel''.<ref>{{cite encyclopedia|title=Portmanteau word|url=http://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/471231/portmanteau-word|encyclopedia=Encyclopædia Britannica}}</ref> The term "portmanteau", derived from the French {{lang|fr|porte-manteau}}, literally is a [[Portmanteau (luggage)|two-part piece of luggage]] that was first applied metaphorically in [[Lewis Carroll]]'s ''[[Through the Looking-Glass]]'' (1871) to describe the combination of words.

==Similar concepts==
A blend is similar to but distinct from a [[contraction (grammar)|contraction]]. On one hand, mainstream blends tend to be formed at a particular historical moment followed by a rapid rise in popularity. On the other hand, contractions are formed by the gradual drifting together of words over time due to the words commonly appearing together in sequence, such as ''do not'' naturally becoming ''don't'' (phonologically, {{IPAc-en|d|uː|_|n|ɒ|t}} becoming {{IPAc-en|d|oʊ|n|t}}); however, ''don't'' is also an example of a [[#Portmanteau morph|portmanteau morph]]. 

A blend also differs from a [[compound (linguistics)|compound]], which fully preserves the [[stem (linguistics)|stems]] of the original words. The British [[lecturer]] Valerie Adams's 1973 ''Introduction to Modern English Word-Formation'' explains that "In words such as ''motel''..., ''hotel'' is represented by various shorter substitutes – ''{{nbh}}otel''... – which I shall call splinters. Words containing splinters I shall call blends".<ref>Valerie Adams, ''An Introduction to Modern English Word-Formation'', Harlow, Essex: Longman, 1973; {{ISBN|0-582-55042-4}}, p. 142.</ref><ref group="n">Adams attributes the term ''splinter'' to J.&nbsp;M. Berman, "Contribution on blending," ''Zeitschrift für Anglistik und Amerikanistik'' 9 (1961), pp. 278–281.</ref> Thus, at least one of the parts of a blend, strictly speaking, is not a complete [[morpheme]], but instead a mere splinter or leftover word fragment. For instance, ''starfish'' is a compound, not a blend, of ''star'' and ''fish'', as it includes both words in full. However, if it were called a "''stish''" or a "''starsh''", it would be a blend. Furthermore, when blends are formed by shortening established compounds or phrases, they can be considered [[clipped compound]]s, such as ''romcom'' for ''romantic comedy''.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Fandrych|first1=Ingrid|title=Submorphemic elements in the formation of acronyms, blends and clippings|journal=Lexis|date=10 November 2008|issue=2|doi=10.4000/lexis.713|doi-access=free}}</ref>

''Contamination'', in some languages, refers to a subset of blends, where the words combined are synonyms or have similar meanings.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Murugova |first=Elena V. |date=2018-04-30 |title=Linguacognitive Mechanisms Of Conceptual Contaminants Integration In The Modern English Language |url=https://www.europeanproceedings.com/article/10.15405/epsbs.2018.04.02.59 |journal=European Proceedings of Social and Behavioural Sciences |language=en |volume=Word, Utterance, Text: Cognitive, Pragmatic and Cultural Aspects |doi=10.15405/epsbs.2018.04.02.59 |issn=2357-1330|doi-access=free }}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Wheeler |first=Benjamin Ide |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7591/j.cttq44vj |title=Analogy and the Scope of Its Application in Language |date=1887 |publisher=Cornell University Press |volume=2 |doi=10.7591/j.cttq44vj}}</ref> This kind of blend can be deliberate or accidental.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Vasileanu |first=Monica |last2=Niculescu-Gorpin |first2=Anabella-Gloria |last3=Radu-Bejenaru |first3=Cristina-Andreea |date=July 2024 |title=Keep calm and carry on blending: Experimental insights into Romanian lexical blending |url=https://www.euppublishing.com/doi/10.3366/word.2024.0236 |journal=Word Structure |language=en |volume=17 |issue=1-2 |pages=56–90 |doi=10.3366/word.2024.0236 |issn=1750-1245|url-access=subscription |doi-access=free }}</ref>

==Classification==
Blends of two or more words may be classified from each of three viewpoints: morphotactic, morphonological, and morphosemantic.<ref name="mattiello_chap4">Elisa Mattiello, "Blends." Chap.&nbsp;4 (pp.&nbsp;111–140) of ''Extra-grammatical Morphology in English: Abbreviations, Blends, Reduplicatives, and Related Phenomena'' (Berlin: De Gruyter Mouton, 2013; {{doi|10.1515/9783110295399}}; {{ISBN|978-3-11-029539-9}}).</ref>

===Morphotactic classification===
Blends may be classified [[Morphotactics|morphotactically]] into two kinds: ''total'' and ''partial''.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/>

====Total blends====
In a total blend, each of the words creating the blend is reduced to a mere splinter.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/> Some linguists limit blends to these (perhaps with additional conditions): for example, [[Ingo Plag]] considers "proper blends" to be total blends that semantically are coordinate, the remainder being "shortened compounds".<ref>Ingo Plag, ''Word Formation in English'' (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2003; {{ISBN|0-521-81959-8}}, {{ISBN|0-521-52563-2}}), 121–126.</ref>

Commonly for English blends, the beginning of one word is followed by the end of another:
*''breakfast'' + ''lunch''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''brunch''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example" >Example provided by Elisa Mattiello's chapter "Blends" (of ''Extra-grammatical Morphology in English: Abbreviations, Blends, Reduplicatives, and Related Phenomena'', Berlin: De Gruyter Mouton, 2013) of a blend of this kind.</ref>
*''low-key + genuinely{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}lowkenuinely''
*''motor + hotel{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}motel''

Much less commonly in English, the beginning of one word may be followed by the beginning of another:
*''teleprinter'' + ''exchange''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''telex''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''American'' + ''Indian''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''Amerind''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''microcomputer'' + ''software'' {{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}} ''Microsoft''
Some linguists do not regard beginning+beginning concatenations as blends, instead calling them complex clippings,<ref>Stefan Th. Gries, "Quantitative corpus data on blend formation: Psycho- and cognitive-linguistic perspectives", in Vincent Renner, François Maniez, Pierre Arnaud, eds, ''Cross-Disciplinary Perspectives on Lexical Blending'' (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2012; {{ISBN|978-3-11-028923-7}}), 145–168.</ref> clipping compounds<ref>[[Laurie Bauer]], "Blends: Core and periphery", in Vincent Renner, François Maniez, Pierre Arnaud, eds, ''Cross-Disciplinary Perspectives on Lexical Blending'' (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2012; {{ISBN|978-3-11-028923-7}}), 11–22.</ref> or [[clipped compound]]s.<ref>Outi Bat-El and Evan-Gary Cohen, "Stress in English blends: A constraint-based analysis", in Vincent Renner, François Maniez, Pierre Arnaud, eds, ''Cross-Disciplinary Perspectives on Lexical Blending'' (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2012; {{ISBN|978-3-11-028923-7}})</ref>

Unusually in English, the end of one word may be followed by the end of another:
*''Red Bull'' + ''margarita''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''bullgarita''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''Hello Kitty'' + ''delicious''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''kittylicious''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>

A splinter of one word may replace part of another, as in two coined by [[Lewis Carroll]] in "[[Jabberwocky]]":
*''chuckle'' + ''snort''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''chortle''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''slimy'' + ''lithe''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''slithy''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
They are sometimes termed ''intercalative'' blends; these words are among the original "portmanteaus" for which this meaning of the word was created.<ref name="kemmer2003">Suzanne Kemmer, "Schemas and lexical blends." In Hubert C. Cuyckens et al., eds, ''Motivation in Language: From Case Grammar to Cognitive Linguistics: Studies in Honour of Günter Radden'' (Amsterdam: Benjamins, 2003; {{ISBN|9789027247551}}, {{ISBN|9781588114266}}).</ref>

====Partial blends====
In a partial blend, one entire word is concatenated with a splinter from another.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/> Some linguists do not recognize these as blends.<ref>[[Angela Ralli]] and George J. Xydopoulos, "Blend formation in Modern Greek", in Vincent Renner, François Maniez, Pierre Arnaud, eds, ''Cross-Disciplinary Perspectives on Lexical Blending'' (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2012; {{ISBN|978-3-11-028923-7}}), 35–50.</ref>

An entire word may be followed by a splinter:
*''fan'' + ''magazine''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''fanzine''<ref group="n">Example provided by Elisa Mattiello's chapter "Blends" (of ''Extra-grammatical Morphology in English: Abbreviations, Blends, Reduplicatives, and Related Phenomena'', Berlin: De Gruyter Mouton, 2013) of a blend of this kind. (Etymologically, ''fan'' is a clipping of ''fanatic''; but it has since become lexicalized.)</ref>
*''dumb'' + ''confound''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''dumbfound''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example" />

A splinter may be followed by an entire word:
*''Brad'' + ''Angelina''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''Brangelina''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''American'' + ''Indian''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''Amerindian''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>

An entire word may replace part of another:
*''adorable'' + ''dork''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''adorkable''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''disgusting'' + ''gross''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''disgrossting''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
These have also been called ''sandwich'' words,<ref>[[Harold Wentworth (lexicographer)|Harold Wentworth]], "'Sandwich' words and rime-caused nonce words", ''West Virginia University Bulletin: Philological Studies'' 3 (1939), 65–71; cited in {{Cite journal|jstor=454719|title=Blends, a Structural and Systemic View|last1=Algeo|first1=John|authorlink=John Algeo|journal=American Speech|volume=52|issue=1/2|pages=47–64|year=1977|doi=10.2307/454719}}</ref> and classed among ''intercalative'' blends.<ref name="kemmer2003"/>

(When two words are combined in their entirety, the result is considered a [[compound word]] rather than a blend. For example, [[bagpipe]] is a compound, not a blend, of ''bag'' and ''pipe.'')

===Morphological classification===
Morphologically, blends fall into two kinds: ''overlapping'' and ''non-overlapping''.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/>

====Overlapping blends====
Overlapping blends are those for which the ingredients' consonants, vowels or even syllables overlap to some extent. The overlap can be of different kinds.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/> These are also called haplologic blends.<ref>Francis A. Wood, "Iteratives, blends, and 'Streckformen'," ''Modern Philology'' 9 (1911), 157–194.</ref>

There may be an overlap that is both phonological and orthographic, but with no other shortening:
*''anecdote'' + ''dotage''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''anec<u>dot</u>age''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''pal'' + ''alimony''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''p<u>al</u>imony''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>

The overlap may be both phonological and orthographic, and with some additional shortening to at least one of the ingredients:
*''California'' + ''fornication''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''Cali<u>forni</u>cation''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_list">Elisa Mattiello, "Lexical index." Appendix (pp.&nbsp;287–329) to ''Extra-grammatical Morphology in English: Abbreviations, Blends, Reduplicatives, and Related Phenomena'' (Berlin: De Gruyter Mouton, 2013; {{doi|10.1515/9783110295399}}; {{ISBN|978-3-11-029539-9}}).</ref>
*''picture'' + ''dictionary''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''p<u>ict</u>ionary''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>

Such an overlap may be discontinuous:
*''politician'' + ''pollution''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''<u>poll</u>uti<u>cian</u>''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example_am">Example provided by Elisa Mattiello's chapter "Blends" (of ''Extra-grammatical Morphology in English: Abbreviations, Blends, Reduplicatives, and Related Phenomena'', Berlin: De Gruyter Mouton, 2013) of a blend of this kind, slightly amended.</ref>
*''beef'' + ''buffalo''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''<u>b</u>ee<u>f</u>alo''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
These are also termed imperfect blends.<ref>{{Cite journal|jstor=454719|title=Blends, a Structural and Systemic View|last1=Algeo|first1=John|journal=American Speech|volume=52|issue=1/2|pages=47–64|year=1977|doi=10.2307/454719}}</ref><ref>Michael H. Kelly, "To 'brunch' or to 'brench': Some aspects of blend structure," ''Linguistics'' 36 (1998), 579–590.</ref>

It can occur with three components:
*''camisade'' + ''cannibalism'' + ''ballistics''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''<u>ca</u>m<u>ibali</u>stics''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example_fw">Example provided by Mattiello of a blend of this kind. The word is found in ''[[Finnegans Wake]]''; Mattiello credits Almuth Grésillon, ''La règle et le monstre: Le mot-valise. Interrogations sur la langue, à partir d'un corpus de Heinrich Heine'' (Tübingen: Max Niemeyer, 1984), 15, for bringing it to her attention.</ref>
*''meander'' + ''Neanderthal'' + ''tale''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''meandertale''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example_fw"/>

The phonological overlap need not also be orthographic:
*''back'' + ''acronym''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''b<u>ack</u>ronym''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''war'' + ''orgasm''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''w<u>ar</u>gasm''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>

If the phonological but non-orthographic overlap encompasses the whole of the shorter ingredient, as in
*''sin'' + ''cinema''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''<u>sin</u>ema''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''sham'' + ''champagne''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''<u>sham</u>pagne''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
then the effect depends on orthography alone. (They are also called orthographic blends.<ref>Adrienne Lehrer, "Blendalicious," in Judith Munat, ed., ''Lexical Creativity, Texts and Contexts'' (Amsterdam: Benjamins, 2007; {{ISBN|9789027215673}}), 115–133.</ref>)

An orthographic overlap need not also be phonological:
*''smoke'' + ''fog''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''sm<u>o</u>g''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''binary'' + ''digit''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''b<u>i</u>t''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''bro'' + ''trogladyte''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''b<u>ro</u>gladyte''

For some linguists, an overlap is a condition for a blend.<ref>Giorgio-Francesco Arcodia and Fabio Montermini, "Are reduced compounds compounds? Morphological and prosodic properties of reduced compounds in Russian and Mandarin Chinese", in Vincent Renner, François Maniez, Pierre Arnaud, eds, ''Cross-Disciplinary Perspectives on Lexical Blending'' (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2012; {{ISBN|978-3-11-028923-7}}), 93–114.</ref>

====Non-overlapping blends====
Non-overlapping blends (also called substitution blends) have no overlap, whether phonological or orthographic:
*''California'' + ''Mexico''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''Calexico''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_example"/>
*''beautiful'' + ''delicious''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''beaulicious''<ref group="n" name="mattiello_list"/>

===Morphosemantic classification===
Morphosemantically, blends fall into two kinds: ''attributive'' and ''coordinate''.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/>

====Attributive blends====
Attributive blends (also called syntactic or telescope blends) are blends where one of the ingredients is the head and the other is attributive. A ''porta-light'' is a portable light, not a 'light-emitting' or light portability; in this instance, ''light'' is the head, while "porta-" is attributive. A ''snobject'' is a snobbery-satisfying object and not an objective or other kind of snob; object is the head.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/>

As is also true for (conventional, non-blend) attributive [[Compound (linguistics)|compounds]] (among which ''bathroom'', for example, is a kind of room, not a kind of bath), the attributive blends of English are mostly [[Head (linguistics)|head-final]] and mostly [[Endocentric and exocentric|endocentric]]. As an example of an exocentric attributive blend, ''[[Fruitopia]]'' may metaphorically take the buyer to a fruity utopia (and not a utopian fruit); however, it is not a utopia but a drink.

====Coordinate blends====
Coordinate blends (also called associative or portmanteau blends) combine two words having equal status, and have two heads. Thus ''brunch'' is neither a breakfasty lunch nor a lunchtime breakfast but instead some hybrid of breakfast and lunch; ''[[Oxbridge]]'' is equally Oxford and Cambridge universities. This too parallels (conventional, non-blend) compounds: an ''actor–director'' is equally an actor and a director.<ref name="mattiello_chap4"/>

Two kinds of coordinate blends are particularly conspicuous: those that combine (near{{nbh}}) synonyms:
*''gigantic'' + ''enormous''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''ginormous''
*''insinuation'' + ''innuendo''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''insinuendo''
and those that combine (near{{nbh}}) opposites:
*''transmitter'' + ''receiver''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''transceiver''
*''friend'' + ''enemy''{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}''frenemy''

===Blending of two roots===
Blending can also apply to [[Root (linguistics)|root]]s rather than words, for instance in [[Israeli Hebrew]]:
* {{Lang|he|רמז}} ({{Lang|he-latn|√rmz}} 'hint') + {{Lang|he|אור}} ({{Lang|he-latn|or}} 'light'){{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}{{Lang|he|רמזור}} ({{Lang|he-latn|ramzor}} 'traffic light')
* {{Lang|he|מגדל}} ({{Lang|he-latn|migdal}} 'tower') + {{Lang|he|אור}} ({{Lang|he-latn|or}} 'light'){{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}{{Lang|he|מגדלור}} ({{Lang|he-latn|migdalor}} 'lighthouse')
* [[Mishnaic Hebrew]]: {{Lang|he|דחפ}} ({{Lang|he-latn|√dħp}} 'push') + [[Biblical Hebrew]]: {{Lang|he|חפר}} ({{Lang|he-latn|√ħpr}} 'dig'){{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}{{Lang|he|דחפור}} ({{Lang|he-latn|dakhpór}} 'bulldozer')
* Israeli שלטוט ''shiltút'' 'zapping, surfing the channels, flipping through the channels' derives from
** (i) ([[Hebrew]]>) Israeli שלט ''shalát'' 'remote control', an ellipsis – like [[English language|English]] ''remote'' (but using the noun instead) – of the (widely known) compound שלט רחוק ''shalát rakhók'' – cf. the [[Academy of the Hebrew Language]]'s שלט רחק ''shalát rákhak''; and
** (ii) (Hebrew>) Israeli שטוט ''shitút'' 'wandering, vagrancy'. Israeli שלטוט ''shiltút'' was introduced by the Academy of the Hebrew Language in [...] 1996. Synchronically, it might appear to result from reduplication of the final consonant of ''shalát'' 'remote control'.
* Another example of blending which has also been explained as mere reduplication is Israeli גחלילית ''gakhlilít'' 'fire-fly, glow-fly, ''[[Lampyris]]''<nowiki>'</nowiki>. This coinage by [[Hayyim Nahman Bialik]] blends (Hebrew>) Israeli גחלת ''gakhélet'' 'burning coal' with (Hebrew>) Israeli לילה ''láyla'' 'night'. Compare this with the unblended חכלילית ''khakhlilít'' '(black) redstart, ''[[Phœnicurus]]''' (<Biblical Hebrew חכליל 'dull red, reddish'). Synchronically speaking though, most native Israeli-speakers feel that ''gakhlilít'' includes a reduplication of the third radical of גחל √għl. This is incidentally how [[Ernest Klein]]<ref>{{Cite book|last=Klein|first=Ernest|year=1987|title=A Comprehensive Etymological Dictionary of the Hebrew Language|location=Jerusalem|publisher=Carta}} See p. 97.</ref> explains ''gakhlilít''. Since he is attempting to provide etymology, his description might be misleading if one agrees that Hayyim Nahman Bialik had blending in mind."<ref>{{Cite book|authorlink=Ghil'ad Zuckermann|last=Zuckermann|first=Ghil'ad|year=2003|url=http://www.palgrave.com/br/book/9781403917232|title=Language Contact and Lexical Enrichment in Israeli Hebrew|publisher=Palgrave Macmillan|isbn=978-1403917232|page=66}}</ref>

"There are two possible etymological analyses for Israeli Hebrew כספר ''kaspár'' 'bank clerk, teller'. The first is that it consists of (Hebrew>) Israeli כסף ''késef'' 'money' and the ([[Internationalism (linguistics)|International]]/Hebrew>) Israeli [[Agent noun|agentive]] [[suffix]] ר- ''-ár''. The second is that it is a quasi-[[portmanteau word]] which blends כסף ''késef'' 'money' and (Hebrew>) Israeli ספר √spr 'count'. Israeli Hebrew כספר ''kaspár'' started as a brand name but soon entered the common language. Even if the second analysis is the correct one, the final syllable ר- ''-ár'' apparently facilitated nativization since it was regarded as the Hebrew suffix ר- ''-år'' (probably of [[Persian language|Persian]] pedigree), which usually refers to craftsmen and professionals, for instance as in [[Mendele Mocher Sforim]]'s coinage סמרטוטר ''smartutár'' 'rag-dealer'."<ref>Zuckermann 2003, p. 67.</ref>

====Lexical selection====
Blending may occur with an error in ''lexical selection'', the process by which a speaker uses his semantic knowledge to choose words. Lewis Carroll's explanation, which gave rise to the use of 'portmanteau' for such combinations, was:

<blockquote>Humpty Dumpty's theory, of two meanings packed into one word like a portmanteau, seems to me the right explanation for all. For instance, take the two words "fuming" and "furious." Make up your mind that you will say both words ... you will say "frumious."<ref>{{Cite book|first=Lewis|last=Carroll|year=2009|title=Alice's Adventures in Wonderland ''and'' Through the Looking-Glass|location=Oxford|publisher=Oxford University Press|isbn=978-0-19-955829-2}}</ref></blockquote>

The errors are based on similarity of meanings, rather than [[phonology|phonological]] similarities, and the morphemes or phonemes stay in the same position within the syllable.<ref>{{Cite book|last1=Fromkin|first1=Victoria|authorlink1=Victoria Fromkin|author-last2=Rodman|author-first2= Robert|author-last3=Hyams|author-first3= Nina|authorlink3=Nina Hyams|year=2007|title=An Introduction to Language|edition=8th|location=Boston|publisher=Thomson Wadsworth|isbn=978-1-4130-1773-1}}</ref>

==Use==
{{Unreferenced section|date=March 2011}}
Some languages, like [[Japanese language|Japanese]], encourage the shortening and merging of borrowed foreign words (as in [[gairaigo]]), because they are long or difficult to pronounce in the target language.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Myatt |first=James |date=2024-02-21 |title=Anglicisms in JapaneseWord Connection |url=https://www.word-connection.com/post/anglicisms-in-japanese |access-date=2025-08-03 |website=Word Connection |language=en}}</ref> For example, ''karaoke'', a combination of the Japanese word ''kara'' (meaning ''empty'') and the clipped form ''oke'' of the English loanword "orchestra" (J. ''ōkesutora'', {{Lang|ja|オーケストラ}}), is a Japanese blend that has entered the English language. The [[Vietnamese language]] also encourages blend words formed from [[Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary]]. For example, the term [[Viet Cong|Việt Cộng]] is derived from the first syllables of "Việt Nam" (Vietnam) and "Cộng sản" (communist).

Many corporate [[brand name]]s, trademarks, and initiatives, and names of corporations and organizations themselves, are blends. For example, [[Wiktionary]], one of [[Wikipedia]]'s sister projects, is a blend of ''[[wiki]]'' and ''[[dictionary]]''.

==Origin of the term ''portmanteau''==
=== Terminological history ===
==== In France ====
According to ''[[The American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language]]'' (''AHD''), the etymology of the word is the French {{lang|fr|porte-manteau}}, from {{lang|fr|porter}}, "to carry", and {{lang|fr|manteau}}, "[[overcoat]]"<!-- https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manteau_(vêtement) --> (from Old French {{lang|fr|mantel}}, from Latin {{lang|la|mantellum}}).<ref>"Portmanteau". ''The American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language: Fourth Edition''. 2000.</ref> 

According to the [[OED Online]], the etymology of the word is the "officer who carries the mantle of a person in a high position (1507 in Middle French), case or bag for carrying clothing (1547), clothes rack (1640)".<ref name="OED_online_portmanteau">{{Cite OED|portmanteau|access-date=18 July 2019}}</ref>

In ''[[Encyclopédie|Encyclopedia, or a Reasoned Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts and Crafts]]'' (1765),<ref name="Diderotd'Alembert">{{cite book |last1=Diderot |first1=Denis |author-link1=Denis Diderot |last2=d'Alembert |first2=Jean Le Rond |author-link2=Jean Le Rond d'Alembert |orig-year=1765 |year=1984|title=Encyclopédie ou Dictionnaire Raisonné des Sciences, des Arts et des Métiers: Pom − Regg. 13 |trans-title=Encyclopedia, or a Reasoned Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts and Crafts: Pom − Regg. 13 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=RCHyF7kETokC&newbks=0&printsec=frontcover&pg=PA142 |language=fr |location=Paris |publisher=Briasson |page=142 |isbn=978-0-0809-0105-3 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260207150653/https://books.google.com/books?id=RCHyF7kETokC&newbks=0&printsec=frontcover&pg=PA142 |archive-date=7 February 2026 |access-date=7 February 2026}}</ref> in [[History of French#Modern French|Classical French]] language,<ref>{{cite book |last1=Willis |first1=David |author-link1=David Willis (linguist) |last2=Lucas |first2=Christopher |last3=Breitbarth |first3=Anne |year=2013 |title=The History of Negation in the Languages of Europe and the Mediterranean |volume=I Case Studies |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=-WEUAAAAQBAJ&newbks=0&printsec=frontcover&pg=PA53 |location=England |publisher=[[Oxford University Press]] |page=53 |isbn=978-0-1996-0253-7 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260207150411/https://books.google.com/books?id=-WEUAAAAQBAJ&newbks=0&printsec=frontcover&pg=PA53 |archive-date=7 February 2026 |access-date=7 February 2026}}</ref> {{lang|fr|[[Denis Diderot]]|italic=no}} and {{lang|fr|[[Jean Le Rond d'Alembert]]|italic=no}} wrote,

{{Blockquote|{{lang|fr|Porte-manteau|italic=no}},  f.m. ({{lang|fr|Hift. mod.}}) is said of an officer in the household of the King of France. There are 12 of them. Their charge is to guard the King's hat, gloves, cane, sword, etc., to receive them from his hand, and to bring them to him when he needs them. The {{lang|fr|portemanteau}} follows the King on a hunt, with a suitcase or {{lang|fr|[[Hatstand|porte-manteau]]}} garnished with handkerchiefs, shirts, and other undergarments, so that [[Majesty|H. M.]] can change in case of need.{{break}}The [[Dauphin of France|Dauphin]] also has his {{lang|fr|portemanteau}}. Cardinals in Rome have ecclesiastical officers called {{lang|fr|[[Caudatario|caudataires]]}}, because they carry the train of their [[robe]], and in France there are {{lang|fr|[[Valet de chambre|valets-de-chambre]]|italic=no}} charged with the same office, which have some relation to the {{lang|fr|portemanteau}}.<ref name="Diderotd'Alembert"/>}}

In the [[France in the early modern period#France in the 17th and 18th centuries|18th century]] France, {{lang|fr|portemanteaux}} were considered a structure within wardrobes or closets, or attached to a wall, for hanging clothes, hats, and coats.<ref name="Diderotd'Alembert"/>

A {{lang|fr|porte-manteau}} in modern French is also a [[clothes valet]], a coat-tree or similar article of furniture for hanging up jackets, hats, umbrellas and the like.<ref>[[Petit Robert]]: ''portemanteau'' – "malle penderie" (suitcase in which clothes hang).</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.cnrtl.fr/lexicographie/portemanteau|title=Pportemanteau: Définition de Portemanteau|work=[[Centre national de ressources textuelles et lexicales]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140821190449/http://www.cnrtl.fr/lexicographie/portemanteau|archive-date=21 August 2014|df=dmy-all|language=fr}}</ref><ref>Such a "coat bag" is mentioned in Chapter 12 of Alexander Dumas' ''[[The Count of Monte Cristo]]''.</ref>

==== Derivation in the English language ====
A [[portmanteau (luggage)]] was a suitcase that opened into two equal sections.<ref name="Hollandbeck2019">{{cite news |last=Hollandbeck |first=Andy |date=July 2019 |title=In a Word: Fantabulous Portmanteau Words |url=https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2019/07/in-a-word-fantabulous-portmanteau-words/ |newspaper=[[The Saturday Evening Post]] |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260207213839/https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2019/07/in-a-word-fantabulous-portmanteau-words/ |archive-date=7 February 2026 |access-date=7 February 2026}}</ref> According to the OED Online, a portmanteau is a "case or bag for carrying clothing and other belongings when travelling; (originally) one of a form suitable for carrying on horseback; (now esp.) one in the form of a stiff leather case hinged at the back to open into two equal parts".<ref name="OED_online_portmanteau"/>

The word ''portmanteau'' was introduced in this sense by [[Lewis Carroll]] in the book ''[[Through the Looking-Glass]]'' (1871),<ref name="Hollandbeck2019"/><ref name="oed">{{cite encyclopedia|year=2010|title=portmanteau, ''n.''|encyclopedia=Oxford English Dictionary, third edition|publisher=Oxford University Press|location=Oxford|url=http://www.oed.com/view/Entry/148217|access-date=23 February 2011}}</ref> where [[Humpty Dumpty]] explains to [[Alice (Alice's Adventures in Wonderland)|Alice]] the coinage of unusual words used in "[[Jabberwocky]]".<ref name="multiple">Fromkin, V., Rodman, R., and Hyams, N. (2007). ''An Introduction to Language'', Eighth Edition. Boston: Thomson Wadsworth. {{ISBN|1-4130-1773-8}}.</ref> ''Slithy'' means "slimy and lithe" and ''mimsy'' means "miserable and flimsy". Humpty Dumpty explains to Alice the practice of combining words in various ways, comparing it to the then-common [[portmanteau (luggage)|type of luggage]], which opens into two equal parts:
{{Blockquote|You see it's like a portmanteau—there are two meanings packed up into one word.}}

In his introduction to his 1876 poem ''[[The Hunting of the Snark]]'', Carroll again uses ''portmanteau'' when discussing lexical selection:<ref name="multiple"/>
{{blockquote|Humpty Dumpty's theory, of two meanings packed into one word like a portmanteau, seems to me the right explanation for all. For instance, take the two words "fuming" and "furious". Make up your mind that you will say both words, but leave it unsettled which you will say first … if you have the rarest of gifts, a perfectly balanced mind, you will say "frumious".}}

An occasional synonym for "portmanteau word" is ''frankenword'', an [[autological word]] exemplifying the phenomenon it describes, blending "[[Frankenstein]]" and "word".<ref>[https://www.theguardian.com/media/mind-your-language/2016/feb/05/frankenwords-portmanteau-blend-words "Frankenwords: They're Alive!"] ''The Guardian'', 5 February 2016. {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170110043343/https://www.theguardian.com/media/mind-your-language/2016/feb/05/frankenwords-portmanteau-blend-words|date=10 January 2017}}.</ref>

=== Terminology preference ===
==== ''Portmanteau'' and ''blend'' ====
The term "portmanteau word" was retained by literary scholars and "widely imposed itself" in Anglo-Saxon literature. [[Linguistics|Linguists]] such as [[Louise Pound|Pound]] (1914), [[Ruth A. Berman|Berman]] (1961), Adams (1973), and [[Margaret M. Bryant|Bryant]] (1973–74), preferred "blend".<ref name="Grésillon2017">{{cite book |last=Grésillon |first=Almuth |year=2017 |title=La règle et le monstre: le mot-valise: Interrogations sur la langue, à partir d'un corpus de Heinrich Heine |trans-title=The rule and the monster: the portmanteau word: Questions about language, based on a corpus by Heinrich Heine |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=0YJdDwAAQBAJ&newbks=0&printsec=frontcover&pg=PA5 |language=fr |location=Germany |publisher=[[De Gruyter Brill]] |pages=5–6 |isbn= 978-3-4843-0152-8 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260207183700/https://books.google.com/books?id=0YJdDwAAQBAJ&newbks=0&printsec=frontcover&pg=PA5 |archive-date=7 February 2026 |access-date=7 February 2026}}</ref> [[Lexicography|Lexicographers]] also identify such words as "blends".<ref name="Hollandbeck2019"/>

==== ''Mot-valise'' ====
Referring at some point to "the two-part envelope containing the horse rider's pack", the old French word "portemanteau" lost its meaning when it was derived and exported into English as "portmanteau". Author and linguist Almuth Grésillon wrote that the French term {{lang|fr|mot-portemanteau}} ({{lang|fr|'mot'}}: word) "risked not evoking even the vague idea that {{Bracket|Lewis}} Carroll had sketched". Thus, the term "''[[:fr:mot-valise|mot-valise]]''" ({{literal translation|word-suitcase}}), and not "portmanteau", is used in France to designate a word formed by the merging of other words.<ref name="Grésillon2017"/>

==Examples in English==
{{Main list|List of portmanteaus
}}
[[File:The Gerry-Mander Edit.png|thumb|right|The original ''[[Gerrymander]]'' pictured in an 1812 cartoon. The word is a portmanteau of Massachusetts Governor [[Elbridge Gerry]]'s name with ''[[Salamander (legendary creature)|salamander]]''.]]

Many [[neologism]]s are examples of blends, but many blends have become part of the lexicon.<ref name="multiple"/> In ''[[Punch (magazine)|Punch]]'' in 1896, the word [[brunch]] (breakfast + lunch) was introduced as a "portmanteau word".<ref>''[[Punch (magazine)|Punch]]'', 1 August 1896, 58/2</ref> In 1964, the newly independent African republic of [[Tanganyika (1961–1964)|Tanganyika]] and [[Zanzibar]] chose the portmanteau word [[Tanzania]] as its name. Similarly, [[Eurasia]] is a portmanteau of Europe and Asia.

Some city names are portmanteaus of the border regions they straddle: [[Texarkana, Texas|Texarkana]] spreads across the Texas-Arkansas-Louisiana border, while [[Calexico, California|Calexico]] and [[Mexicali]] are respectively the American and Mexican sides of a single [[conurbation]]. A scientific example is a ''[[liger]]'', which is a cross between a male lion and a female tiger (a ''[[tigon]]'' is a similar cross in which the male is a tiger). A more modern blend of ‘Cat’ and ‘Rabbit’ was founded 2023 on X (formerly known as Twitter) to describe a circulating image of a mix between the two, producing the word ‘[[Cabbit]]’.

Many company or brand names are portmanteaus, including [[Microsoft]], a portmanteau of ''[[microcomputer]]'' and ''software''; the cheese ''[[Cambozola]]'' combines a similar rind to ''[[Camembert]]'' with the same mould used to make ''[[Gorgonzola]]''; passenger rail company ''[[Amtrak]]'', a portmanteau of ''[[United States|America]]'' and ''[[Track (rail transport)|track]]''; ''[[Velcro]]'', a portmanteau of the French {{Lang|fr|velours}} (velvet) and {{Lang|fr|crochet}} (hook); ''[[Verizon]]'', a portmanteau of {{Lang|la|veritas}} (Latin for truth) and ''horizon''; [[Viacom (2005-2019)|Viacom]], a portmanteau of Video and Audio communications, and [[ComEd]] (a Chicago-area electric utility company), a portmanteau of ''Commonwealth'' and ''[[Thomas Edison|Edison]]''.

''Jeoportmanteau!'' is a recurring category on the American television [[quiz show]] ''[[Jeopardy!]]'' The category's name is itself a portmanteau of the words ''Jeopardy'' and ''portmanteau''. Responses in the category are portmanteaus constructed by fitting two words together.

Portmanteau words may be produced by joining [[proper nouns]] with common nouns, such as "[[gerrymandering]]", which refers to the scheme of Massachusetts Governor [[Elbridge Gerry]] for politically contrived redistricting; the perimeter of one of the districts thereby created resembled a very curvy [[Salamander (legendary creature)|salamander]] in outline. The term gerrymander has itself contributed to portmanteau terms [[bjelkemander]] and [[playmander]].

[[Oxbridge]] is a common portmanteau for the UK's two oldest universities, those of [[Oxford]] and [[Cambridge]]. In 2016, Britain's planned [[withdrawal from the European Union|exit from the European Union]] became known as "[[Brexit]]".

[[File:Spork.jpg|thumb|right|A [[spork]]]]

The word ''refudiate'' was famously used by [[Sarah Palin]] when she misspoke, conflating the words ''refute'' and ''repudiate''. Though the word was a [[gaffe]], it was recognized as the ''New Oxford American Dictionary''{{'}}s "Word of the Year" in 2010.<ref>{{cite web|title=New Oxford American Dictionary's 2010 Word of the Year Is...|url=http://blog.oxforddictionaries.com/2010/11/noad-2010-word-of-the-year/|access-date=30 January 2012|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120116163439/http://blog.oxforddictionaries.com/2010/11/noad-2010-word-of-the-year/|archive-date=16 January 2012}}</ref>

The business lexicon includes words like "[[advertainment]]" (advertising as entertainment), "[[advertorial]]" (a blurred distinction between advertising and editorial), "[[infotainment]]" (information about entertainment or itself intended to entertain by its manner of presentation), and "[[infomercial]]" (informational commercial).

Company and product names may also use portmanteau words: examples include ''Timex'' (a portmanteau of ''Time'' [referring to [[Time magazine]]] and [[Kleenex]]),<ref name="kleenex">{{cite magazine|title=The crazy, true-life adventures of Norway's most radical billionaire|magazine=Fortune|first=Shawn|last=Tully|date=7 March 2015|quote=A few years later Thomas Olsen would rechristen the company Timex. He claims he hatched the iconic name from an unusual confluence of sources. Recalls Fred: "My father always loved to noodle with words. He liked to read Time magazine, and he used a lot of Kleenex, so he put the two names together and got Timex."|url=http://fortune.com/2015/03/07/fred-olsen/|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160728022406/http://fortune.com/2015/03/07/fred-olsen/|archive-date=28 July 2016|df=dmy-all}}</ref> [[Renault]]'s ''[[Renault Twingo|Twingo]]'' (a combination of ''twist'', ''swing'' and ''tango''),<ref>{{cite web|title=Twingo I|url=https://www.press.renault.co.uk/models/twingo-i|website=Renault UK Press Office|publisher=Renault|access-date=15 July 2019|archive-date=8 September 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190908235714/https://www.press.renault.co.uk/models/twingo-i|url-status=live}}</ref> and [[Garmin]] (portmanteau of company founders' first names [[Gary Burrell]] and [[Min Kao]]). "[[Desilu Productions]]" was a Los Angeles–based company jointly owned by actor couple [[Desi Arnaz]] and [[Lucille Ball]]. [[Miramax]] is the combination of the first names of the parents of the [[Weinstein Brothers|Weinstein brothers]].

===Name-meshing===
{{Main|Name blending}}

Given names may originate as portmanteaux. For example, [[Cheryl]] may be a combination of [[chérie]] and [[Beryl (given name)|Beryl]].<ref>{{Cite news |last=Diffin |first=Elizabeth  |date=2010-02-25 |title=How do you pronounce 'Cheryl'? |url=http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/uk_news/magazine/8536213.stm |access-date=2026-03-28 |work=BBC News |language=en-GB}}</ref> The name [[Jalen]] originated with [[Jalen Rose]] as a combination of James and Leonard.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Berzon |first=Alexandra |last2=Kirkham |first2=Chris |date=2017-03-22 |title=It’s Not Your Imagination: There are Loads of Jalens in College Basketball |url=https://www.wsj.com/articles/its-not-your-imagination-there-are-loads-of-jalens-in-college-basketball-1490197872 |access-date=2026-03-28 |work=Wall Street Journal |language=en-US |issn=0099-9660}}</ref> [[Jonathan Swift]] created the nickname [[Vanessa (name)|Vanessa]] for [[Esther Vanhomrigh|Esther (Essa) Vanhomrigh]]. [[Marlene Dietrich]], born Marie Magdalene, combined her names.  

Two names can also be used in creating a portmanteau word in reference to the partnership between people, especially in cases where both persons are well-known, or sometimes to produce [[epithet]]s such as "Billary" (referring to former United States president [[Bill Clinton]] and his wife, former United States Secretary of State [[Hillary Clinton]]). In this example of recent American political history, the purpose for blending is not so much to combine the meanings of the source words but "to suggest a resemblance of one named person to the other"; the effect is often derogatory, as linguist [[Benjamin Zimmer]] states.<ref>{{cite web|last=Zimmer|first=Benjamin|author-link=Benjamin Zimmer|title=A perilous portmanteau?|work=[[Language Log]]|publisher=[[University of Pennsylvania]]|date=1 November 2005|url=https://itre.cis.upenn.edu/~myl/languagelog/archives/002610.html|access-date=11 November 2008|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081229180953/http://itre.cis.upenn.edu/~myl/languagelog/archives/002610.html|archive-date=29 December 2008|df=dmy-all}}</ref> For instance, [[Putler]] is used by critics of [[Vladimir Putin]], merging his name with [[Adolf Hitler]]. By contrast, the public, including the media, use portmanteaus to refer to their favorite pairings as a way to "...giv[e] people an essence of who they are within the same name."<ref name="news.BBC.co.uk">{{cite news|first=Denise|last=Winterman|title=What a mesh|work=BBC News Magazine|date=3 August 2006|access-date=17 July 2008|url=http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/magazine/5239464.stm|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071216171930/http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/magazine/5239464.stm|archive-date=16 December 2007}}</ref> This is particularly seen in cases of fictional and real-life "[[supercouple]]s". An early known example, [[Bennifer]], referred to film stars [[Ben Affleck]] and [[Jennifer Lopez]]. Other examples include [[Brangelina]] ([[Brad Pitt]] and [[Angelina Jolie]]) and [[TomKat]] ([[Tom Cruise]] and [[Katie Holmes]]).<ref name="news.BBC.co.uk" /> On Wednesday, 28 June 2017, ''[[The New York Times]]'' [[crossword]] included the quip, "How I wish [[Natalie Portman]] dated [[Jacques Cousteau]], so I could call them 'Portmanteau{{' "}}.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nytimes.com/crosswords/game/daily/2017/06/28|url-access=subscription|title=The Daily Crossword|website=[[The New York Times]]|date=28 June 2017}}</ref>

Holidays are another example, as in [[Thanksgivukkah]], a portmanteau neologism given to the convergence of the American holiday of [[Thanksgiving (United States)|Thanksgiving]] and the first day of the [[Jewish holiday]] of [[Hanukkah]] on Thursday, 28 November 2013.<ref>{{cite web|author-first1=Christine |author-last1=Byrne|url=https://www.buzzfeed.com/christinebyrne/thanksgivukkah|title=How To Celebrate Thanksgivukkah, The Best Holiday Of All Time|publisher=Buzzfeed|date=2 October 2013|access-date=10 October 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131009221928/http://www.buzzfeed.com/christinebyrne/thanksgivukkah|archive-date=9 October 2013}}</ref><ref>{{cite news|author-first1=Stu |author-last1=Bykofsky|url=http://www.philly.com/philly/news/20131011_Thanks_for_Thanukkah_.html|title=Thanks for Thanukkah!|website=Philly.com|date=22 October 2012|access-date=11 October 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131014085041/http://www.philly.com/philly/news/20131011_Thanks_for_Thanukkah_.html|archive-date=14 October 2013}}</ref> [[Chrismukkah]] is another pop-culture portmanteau neologism popularized by the TV drama ''[[The O.C.]]'', a merging of the holidays of Christianity's Christmas and Judaism's Hanukkah.
[[File:WISCOMPTON t-shirt 09-15-2012 089 (7997657762) (cropped).jpg|thumb|upright|This T-shirt combines the names of two places, [[Wisconsin]] and [[Compton, California]], to form "Wiscompton".]]
In the [[The Walt Disney Company|Disney]] film ''[[Big Hero 6 (film)|Big Hero 6]]'', the film is situated in a fictitious city called "San Fransokyo", which is a portmanteau of two real locations, [[San Francisco]] and [[Tokyo]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.gizmodo.com.au/2014/10/a-tour-of-san-fransokyo-the-hybrid-city-disney-built-for-big-hero-6/|title=A Tour Of 'San Fransokyo', The Hybrid City Disney Built For Big Hero 6|date=8 October 2014|website=Gizmodo Australia|language=en|access-date=5 June 2019|archive-date=5 June 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190605035900/https://www.gizmodo.com.au/2014/10/a-tour-of-san-fransokyo-the-hybrid-city-disney-built-for-big-hero-6/|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Other languages==
===Modern Hebrew===
[[Modern Hebrew]] abounds with blending. *{{langx|he|label=none|מחזמר}} ({{transliteration|he|makhazémer}} 'musical'), from {{langx|he|label=none|מחזה}} ({{transliteration|he|makhazé}} 'theatre play') and {{langx|he|label=none|זמר}} ({{transliteration|he|zémer}} 'singing [gerund]')

===Icelandic===
There is a tradition of [[linguistic purism in Icelandic]], and [[neologism]]s are frequently created from pre-existing words. For example, {{lang|is|tölva}} 'computer' is a portmanteau of {{lang|is|tala}} 'digit, number' and {{lang|is|völva}} 'oracle, seeress'.<ref>Kristján Árnason; Sigrún Helgadóttir (1991), "Terminology and Icelandic Language Policy", Behovet och nyttan av terminologiskt arbete på 90-talet, Nordterm 5, Nordterm-symposium, pp.&nbsp;7–21.</ref>

===Indonesian===
{{main|Indonesian language#Acronyms and portmanteau}}

In [[Indonesian language|Indonesian]], portmanteaus and [[acronym]]s are very common in both formal and informal usage.

A common use of a portmanteau in the Indonesian language is to refer to locations and areas of the country. For example, [[Jakarta metropolitan area|Jabodetabek]] is a portmanteau that refers to the '''Jakarta metropolitan area''' or '''Greater Jakarta''', which includes the regions of Jakarta, Bogor, Depok, Tangerang, Bekasi).

====Malaysian====
In the Malaysian national language of [[Bahasa Melayu]], the word '''{{Lang|ms|jadong}}''' was constructed out of three Malay words for evil ({{Lang|ms|jahat}}), stupid ({{Lang|ms|bodoh}}) and arrogant ({{Lang|ms|sombong}}) to be used on the worst kinds of community and religious leaders who mislead naive, submissive and powerless folk under their thrall.{{citation needed|date=January 2023}}

===Japanese===
{{main|Japanese abbreviated and contracted words}}

A very common type of portmanteau in Japanese forms one word from the beginnings of two others (that is, from two [[clipping (morphology)|back-clippings]]).<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.sljfaq.org/afaq/contractions.html|title=What are contracted words like rimokon?|website=Sljfaq.org|access-date=3 October 2013|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131004232800/http://www.sljfaq.org/afaq/contractions.html|archive-date=4 October 2013}}</ref> The portion of each input word retained is usually two [[on (Japanese prosody)|morae]], which is tantamount to one [[kanji]] in most words written in kanji.

The inputs to the process can be native words, [[Sino-Japanese vocabulary|Sino-Japanese words]], [[gairaigo]] (later borrowings), or combinations thereof. A Sino-Japanese example is the name {{nihongo krt||東大|''Tōdai''}} for the [[University of Tokyo]], in full {{nihongo krt||'''東'''京'''大'''学|'''Tō'''kyō '''dai'''gaku}}. With borrowings, typical results are words such as {{nihongo krt||パソコン|pasokon}}, meaning [[personal computer]] (PC), which despite being formed of English elements does not exist in English; it is a [[wasei-eigo|uniquely Japanese]] contraction of the English {{nihongo|''personal computer''|'''パ'''ー'''ソ'''ナル'''・コン'''ピュータ|'''pāso'''naru '''kon'''pyūta}}. Another example, {{nihongo|''[[Pokémon]]''|ポケモン}}, is a contracted form of the English words {{nihongo|''pocket''|'''ポケ'''ット|'''poke'''tto}} and {{nihongo|''monsters''|'''モン'''スター|'''mon'''sutā}}.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.sfu.ca/gradlings/SFUWPL/ICEAL2/Rosen_E.pdf|title=Japanese loanword accentuation: epenthesis and foot form interacting through edge-interior alignment∗|first=Eric|last=Rosen|work=University of British Columbia|access-date=25 November 2010|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110623131040/http://www.sfu.ca/gradlings/SFUWPL/ICEAL2/Rosen_E.pdf|archive-date=23 June 2011}}</ref> A famous example of a blend with mixed sources is {{nihongo|''[[karaoke]]''|カラオケ|karaoke}}, blending the Japanese word for {{nihongo|''empty''|空|'''kara'''}} and the Greek word {{nihongo|''orchestra''|オーケストラ|'''ōke'''sutora}}. The Japanese fad of egg-shaped keychain pet toys from the 1990s, ''[[Tamagotchi]]'', is a portmanteau combining the two Japanese words {{Nihongo3|'egg'|たまご|tamago}}, and {{Nihongo3|'watch'|ウオッチ|uotchi}}. The portmanteau can also be seen as a combination of {{Nihongo3|'egg'|たまご|tamago}}, and {{Nihongo3|'friend'|友だち|tomodachi}}.

Some [[Title (publishing)|titles]] also are portmanteaus, such as ''[[Hetalia]]'' ({{lang|ja|ヘタリア}}). It came from {{Transliteration|ja|'''He''ta'''''re|italic=no}} ({{Lang|ja|ヘタレ}}, 'idiot') and {{Transliteration|ja|I'''''ta''lia'''|italic=no}} ({{Lang|ja|イタリア}}, 'Italy'). Another example is ''[[Servamp]]'', which came from the English words {{Nihongo|'''Ser''va'''''nt|サーヴァント}} and {{Nihongo|'''''Va''mp'''ire|ヴァンパイア}}.

===Spanish===
{{More citations needed section|date=June 2020}}
Although traditionally uncommon in Spanish, portmanteaus are increasingly finding their way into the language, mainly for marketing and commercial purposes. Examples in [[Mexican Spanish]] include {{lang|es-mx|cafebrería}} from combining {{lang|es|cafetería}} 'coffee shop' and {{lang|es|librería}} 'bookstore', or {{lang|es|teletón}} '[[telethon]]' from combining {{lang|es|televisión}} and {{lang|es|maratón}}. Portmanteaus are also frequently used to make commercial brands, such as "chocolleta" from "chocolate" + "galleta". They are also often used to create business company names, especially for small, family-owned businesses, where owners' names are combined to create a unique name (such as Rocar, from "Roberto" + "Carlos", or Mafer, from "María" + "Fernanda"). These usages help to create distinguishable trademarks. It is a common occurrence for people with two names to combine them into a single nickname, like Juanca for Juan Carlos, Or Marilú for María de Lourdes.

Other examples:
* {{Lang|es|Cantautor}} 'singer-songwriter', from {{Lang|es|cantante}} 'singer' and {{Lang|es|autor}} 'songwriter'.<ref>{{Cite web |title=cantautor, ra |url=https://dle.rae.es/cantautor |access-date=2024-12-14 |website=Diccionario de la lengua española |language=es |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200229130634/https://dle.rae.es/cantautor |archive-date=29 February 2020}}</ref>
* {{Lang|es|[[Mechatronics|Mecatrónica]]}}{{Citation needed|date=January 2023}} and {{Lang|es|[[:es:Ofimática|Ofimática]]}}, two [[neologism]]s that are blends of {{Lang|es|mecánica}} 'mechanical' with {{Lang|es|electrónica}} 'electronics', and {{Lang|es|oficina}} 'office' with {{Lang|es|informática}} '[[Computer science|informatics]]' respectively.
* {{Lang|es|[[Espanglish]]}}, interlanguage that combines words from both Spanish ({{Lang|es|Español}}) and English.
* {{Lang|es|[[Metrobus (disambiguation)|Metrobús]]}}, blend of {{Lang|es|metro}} 'subway' and {{Lang|es|autobús}}.
* {{Lang|es|[[Autopista (disambiguation)|Autopista]]}}, blend of {{Lang|es|automóvil}} 'car' and {{Lang|es|pista}} 'road, tracks'.
* Company names and brands with portmanteaus are common in Spanish. Some examples of Spanish portmanteaus for Mexican companies include: The Mexican flag carrier [[Aeroméxico]], (Aerovías de México), Banorte (Bank and North), [[Cemex]] (Cement and Mexico), [[Jumex]] (Jugos Mexicanos or Mexican Juice), [[Mabe (company)|Mabe]] (from founders Egon MAbardi and Francisco BErrondo), [[Pemex]] (Petróleos Mexicanos or Mexican Oil), [[Softtek]] (portmanteau and stylization of Software and technology), and [[Telmex]] (Teléfonos de Mexico). [[Gamesa]] (Galletera Mexicana, S.A. or Mexican Biscuit Company, Inc.) and [[Grupo Famsa|Famsa]] (fabricantes Muebleros, S.A.) are examples of portmanteaus of four words, including the "S.A." (Sociedad Anónima).
* Many more portmanteaus in Spanish come from [[Anglicism]]s, which are words borrowed from English, like {{Lang|es|módem}}, {{Lang|es|transistor}}, {{Lang|es|códec}}, {{Lang|es|email}}, {{Lang|es|internet}}, and {{Lang|es|emoticon.}}

A somewhat popular example in Spain is the word {{lang|es|gallifante}},<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.rtve.es/alacarta/videos/para-todos-la-2/gallifantes/1030400//|title=Gallifantes – RTVE.es|date=25 February 2011|website=Rtve.es|access-date=6 February 2018|archive-date=7 February 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180207005306/http://www.rtve.es/alacarta/videos/para-todos-la-2/gallifantes/1030400//|url-status=live}}</ref> a portmanteau of {{lang|es|gallo y elefante}} 'cockerel and elephant'. It was the prize on the Spanish version of the children TV show ''Child's Play'' ({{langx|es|[[:es:Juego de niños (programa de televisión)|Juego de niños]]}}) that ran on the public television channel {{Lang|es|[[La 1 (Spanish TV channel)|La 1]]|italic=no}} of {{Lang|es|[[Televisión Española]]|italic=no}} (TVE) from 1988 to 1992.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://elpais.com/diario/1988/06/04/radiotv/581378404_850215.html|title=Jugar bien vale un 'gallifante'|date=4 June 1988|newspaper=El País|access-date=6 February 2018|archive-date=6 February 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180206190347/https://elpais.com/diario/1988/06/04/radiotv/581378404_850215.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

==Portmanteau morph==
In [[word formation]], a blend is a word formed by fusing material from two or more words. A ''portmanteau morph'' is a single [[Allomorph|morph]] analyzed as expressing two or more [[morpheme]]s at once.<ref name="What is a portmanteau morph">{{cite web|url=http://www.sil.org/Linguistics/GlossaryOfLinguisticTerms/WhatIsAPortmanteauMorph.htm|title=What is a portmanteau morph?|work=LinguaLinks Library|year=2003|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080619140711/http://www.sil.org/linguistics/glossaryoflinguisticterms/WhatIsAPortmanteauMorph.htm|archive-date=19 June 2008|df=dmy-all}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|last=Thomas|first=David|year=1983|title=An invitation to grammar|location=Bangkok|series=Summer Institute of Linguistics|publisher=Mahidol University|page=9}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|last=Crystal|first=David|year=1985|title=A dictionary of linguistics and phonetics|edition=2nd|location=New York|publisher=Basil Blackwell|page=237}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|last1=Hartmann|first1=R.R.K.|last2=Stork|first2=F.C.|year=1972|title=Dictionary of language and linguistics|location=London|publisher=Applied Science|page=180}}</ref> 

For example, in the Latin word {{langx|la|label=none|animalis}} ("of an animal"), the ending {{langx|la|label=none|-is}} marks both [[genitive case]] and [[grammatical number|singular number]]. In English, two separate words are used: '''''of an''' animal''. Other examples include {{langx|fr|link=no|*à le}}{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}{{wikt-lang|fr|au}} {{IPA|fr|o|}} ("to the") and {{langx|fr|label=none|*de le}}{{resize|{{space|2}}⇒{{space|2}}}}{{wikt-lang|fr|du}} {{IPA|fr|dy|}} ("of the").<ref name="What is a portmanteau morph"/>
