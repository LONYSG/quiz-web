'''Cyworld''' ({{korean|hangul=싸이월드}}) is a South Korean [[social network service]]. Cyworld was originally part of SK communication, and became an independent company in 2014.<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.zdnet.co.kr/news/news_view.asp?artice_id=20140408103154 |script-title=ko:싸이월드 완전 독립…네이트도 개편 |access-date=2018-03-12 |archive-date=2018-03-12 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180312205216/http://www.zdnet.co.kr/news/news_view.asp?artice_id=20140408103154 |url-status=live |date=8 April 2014 }}</ref> Members cultivate relationships by forming ''Ilchon'' ({{korean|hangul=일촌}}, [[Hanja]]: <span lang="KO">一寸</span>) or "friendships" with each other through their ''minihompy''. [[Avatar (computing)|Avatar]]s and "mini-rooms"<ref name="cnn" /> (small, decoratable, apartment-like spaces in an [[isometric projection]])<ref name="businessweek" /> are features of the service, which can make for a [[The Sims|Sims]]-like experience.<ref name="wired">{{cite magazine |last=Evans |first=Jonny |title=Koreans Find Secret Cybersauce |date=August 8, 2005 |magazine=Wired |url=https://www.wired.com/2005/08/koreans-find-secret-cybersauce/}}</ref>

The "Cy" in Cyworld can mean "cyber", but is also a [[pun]] on the Korean word for "relationship" ({{korean|hangul=사이}} 'between').<ref name="cnn"/>

Cyworld is a rough equivalent to [[Myspace]] of the [[United States]],<ref name="MySpace">{{cite news|date=March 7, 2007|title=Cyworld fever cools|newspaper=[[The Korea Times]]|author=Kim Tae-gyu|url=http://www.international.ucla.edu/cnes/article/65289|access-date=January 5, 2016|archive-date=March 4, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304195420/http://www.international.ucla.edu/cnes/article/65289|url-status=live}}</ref> with the main difference being that revenue is generated through the sale of ''dotori'' ({{korean|hangul=도토리|}}), or acorns,<ref name="sea">[http://www.seattlepi.com/business/411768_virtual03.html Tapping into growing market for virtual goods] seattlepi.com, November 2, 2009 9:56 p.m. PT</ref> which can be used to purchase [[virtual goods]],<ref name="businessweek">[http://www.businessweek.com/magazine/content/05_39/b3952405.htm E-Society: My World Is Cyworld] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20051231204826/http://www.businessweek.com/magazine/content/05_39/b3952405.htm |date=2005-12-31 }} businessweek.com, Sep 26, 2005</ref> such as background music, pixelated furniture, and virtual appliances.<ref name="cnn">[https://money.cnn.com/2006/07/27/technology/cyworld0727.biz2/index.htm Cyworld ready to attack MySpace] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221028092706/https://money.cnn.com/2006/07/27/technology/cyworld0727.biz2/index.htm |date=2022-10-28 }} money.cnn.com, July 27, 2006: 11:35 AM EDT</ref>

Cyworld also has operations in [[China]] and [[Vietnam]].<ref name="pullout">[http://www.telecomskorea.com/business-8268.html Korean SNS Cyworld To Withdraw From U.S. and Taiwan] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221028092704/http://www.telecomskorea.com/business-8268.html |date=2022-10-28 }} telecomskorea.com,  2009/11/09</ref>

==History==
[[Image:Seoul-Cyworld control room.jpg|thumb|Cyworld control room in Seoul, Korea. Operation of the site is monitored here by the staff of SK Communications.]]

===Overview===
Cyworld launched in 1999<ref name=suspectsource>[http://www.ica-it.org/index.php?option=com_docman&task=doc_download&gid=257&Itemid=56 Evolution of Social Network Service into Media -Cyworld Case- October 2008] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150924032412/http://www.ica-it.org/index.php?option=com_docman&task=doc_download&gid=257&Itemid=56 |date=2015-09-24 }} Posted on '''ica-it.org''', A SK Communications slide-show presentation from 42nd International Council for Information Technology in Government Administration Conference in Korea in 2008</ref> and was purchased by  [[SK Communications]] in 2003.<ref name="cnn"/> It became one of the first<ref name="sea"/> companies to profit from the sale of [[virtual goods]].<ref name="businessweek"/>

Cyworld was wildly popular in its home market, with 2005 claims that nearly every South Korean in their twenties<ref>{{cite news |title=Koreans cybertrip to a tailor-made world |url=https://www.theage.com.au/national/koreans-cybertrip-to-a-tailor-made-world-20050509-gdl9tt.html |date=2005-05-09 |newspaper=[[The Age]] |access-date=2006-01-03 |archive-date=2023-08-10 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230810225727/https://www.theage.com.au/national/koreans-cybertrip-to-a-tailor-made-world-20050509-gdl9tt.html |url-status=live }}</ref>
and 25 percent of the South Korean population<ref name="miniroom">{{cite web |title=Will Cyworld Stop MySpace Juggernaut? |url=http://gigaom.com/2006/04/16/will-cyworld-stop-myspace-juggernaut/ |date=2006-04-16 |publisher=GigaOM |access-date=2006-04-20 |archive-date=2012-09-23 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120923145833/http://gigaom.com/2006/04/16/will-cyworld-stop-myspace-juggernaut/ |url-status=dead }}</ref> were users. By 2006 its domestic user base numbered 19 million,<ref name=ft3>[https://www.ft.com/content/59ab33da-64c4-11db-90fd-0000779e2340 The high priestess of internet friendship] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110207094358/http://www.ft.com/cms/s/2/59ab33da-64c4-11db-90fd-0000779e2340.html#ixzz17ZwBRhHq |date=2011-02-07 }} ft.com, October 27, 2006</ref> but this dropped to 18 million by 2008.<ref name=krtms>[https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/biz/2010/02/123_20284.html SK Communications Decides to Close European Unit]  koreatimes.co.kr, 03-07-2008</ref>

Cyworld's reception in some overseas markets did not prove as enthusiastic, and by 2010 Cyworld had ended its operations in Germany, Japan, and the United States.<ref name="pullout"/> As of 2009, it continues to provide service to the Chinese and Vietnamese markets where it has subscriber bases of seven million and 450,000, respectively.<ref name="pullout"/>

===Initial stages===
The idea for Cyworld started in August 1999 by [[KAIST]] student organization the 'EC club', a club that took on online business projects. The club members got the idea to create a social networking website while discussing topics for a research project. Though most club members abandoned the project after graduation, Dong-Hyung Lee continued to pursue the project, taking on the role of CEO from December 1999.<ref name="Article 1">{{cite news|title=Interview with the creator of Cyworld: The dream of a countryside boy|url=http://www.chosun.com/economy/news/200407/200407220108.html|access-date=21 November 2012|date=22 July 2004|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110093748/http://www.chosun.com/economy/news/200407/200407220108.html|archive-date=10 January 2014|url-status=dead}}</ref>

The word 'cy' is a Korean word meaning 'between people', underlining the networking aspect of the website and connoting a close relation between the website users. However, most misinterpret 'cy' as an abbreviation for 'cyber' due to its [[Fortis (linguistics)|fortis]]; 'sai' corresponds to a more accurate pronunciation of a Korean word for 'between.'<ref name="Article 1" />   The original nature of the term 'cy' demonstrates Dong-Hyung Lee's vision for the site. He wanted to create an Internet community that allowed people to form close relationships, rather than a community where people merely sought information for business prospects.

Cyworld in its early stages was quite different from what it is today. It was a website that showed a list of members from the same hometown or school.<ref name="Article 1" />  The address book for each member was updated automatically according to the personal information its members provided.<ref name="Article 4">{{cite news|title=A social networking site begins its services|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=001&aid=0004459717|access-date=21 November 2012|date=31 August 1999|archive-date=25 February 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225035250/https://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=001&aid=0004459717|url-status=live}}</ref>  It was not a place where people could express themselves, but rather a website that allowed people to gain means of contact, so that members could meet offline. Cyworld at its early stages was far from successful, once running a deficit of 1.5 billion won.<ref name="Article 1" />

===Minihomepy===
In the summer of 2002, Cyworld launched the "minihomepy" project, a last chance to turn things around before the business had to shut down. CEO Dong-Hyung Lee put contents provider Ram Lee in charge of the project. It was an instant success.<ref name="Article 2">{{cite news|title=Without Ram Lee, there would be no 'cyjil'|url=http://www.segye.com/Articles/News/Culture/Article.asp?aid=20040825000450|access-date=21 November 2012|date=26 August 2004|archive-date=10 August 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230810225107/http://www.segye.com/newsView/20040825000450|url-status=live}}</ref>

Offering many methods of expressing oneself, the minihomepy had features such as a main picture, history, user profile, photo story, story room, background music, photo album, diary, bulletin board, video clips, and decorating links. Another component of the minihomepy was the miniroom, a decorative online room furnished with virtual furniture.

One of the main reasons for minihomepy's success was people's dissatisfaction with the "individual homepages" that were prevalent in Korea at the time.<ref name="Article 2" />   While individual homepages were initially widely popular because they enabled people to express themselves online, the programming knowledge (e.g. HTML, FTP) required to create an individual homepage was too daunting for most people. Although knowledge barrier was partially resolved through homepage programming services such as High Home({{Korean|hangul=하이홈|labels=no}}), there still remained a significant issue: the means of communication between individual homepage users was absent.<ref name="Article 2" />  Individual homepages were like "stranded islands" in the vast sea called the Internet.<ref name="Article 2" />  Minihomepy addressed the desire for interpersonal communication. Minihomepies were easy to create and maintain. Minihomepies had components like visitor logs and comments, which provided a means of contact, while features such as the diary and bulletin boards allowed for individual expression; it was amicably received by the public. Members had to become Ilchons in order to gain access to each other's minihomepies.

The minihomepy service was officially launched on 9 September 2002.<ref name="Article 5">{{cite news|title=Competitors Cyworld and Damoim form an alliance|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=101&oid=015&aid=0000546186|access-date=21 November 2012|date=6 September 2002|archive-date=27 February 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210227024955/https://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=101&oid=015&aid=0000546186|url-status=live}}</ref> Cyworld uses its own form of cybermoney, called ''dotori'' (acorns). The items for decorating the minihomepies and minirooms could be bought with dotoris, and people voluntarily spent money on dotori as their minihomepy decoration was perceived as another expression of themselves.<ref name="Dissertation 2">{{cite web|last=Kim|first=Nanha|title=A study of the peeping desire in Korean-type Blog (focused on Cyworld Minihompy) written by Nanha Kim of The Graduate School of Ewha University|url=http://yulprm1.yonsei.ac.kr/primo_library/libweb/action/search.do?dscnt=1&scp.scps=scope%3A%28YONSEI%29%2Cprimo_central_multiple_fe&frbg=&tab=remote1&dstmp=1353508448715&srt=rank&ct=search&mode=Basic&dum=true&indx=1&tb=t&vl(freeText0)=peeping+cyworld&fn=search&vid=yul&fromLogin=true&fromLogin=true|archive-url=https://archive.today/20121219220216/http://yulprm1.yonsei.ac.kr/primo_library/libweb/action/search.do?dscnt=1&scp.scps=scope:(YONSEI),primo_central_multiple_fe&frbg=&tab=remote1&dstmp=1353508448715&srt=rank&ct=search&mode=Basic&dum=true&indx=1&tb=t&vl(freeText0)=peeping+cyworld&fn=search&vid=yul&fromLogin=true&fromLogin=true|url-status=dead|archive-date=19 December 2012|publisher=The Graduate School of Ewha University|access-date=21 November 2012}}</ref>

Cyworld gained further success when in November 2002, its competitor [[Freechal]] (another networking site for online communities) decided to charge its users 3,000 won per month. Freechal also announced plans to shut down communities operated by those who failed to pay the fee. This "pay-or-shut-down" policy prompted a horde of members to cancel their subscriptions and move to other free online community hosts, including Cyworld.<ref name="Article 7">{{cite news|title=Cyworld set to pass Freechal in community users|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=108&oid=044&aid=0000037866|access-date=21 November 2012|newspaper=[[The Korea Herald]]|date=29 October 2003|archive-date=10 August 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230810215105/https://n.news.naver.com/mnews/article/044/0000037866?sid=108|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Merger with SK Telecom===
[[File:Cyworld market growth in the golden years.jpg|thumb|Graph showing Cyworld's dramatically increasing subscriber base]]

In August 2003, Cyworld merged with [[SK Telecom]], a Korean communication company and owner of the domain [[Nate (web portal)|Nate]].<ref name="Article 6">{{cite news|title=SK Communications merges Cyworld|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=029&aid=0000029670|access-date=21 November 2012|date=3 June 2003|archive-date=25 February 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225025915/https://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=029&aid=0000029670|url-status=live}}</ref>  The rapidly increasing number of subscribers was getting too difficult for Cyworld to manage on its own, and SK promised resources for further growth in the market. Although it was incorporated into the SK community department, Cyworld retained  its brand name, service, and independence.<ref name="Article 6" />  The CEO of Cyworld Dong-Hyung Lee was appointed as the general manager of the Cyworld department, and he moved on to be the CEO of Cyworld Japan in May 2005 until he left the company in December 2008 to pursue other business adventures.<ref name="Article 3">{{cite news|title=Former Creator of Cyworld comes back with location-based SNS|url=http://www.dt.co.kr/contents.html?article_no=2009021702010531699002|access-date=21 November 2012|date=17 February 2009|archive-date=10 August 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230810225730/http://www.dt.co.kr/contents.html?article_no=2009021702010531699002|url-status=live}}</ref>

The merger immediately led to significant market success. By the end of 2003, Cyworld gave rise to the terms "cyholic" (a Cyworld addict) and "cyjil" (''jil'' is a Korean pun for action, so cyjil means doing Cyworld-related activities).<ref name="Article 8">{{cite news|title=Cyworld becomes wildly popular giving rise to the term "cyholic"|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=031&aid=0000034064|access-date=21 November 2012|date=12 December 2003|archive-date=27 February 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210227014049/https://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=031&aid=0000034064|url-status=live}}</ref>  Cyworld became a sensation among the Korean public, more than tripling in monthly visitors (from 2 million to 7 million) from early 2003 to November 2003.<ref name="Article 8" />

Cyworld's success grew even further when it began to collaborate with [[NateOn|Nate-on]], the largest online messenger service in Korea and also owned by SK. The collaboration was established in 2005, allowing Cyworld and Nate-on members to use both services simultaneously by logging into just one site.<ref name="Article 16">{{cite news|title=Cyworld and Nate-on launch linkage service|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=015&aid=0000825850|access-date=21 November 2012|date=10 August 2005|archive-date=26 February 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210226182922/https://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=105&oid=015&aid=0000825850|url-status=live}}</ref>

In early 2007, Cyworld topped 20 million members for its services (roughly half the population of South Korea) giving rise to the term 'sa-chon era', meaning any two Cyworld members are likely to be Cyworld ilchons through fewer than four connections.<ref name="Article 11">{{cite news|title=Cyworld tops 20 million users|url=http://www.edaily.co.kr/news/NewsRead.edy?SCD=DB41&newsid=01682646583027896&DCD=A10105&OutLnkChk=Y|access-date=21 November 2012|date=6 February 2007|archive-date=11 May 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130511203848/http://www.edaily.co.kr/news/NewsRead.edy?SCD=DB41&newsid=01682646583027896&DCD=A10105&OutLnkChk=Y|url-status=dead}}</ref> In 2009, Cyworld unified its domain with SK's Nate. This move was meant for "user convenience", as the two domains had over 17 million overlapping members.<ref name="Article 12">{{cite news|title=Nate bulks up, combining main page with Cyworld|url=http://news.inews24.com/php/news_view.php?g_serial=438589&g_menu=020300|access-date=21 November 2012|date=27 August 2009|archive-date=2 May 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130502210323/http://news.inews24.com/php/news_view.php?g_serial=438589&g_menu=020300|url-status=live}}</ref>  No significant change was made to the site after the union. As of 2011, Cyworld had over 25 million members.<ref name="Article 13">{{cite news|last=Yoon|first=Ja-young|title=Personal info of 35 mil. Nate, Cyworld users feared leaked|url=https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/biz/2011/07/123_91802.html|access-date=21 November 2012|newspaper=[[The Korea Times]]|date=12 July 2011|archive-date=10 January 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110184132/http://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/biz/2011/07/123_91802.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

===2011 data leakage and decline===
As [[Facebook]] began its services in Korea in 2009, Cyworld slowly began to lose its status as the only popular [[Social networking service|SNS]] service in Korea. According to some research, Facebook's simpler design and function were more appealing and easier to use than Cyworld.<ref name="Article 14">{{cite news|title=Is homegrown Cyworld giving in to Facebook?|url=https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/biz/2011/10/123_96267.html|access-date=21 November 2012|newspaper=[[The Korea Times]]|date=12 October 2011|archive-date=8 November 2011|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111108050518/http://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/biz/2011/10/123_96267.html|url-status=live}}</ref>  As Cyworld did not offer global services, it was an undesirable service for people who wanted to contact friends around the world.<ref name="Article 14" />  The introduction of [[smartphones]] to the market was also a key factor in Cyworld's decline, as both [[Facebook]] and [[Twitter]] offered stronger interconnectivity with mobile platforms.<ref name="Article 15">{{cite news|title=Mobile Internet sidelines Cyworld|url=https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/tech/2012/08/133_117218.html|access-date=21 November 2012|newspaper=[[The Korea Times]]|date=12 August 2012|archive-date=10 January 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110184953/http://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/tech/2012/08/133_117218.html|url-status=live}}</ref>  Kakao Story, an SMS service exclusive for smartphone users, had an advantage over its rivals due to its connection with mobile service [[KakaoTalk]]({{Korean|hangul=카카오톡|labels=no}}), which has over 55 million members.<ref name="Article 15" /><ref>{{cite news|title=KakaoTalk Daily Traffic Hits 3 Billion|url=http://english.chosun.com/site/data/html_dir/2012/07/27/2012072701392.html|newspaper=[[The Chosun Ilbo]]|access-date=January 10, 2014|archive-date=January 10, 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110212059/http://english.chosun.com/site/data/html_dir/2012/07/27/2012072701392.html|url-status=live}}</ref>  Cyworld's inability to keep up with the trend made it fall behind in the market.

Cyworld's declining market share was further aggravated by a 2011 data leakage. In July 2011, Cyworld/Nate was hacked into by criminals who stole the personal information of more than 35 million users.<ref name="Article 13" />  (Nate had 33 million users and Cyworld had 25 million, and combined, they had about 35 million members.) The information for almost all of the Cyworld/Nate members, and by extension about 70 percent of the Korean population,<ref name="Article 17">{{cite news|title=Cyworld/Nate face more legal woes|url=http://khnews.kheraldm.com/view.php?ud=20110805000558&md=20120317141332_AK|access-date=21 November 2012|newspaper=[[The Korea Herald]]|date=5 August 2011|archive-date=10 January 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110182658/http://khnews.kheraldm.com/view.php?ud=20110805000558&md=20120317141332_AK|url-status=live}}</ref> was compromised.<ref name="Article 13" /> The hackers accessed Cyworld's system by using an Internet protocol address based in China. Because Cyworld/Nate requires its members to submit personal information for membership, the 2011 data leakage was quite detrimental as the hackers had the members' [[resident registration numbers]], phone numbers, and email addresses. Though SK communications insisted that the resident registration numbers and passwords were encrypted and are not likely to be abused even in the hands of the hackers, nobody gave the company the benefit of the doubt.<ref name="Article 18">{{cite news|title=Nate, Cyworld users pursue class action amid identity theft fears|url=https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/tech/2011/08/133_92080.html|access-date=21 November 2012|newspaper=[[The Korea Times]]|date=13 August 2011|archive-date=16 December 2011|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111216164447/http://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/tech/2011/08/133_92080.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

SK Telecom took measures trying to minimize the harm, but it was soon flooded with lawsuits demanding compensation for the leakage. The leakage yielded costly lawsuits for the company, in a country with virtually no precedent in class-action lawsuits.<ref name="Article 17" />  The public's discontent with the data leak led straight to the plummet of stock prices. The company's reputation was tarnished and information-sensitive Koreans moved to other [[Social networking service|SNS]] service after the leakage. This phenomenon was directly reflected in the plunging page views for Cyworld/Nate. Between March 2011 and April 2012, the monthly UV (Unique Visitors) and PV (Page View) for Cyworld dropped from 21.5 million and 7.5 billion to 16.5 million and 1.7 billion, respectively.<ref name="Article 20">{{cite news|title=Cyworld's PV declines to one fourth of the previous year|url=http://www.ddaily.co.kr/news/news_view.php?uid=89965|access-date=21 November 2012|date=24 April 2012|archive-date=10 September 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130910071143/http://www.ddaily.co.kr/news/news_view.php?uid=89965|url-status=live}}</ref>  Statistics show that the fall in UV and PV became more dramatic after the July 2011 data leakage.<ref name="Article 20" />

In September 2012, [[SK Telecom]] announced its decision to allow membership for Cyworld without obligating members to register resident registration numbers and real names. A minimum amount of personal information, such as email address and nationality, would be asked of future members.<ref name="Article 23">{{cite news|title=Cyworld, T World and 8 other services allow membership with no resident registration number and real name |url=http://www.hankyung.com/news/app/newsview.php?aid=201209255234g |access-date=21 November 2012 |newspaper=[[The Korea Economic Daily]] |date=25 September 2012 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110192128/http://www.hankyung.com/news/app/newsview.php?aid=201209255234g |archive-date=10 January 2014 }}</ref>

Cyworld brought an end to its minihompy service on 31 September 2015 and had announced plans to change over to a new platform named Cyhome.<ref>{{cite web |date=30 September 2015 |script-title=ko:싸이월드 미니홈피, '싸이홈'으로 개편…'백업하세요' |url=http://news.mk.co.kr/newsRead.php?year=2015&no=936092 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230810215102/https://www.mk.co.kr/news/it/6979601 |archive-date=10 August 2023 |access-date=12 March 2018}}</ref>

===Engagements in foreign markets===
After its domestic success Cyworld began to venture into foreign markets. In 2005, it started services in China and subsequently entered the Japan and Vietnam markets.<ref name="Article 22">{{cite news|title=SK Communications 'Cyworld' withdraws from US and Taiwan market|url=http://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=101&oid=003&aid=0002944864|access-date=21 November 2012|date=6 November 2009|archive-date=24 July 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200724191831/https://news.naver.com/main/read.nhn?mode=LSD&mid=sec&sid1=101&oid=003&aid=0002944864|url-status=live}}</ref> Cyworld lost out to Japan's [[Mixi]] and exited the Japanese market in August 2008.<ref name="Article 22" /> As of 2009, its operations in China and Vietnam were more optimistic; it had seven million and 450,000 members, respectively.<ref name="Article 22" />

Cyworld entered the US market in 2006, believing that many US teenagers would use multiple [[social network]]s<ref name=ft1>[https://www.ft.com/content/5f33a28e-906f-11db-a4b9-0000779e2340 Social network sites battle to befriend US teenagers] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140703193937/http://www.ft.com/cms/s/0/5f33a28e-906f-11db-a4b9-0000779e2340.html#ixzz17Zl9jQLE |date=2014-07-03 }} ft.com, December 20, 2006</ref> and seeking early access to a then-quickly growing market.<ref name=ft2>[https://www.ft.com/content/eacfbf3c-3938-11db-a21d-0000779e2340 Korean site tackles might of MySpace] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110228041101/http://www.ft.com/cms/s/2/eacfbf3c-3938-11db-a21d-0000779e2340.html#ixzz17ZmJDsxa |date=2011-02-28 }} ft.com, August 31, 2006</ref> However, it lost market share to [[Facebook]] and exited the US market in February 2010.<ref name="pullout"/>

In 2006 Cyworld entered a [[joint venture]] with a German [[Deutsche Telekom]] subsidiary, [[T-Online]],<ref name=ft2/> and launched its European version a year later.<ref>[https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/news/biz/2009/11/123_12131.html Cyworld Opens German Site Last Week]  koreatimes.co.kr, 10-18-2007</ref> Strong competition from site like [[StudiVZ]] and [[Skyrock (social network site)|Skyrock]], as well as a [[market saturation|saturated]] market made for dismal future prospects, and by 2008 Cyworld had closed all operations.<ref name=krtms/>

==Website==

===Ilchon===
Ilchon is originally a Korean word that denotes very close familial relations, such as between a parent and a child. Becoming ilchon is how users in Cyworld begin their interaction. The user sends an ilchon request for another user to receive. If the request is accepted, the ilchons can see the content of each other's minihomepy that are not made available to those who are not ilchons, such as the diary and photo sections. One can also assign an ''interest ilchon'' to some of their friends. If interest ilchon is assigned, the user is notified when the interest ilchon's homepage is updated. Users can also see their friends' online statuses. Until Twitter and Facebook came out, the Il-Chon system was one of the most popular online social networking tools in Korea.

===Dotori===
Cyworld uses its own virtual currency called ''dotori'' ({{korean|hangul=도토리|}}), or acorns.<ref name="businessweek"/> Dong-Hyung Lee coined the term in 2002.<ref name="Article 1" /> One ''dotori'' costs 100 [[South Korean won|won]], and they are used to purchase [[virtual goods]].<ref name="businessweek"/> Prices vary from about 2 acorns for a wall painting or 6 acorns for a song that plays in your miniroom to 40 acorns for a homepage background for your for that last for a year.{{Citation needed|date=November 2009}} (Most items purchased with dotori have a time limit.) Dotori can be given to ilchons, and is often given to friends as birthday presents.

As the currency unit for Cyworld, dotori is the main source of revenue for the company. In 2006, 80% of Cyworld's Korean income was generated from the sale of virtual goods.<ref name=ft1/> CLINK<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://clinkico.cyworld.com/en/ |title=CLINK - Cyworld blockchain service |access-date=2019-02-05 |archive-date=2020-07-16 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200716034256/http://clinkico.cyworld.com/en/ |url-status=live }}</ref> service has been launched in 2018<ref>{{Cite web|url = http://www.hankookilbo.com/News/Read/201808211542026840|script-title=ko:싸이월드, 암호화폐 '클링'으로 제2 도토리 신화 쓴다|date = 21 August 2018|access-date = 5 February 2019|archive-date = 7 February 2019|archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20190207020210/http://www.hankookilbo.com/News/Read/201808211542026840|url-status = live}}</ref> for further development of digital currency ecosystem.

CLINK has replaced ''dotori'' as ''podo'' during its service. CLINK has also shown ''coconut'' as currency.<ref>{{Cite web |author1=싸이월드 |work=싸이홈 &#124; BY 싸이월드 |url=https://cy.cyworld.com/home/ALG0674F/post/5B7691486C41491AA85EA001 |script-title=ko:클링(Clink)이 뭐야? - 싸이월드 싸이홈 |access-date=2019-02-06 |archive-date=2019-04-25 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190425060639/https://cy.cyworld.com/home/ALG0674F/post/5B7691486C41491AA85EA001 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Minihomepy===
Minihomepy is essentially a cyberspace allotted to each Cyworld member. Using dotori, users can decorate their minihomepies as they see fit. Minihomepy features include the main background, history, profile, photo story, story room, background music, photo album, diary, and bulletin board, allowing for self-expression. The miniroom is a virtual room with a minime (avatar), a self-representational space that the users can decorate with virtual "items" purchased with their dotori. Dotori can be used to buy new background skins, miniroom interiors, background music, banners, and fonts. There are also other special features, such as ''ilchon padotagi'', which are links for surfing the minihompies of fellow ilchons, and ''random padotagi'', links for surfing the minihompies of strangers.

===Club===
Clubs are online communities for Cyworld minihomepy users. Like in the minihomepy, dotori can be used to decorate the clubs. It is not very different from other online clubs in other websites.

===Blog===
The Cyworld blog is not unlike blogs in other websites. Dotori is used for decorating users' blogs. Cyworld blogs permit anyone to access its contents, but the user can make it function as another version of minihomepy and allow it only viewable by ilchons. Wizets, photo logs, and bulletin boards are features of the blog. Cyworld blogs also link with NateOn.

===NateOn===
Cyworld collaborates with [[NateOn]] ({{Korean|hangul=네이트온|labels=no}}), a widely used instant messenger service in Korea. If Cyworld users buy fonts with dotori in Cyworld, they can use those fonts with NateOn, too.

==Impact==
[[File:Cyworld Famous people usage.jpg|thumb|left|400x600px|Minihomepies of some public figures who use Cyworld]]
Cyworld has exerted significant influence on Korea's Internet culture. The popular use of the term "cyholic" is indicative of this. Self-expression is a desire that Cyworld can satisfy; Cyworld has provided a cyber space where users can readily express their feelings to ilchons.<ref name="Dissertation 1">{{cite web|title=How consumers perceive, interpret, and Use the Cyworld|url=http://kiss.kstudy.com/Search/Result_View.asp?image_key=30100916|publisher=CAU|access-date=21 November 2012|archive-date=2 March 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130302050816/http://kiss.kstudy.com/Search/Result_View.asp?image_key=30100916|url-status=dead}}</ref>  It also allows the viewer of another person's minihomepy to get satisfaction from learning about the other person's life;<ref name="Dissertation 2" /> however, uploaded materials and diaries tend to be somewhat dissembling, if not pretentious, for the sake of gaining sympathy from other users.<ref name="Dissertation 1" />  Minihompies succeeded in functioning as social platforms through which users could express their personal traits and private thoughts. Another unique feature of Cyworld is the tracker displayed on the upper left-hand side of the minihomepy. It shows two numbers, called ''today'' (the number of visitors to the minihomepy on that day) and ''total'' (the total number of visitors to the minihomepy). If the number of visitors is high, the visitor assumes the owner of the minihomepage is popular.<ref name="Dissertation 2" />  Indeed, "What's his today?" was a popular phrase among Cyworld users as it was taken by many to be a sign of popularity. Online tools that manipulated such indicators of popularity were also produced: for instance, one service offered to increase the customer's today total by 20 a day,

Celebrity diaries were vastly popular, bringing about thousands of comments with each entry.<ref name="Article 21">{{cite news|title=Write Diaries on Cyworld!|url=http://www.segye.com/Articles/NEWS/CULTURE/Article.asp?aid=20080421003078&subctg1=&subctg2=|access-date=21 November 2012|date=21 April 2008|archive-date=10 August 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230810215056/http://www.segye.com/newsView/20080421003078|url-status=live}}</ref>  Minihomepies began to function as an official way to learn about the celebrity's life, but celebrities often controlled their public images through their minihomepies.<ref name="Article 21-2">{{cite news|title=Celebrity Minihomepy Marketing:"News of private life for sale"|url=http://www.newsen.com/news_view.php?uid=200807111630531010|access-date=21 November 2012|date=11 July 2008|archive-date=10 January 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140110203235/http://www.newsen.com/news_view.php?uid=200807111630531010|url-status=live}}</ref>  Celebrities have also employed minihomepies as a marketing strategy, using them to enhance their image or to advertise programs that they are in.<ref name="Article 21-2" />
