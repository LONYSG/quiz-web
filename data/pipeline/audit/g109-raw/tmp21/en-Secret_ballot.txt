{{Voting}}
The '''secret ballot''' is a voting method in which a [[voter]]'s identity in an [[election]] or a [[referendum]] is anonymous.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Australian-ballot|title=Australian ballot &#124; politics|website=Encyclopedia Britannica|access-date=2018-11-09|archive-date=2019-08-11|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190811051156/https://www.britannica.com/topic/Australian-ballot|url-status=live}}</ref> This forestalls attempts to influence the voter by [[intimidation]], blackmailing, and potential [[vote buying]]. This system is one means of achieving the goal of political privacy.

Secret [[ballot]]s are used in conjunction with various [[voting system]]s. The most basic form of a secret ballot uses paper ballots upon which each voter marks their choices. Without revealing the votes, the voter folds the ballot paper in half (if necessary) and places it in a sealed box. This box is later emptied for counting. An aspect of secret voting is the provision of a [[voting booth]] to enable the voter to write on the ballot paper without others being able to see what is being written. Today, printed ballot papers are usually provided, with the names of the candidates or questions and respective check boxes. Provisions are made at the [[polling place]] for the voters to record their preferences in secret, and the ballots are designed to eliminate bias and prevent anyone from linking voters to the ballot.

In systems of [[direct democracy]], such as the Swiss ''[[Landsgemeinde]]'', voting is typically conducted publicly to ensure all citizens can observe the outcome.

==Secret vs. public methods==

By the late 20th century, the secret ballot had become commonplace for individual electors in [[Democracy|democracies]].{{citation needed|date=February 2026}}

Votes by elected officials are generally entitled to the same secrecy as other citizens when their capacity to vote derives from their status as citizens, such as at elections. However, votes in their public capacity as representatives are typically public, so citizens can judge officials' and former officials' voting records in future elections. This may be done with a physical or electronic system or through a [[roll call vote]]. Some faster legislative voting methods do not record who voted in which way, though witnesses in the legislative chambers may still notice a given legislator's vote. These include [[voice vote]]s where the volume of shouting for or against is taken as a measure of numerical support and counting of raised hands. In some cases, a secret ballot is used to allow representatives to choose party leadership without fear of retaliation against those voting for losing candidates. The parliamentary tactics of forcing or avoiding a roll call vote can be used to discourage or encourage representatives to vote in a manner that is politically unpopular among constituents (for example, if a policy considered to be in the public interest is difficult to explain or unpopular but without a better alternative, or to hide pandering to a special interest) or to create or prevent fodder for political campaigns.

Public methods of citizen voting have included:
* Oral proclamation, where votes are shouted out one at a time, usually at an assembly<ref name="throughline">{{cite web |url=https://www.npr.org/transcripts/919532786 |title=How We Vote – Throughline podcast |website=[[NPR]] |date=22 Oct 2020 |access-date=7 February 2021 |archive-date=12 February 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210212101157/https://www.npr.org/transcripts/919532786 |url-status=live }}</ref>
* Going to a particular area at an assembly, such as a [[town meeting]] or the [[Iowa caucus]]. This is the origin of the term ''[[wikt:poll|poll]]'' for an election, originally meaning "top of the head", which is what was being counted at these assemblies.<ref name="throughline" />
* Small balls or other objects, such as corn, pebbles, beans, bullets, colored marbles, or cards. This is the origin of the term ''[[wikt:ballot|ballot]]'', originally meaning "small ball".<ref name="throughline" />
* Raising of hands at an assembly
* Cutting a brightly colored ballot (with the color corresponding to the party of choice) out of a newspaper and bringing it to a polling place<ref name="throughline" />
* An [[open ballot system]]

Private methods of citizen voting have included:
* Writing the name of the preferred candidate or outcome on a piece of paper and putting it in a container (which excludes illiterate voters)<ref name="throughline" />
* Marking a government-printed ballot (which may exclude illiterate voters if they only include words and cannot get assistance,<ref name="throughline" /> but some ballots include colors, symbols, or pictures to avoid this)

==History==

===Ancient===
In [[ancient Greece]], secret ballots were used in several situations like [[ostracism]]<ref name="murray1993">{{cite book |last1=Murray |first1=Oswyn |title=Early Greece |date=1993 |publisher=Harvard University Press |location=Cambridge, Massachusetts |isbn=978-0674221321 |edition=2nd}}</ref> and also to remain hidden from people seeking favors.<ref name="boegehold1963">{{cite journal |last1=Boegehold |first1=Alan L. |title=Toward a Study of Athenian Voting Procedure |journal=Hesperia |date=October 1963 |volume=32 |issue=4 |pages=366–372 |doi=10.2307/147360 |jstor=147360 }}</ref> In early 5th century BC the secrecy of ballot at [[Ecclesia (ancient Greece)|ecclesia]] was not the primary concern, but more of a consequence of using [[ballot]]s to count the votes accurately.<ref name="hansen1983">{{cite book |last1=Hansen |first1=Mogens Herman |title=The Athenian Ecclesia: a collection of articles 1976–1983 |date=1983 |publisher=Museum Tusculanum Press |location=Copenhagen |isbn=9788788073522 |pages=111}}</ref> Secret ballot was introduced into public life of Athens during second half of the fifth century.<ref name="boegehold1963" />

In [[ancient Rome]], the {{lang|la|[[Tabellariae Leges]]}} (English: ''Ballot Laws'') were four laws that implemented secret ballots for votes cast regarding each of the major elected assemblies of the [[Roman Republic]]. Three of the four laws were put in place in relatively quick succession, with one each in the years 139&nbsp;BC, 137&nbsp;BC, and 131&nbsp;BC, applying respectively to the elections of [[Roman magistrate|magistrates]], jury deliberations excepting charges of [[treason]] as well as the passage of laws. The final of the four laws was implemented more than two decades later in 107&nbsp;BC and served solely to expand the law passed in 137&nbsp;BC to require secret ballots for all jury deliberations, including treason.<ref name=":1" />

Before these ballot laws, one was required to verbally provide their vote to an individual responsible for tallying the votes, effectively publicly making every voter's vote known. Mandating secret ballots had the effect of reducing the influence of the Roman aristocracy, who were capable of influencing elections through a combination of bribes and threats. Secret balloting helps assuage both of those concerns, as not only are one's peers unable to determine which way you voted, there is additionally no proof that could be produced that one ''did'' vote certain way, perhaps contravening directions.<ref name=":1">{{Cite journal|url=http://www.jstor.org/stable/4477105|title=Secret Ballot and Its Effects in the Late Roman Republic|last=Yakobson |first=Alexander|year=1995|journal=Hermes|volume=123|issue=4|pages=426–442|jstor=4477105|accessdate=May 3, 2022|archive-date=May 3, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220503190247/https://www.jstor.org/stable/4477105|url-status=live}}</ref>

===France===

The same goes with the [[French Constitution of 1848|constitution of 1848]]:<ref>"Article 24. Suffrage is direct and universal. The act of voting is by secret ballot." [[:s:French Constitution of 1848 (New York Translation)|Constitution of 4 November 1848]] – via Wikisource.</ref> voters could hand-write the name of their preferred candidate on their ballot at home (the only condition was to write on white paper<ref>{{lang|fr|"Pour être recevable, chaque vote doit être inscrit sur un papier blanc"}}. {{lang|fr|italic=no|"[[:fr:Élection présidentielle française de 1848|Élection présidentielle française de 1848]]"}}. {{lang|fr|Wikipédia}} {{in lang|fr}}.{{unreliable source?|certain=y|reason=[[WP:RS/P]]|date=February 2026}}</ref>) or receive one distributed on the street.<ref name=":0">See the picture captioned {{lang|fr|"Distribution des bulletins d'élections dans les rues. L'Illustration du 23 septembre 1848*}} at the [http://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/histoire/suffrage_universel/suffrage-1848.asp National Assembly website]. {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100820172726/http://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/histoire/suffrage_universel/suffrage-1848.asp |date=2010-08-20 }}.</ref> The ballot was folded in order to prevent other people from reading its contents.<ref name=":0" />

[[Napoleon III|Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte]] attempted to abolish the secret ballot for the [[1851 French constitutional referendum|1851 plebiscite]] with an electoral [[decree]] requesting electors to write down "yes" or "no" under the eyes of everyone. But he faced strong opposition and eventually relented, allowing the secret ballot to occur.<ref>[[Karl Marx]], ''[[The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Napoleon]]'', [http://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1852/18th-brumaire/ch07.htm chapter VII]. {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101018134846/http://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1852/18th-brumaire/ch07.htm |date=2010-10-18 }}. "For the bourgeois and the shopkeeper had learned that in one of his decrees of December 2 Bonaparte had abolished the secret ballot and had ordered them to put a 'yes' or 'no' after their names on the official registers. The resistance of December 4 intimidated Bonaparte. During the night he had placards posted on all the street corners of Paris announcing the restoration of the secret ballot."</ref>

According to the official website of the [[National Assembly (France)|National Assembly]] (the lower house of the French parliament), the [[voting booth]] was permanently adopted only in 1913.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/histoire/suffrage_universel/suffrage-1870.asp#gauche|title=Le suffrage universel – Histoire |website=National Assembly|access-date=24 March 2018|archive-date=24 May 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140524022558/http://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/histoire/suffrage_universel/suffrage-1870.asp#gauche|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Belgium===
The British [[Ballot Act 1872]] inspired Belgian Catholic minister [[Jules Malou]] to implement the Belgian ''Secret Ballot Act of 9 July 1877''. The subsequent [[1878 Belgian general election]] led to a victory for the Liberal Party.

The 1877 law introduced the ballot (as opposed to simply writing any name on a piece of paper) as well as the voting booth.

===United Kingdom===
[[File:An Election III, The Polling, by William Hogarth.jpg|thumb|''[[Humours of an Election|The Polling]]'' by [[William Hogarth]] (1755). Before the secret ballot was introduced, voter intimidation was commonplace.]]
[[File:Cardiganshire Election ballot paper 1880.jpg|thumb|A British "how to vote" card from 1880]]

The demand for a secret ballot was one of the six points of [[Chartism]].<ref>In the words of the [[petition]] that was published in 1838:

: [http://www.chartists.net/The-six-points.htm ''"The suffrage, to be exempt from the corruption of the wealthy and the violence of the powerful, must be secret."''] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070927014747/http://www.chartists.net/The-six-points.htm |date=2007-09-27 }}</ref> The [[Parliament of the United Kingdom|British parliament]] of the time refused even to consider the Chartist demands. Still, [[Thomas Babington Macaulay, 1st Baron Macaulay|Lord Macaulay]], in an 1842 speech, while rejecting Chartism's six points as a whole, admitted that the secret ballot was one of the two points he could support.{{citation needed|date=October 2025}}

The [[London School Board]] election of 1870 was Britain's first large-scale election by secret ballot.{{citation needed|date=October 2025}}

After several failed attempts (several of them spearheaded by [[George Grote]]<ref>Kinzer, Bruce (2004), "George Grote, the Philosophical Radical and Politician", ''Brill's Companion to George Grote and the Classical Tradition'', London: Brill, pp. 16–45</ref>), the secret ballot was eventually extended generally in the [[Ballot Act 1872]], substantially reducing the cost of campaigning (as [[treating]] was no longer realistically possible) and was first used on [[1872 Pontefract by-election|15 August 1872]] to re-elect [[Hugh Childers]] as MP for [[Pontefract (UK Parliament constituency)|Pontefract]] in a [[ministerial by-election (UK)|ministerial by-election]] following his appointment as [[Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster]]. The original [[ballot box]], sealed in wax with a licorice stamp, is held at Pontefract museum.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/ahistoryoftheworld/objects/WryVwsknTr-aa4IQ-ID9iQ|title=BBC – A History of the World – Object : Pontefract's secret ballot box, 1872|website=www.bbc.co.uk|access-date=24 March 2018|archive-date=16 December 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191216105616/http://www.bbc.co.uk/ahistoryoftheworld/objects/WryVwsknTr-aa4IQ-ID9iQ|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Australia and New Zealand===

In Australia, secret balloting appears to have been first implemented in [[Tasmania]] on 7 February 1856.{{citation needed|date=October 2025}}

Until the original Tasmanian ''Electoral Act 1856'' was "re-discovered" recently, credit for the first implementation of the secret ballot often went to Victoria, where the former mayor of Melbourne [[William Nicholson (Australian politician)|William Nicholson]] pioneered it,<ref>Blainey, G 2016, "The story of Australia's people: the rise and rise of a new Australia", Viking, Penguin Random House, Ringwood, Victoria, Australia, p.18.</ref> and simultaneously [[South Australia]].<ref>Terry Newman, {{cite web |url= http://www.mumble.com.au/enrollingthepeople/tasmania/newman_tasmania_and_secret_ballot.pdf |title= Tasmania and the Secret Ballot |access-date= 2015-05-19 |archive-date= 2015-05-20 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20150520135326/http://www.mumble.com.au/enrollingthepeople/tasmania/newman_tasmania_and_secret_ballot.pdf |url-status= live }}&nbsp;{{small|(144&nbsp;[[Kibibyte|KiB]])}} (2003), 49(1) ''Aust J Pol & Hist 93'', accessed May 20, 2015</ref> Victoria enacted [[Electoral Act 1856|legislation]] for secret ballots on [[March 19|19 March]] [[1856]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://foundingdocs.gov.au/item-sdid-21.html|title=Documenting Democracy|website=foundingdocs.gov.au|access-date=24 March 2018|archive-date=3 April 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180403054535/https://www.foundingdocs.gov.au/item-sdid-21.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Singapore===
A secret ballot is used in [[Singaporean elections]] to ensure the election's integrity and that the voter's decision is respected and kept secret. [[Ballot boxes]] undergo [[tamperproofing]] checks before use to ensure that they are not tampered with. These boxes are sealed (except for the opening slots) prior to the voting window, which opens at 8{{nbsp}}a.m. [[Singapore Time|SGT]] for 12{{nbsp}}hours. To facilitate accounting, each ballot has a serial number with a counterfoil attached to it; the counterfoil is also used for facilitating vote tracing if a court order is needed. Per safety and privacy reasons, voters are forbidden from using electronic devices inside a polling station, and may not bring ballot papers out of the polling station. They are also advised not to make any markings (other than a X or a cross) by which they may be identified, as such votes may be rejected by officials.<ref>{{cite web |title=ELD {{!}} Do's and Don'ts of Voting |url=https://www.eld.gov.sg/voters_dosdonts.html |website=www.eld.gov.sg |access-date=5 May 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=ELD {{!}} Polling |url=https://www.eld.gov.sg/candidate_parliamentary_polling.html |website=www.eld.gov.sg |access-date=5 May 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=GE2025: Dos and don'ts on Polling Day |url=https://www.straitstimes.com/singapore/politics/ge2025-dos-and-donts-on-polling-day |website=The Straits Times |access-date=5 May 2025 |language=en |date=29 April 2025}}</ref> [[Exit polls]] are also illegal under the election act to maintain secrecy of voting;<ref>{{cite web |title=ELD {{!}} Voter FAQs |url=https://www.eld.gov.sg/faq_voters.html |website=www.eld.gov.sg |access-date=5 May 2025}}</ref> however, [[sampling (statistics)|sample counts]], a variant of the procedure, have been used since the [[2015 Singaporean general election|2015 general election]] to reduce speculation from unofficial sources.<ref>{{cite web |title=SampleCount Generic.pdf |url=https://www.eld.gov.sg/mediarelease/SampleCount_Generic.pdf |access-date=5 May 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160804024851/https://www.eld.gov.sg/mediarelease/SampleCount_Generic.pdf |archive-date=4 August 2016 |url-status=live}}</ref>

After the voting window ends, a ballot box has its opening sealed and is transported to the polling station's designated counting centre under police escort. The boxes then undergo accounting before being opened for vote-counting to ensure that they are fully present and that their seals are intact. After the election, ballots and other official documents used for the election are placed in a new, separate box, and transferred to a secure vault inside the [[Supreme Court of Singapore|Supreme Court]] where it will be locked for up to six months under safe custody. Only a judge of the Supreme Court has the discretion to open the sealed boxes for inspection, although the power is to institute or maintain a prosecution or an application to invalidate an election. After six months, the boxes are transported to [[Tuas South Incineration Plant]] and destroyed thereafter.<ref>{{cite web |title=ELD {{!}} Ballot Secrecy |url=https://www.eld.gov.sg/voters_ballotsecrecy.html |website=www.eld.gov.sg |access-date=3 May 2025}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=GE2025: Is my vote really secret? |url=https://www.straitstimes.com/singapore/politics/ge2025-is-my-vote-really-secret |website=The Straits Times |access-date=3 May 2025 |language=en |date=28 April 2025}}</ref>

===United States===
[[File:1900 New York polling place.jpg|thumb|[[New York (state)|New York]] polling place {{circa|1900}}, showing [[voting booth]]s on the left]]
Before the final years of the 19th century, partisan newspapers printed filled-out ballots, which party workers distributed on election day so voters could drop them directly into the boxes. Individual states moved to secret ballots soon after the [[1884 United States presidential election|presidential election of 1884]], finishing with [[Kentucky]] in 1891 when it quit using an oral ballot.<ref>Eldon Cobb Evans, ''A History of the Australian Ballot System in the United States#'' (1917) [https://archive.org/details/ahistoryaustral00evangoog <!-- quote=Australian ballot 1890. --> online].</ref>{{page needed|date=January 2021}}

Initially, however, a state's new ballot did not necessarily have all four components of an "Australian ballot":<ref>{{cite book |title=Webster's Seventh New Collegiate Dictionary |url=https://archive.org/stream/webstersseventhn00unse#page/59/mode/1up |publisher=G&C Merriam Company |year=1967 |location=[[Springfield, Massachusetts]] |page=59 }}</ref>
# an official ballot being printed at public expense,
# on which the names of the nominated candidates of all parties and all proposals appear,
# being distributed only at the polling place and
# being marked in secret.

[[File:Ballot-foia.png|thumb|Ballots or scanned images available to public for independent audits]]
[[File:Keepimages.png|thumb|Ballot images saved by election scanners]]
After ballots are cast and no longer identifiable to the voter, several states make the ballots and copies of them available to the public so the public can check counts and do other research with the anonymous ballots.<ref name=rcfp>{{Cite web|url=https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-sections/i-election-records/|title=I. Election Records Archives|accessdate=May 3, 2022|archive-date=May 25, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220525220226/https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-sections/i-election-records/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name=rcfpelect>{{Cite web|url=https://www.rcfp.org/resources/election-legal-guide/|title=Reporters Committee Election Legal Guide &#124; Updated 2020|accessdate=May 3, 2022|website=www.rcfp.org|archive-date=April 27, 2022|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220427044101/https://www.rcfp.org/resources/election-legal-guide/|url-status=live}}</ref>

[[Louisville, Kentucky]], was the first city in the United States to adopt the Australian ballot. It was drafted by [[Lewis Naphtali Dembitz]], the uncle of and inspiration for future [[Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States|Supreme Court associate justice]] [[Louis Brandeis]]. [[Massachusetts]] adopted the first state-wide Australian ballot, written by reformer [[Richard Henry Dana III]], in 1888. Consequently, it is also known as the "Massachusetts ballot". Seven states did not have government-printed ballots until the 20th century. [[Georgia (U.S. state)|Georgia]] started using them in 1922.<ref>See 1922 Georgia session laws, chapter 530, p. 100.</ref> When [[South Carolina]] followed suit in 1950, this completed the nationwide switch to Australian ballots.<ref name="Time19500501">{{cite magazine | title=South Carolina: At Last | magazine=Time | volume=LV | issue=18 | date=1950-05-01 | url=https://time.com/archive/6614621/south-carolina-at-last/ | access-date=2025-04-22}}</ref> The 20th century also brought the first criminal prohibitions against [[Electoral fraud#Vote buying|buying votes]] in 1925.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/597|title=18 U.S. Code § 597 – Expenditures to influence voting|website=LII / Legal Information Institute|access-date=24 March 2018|archive-date=16 February 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180216030702/https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/597|url-status=live}}</ref>

While U.S. elections are now held primarily by secret ballot, there are a few exceptions:
* North Carolina has a confidential but not secret ballot for early in-person voting (one-stop) and absentee-by-mail voting. General Statute § 163-227.5 states that the "ballot shall have a ballot number on it in accordance with G.S. 163-230.1(c), or shall have an equivalent identifier to allow for retrievability." If a voter casts an absentee ballot or votes at a one-stop site (early voting) or absentee-by-mail, but it is discovered that the voter was ineligible (ex., died between casting ballot and election day), the ballot would be retrieved using a unique number written at the top of the ballot. Each county Election Director maintains a database with the names of each voter and associated retrievable ballot numbers.
* Mail-in ballots do not meet the definition of Australian ballots, as they are distributed to voters' homes, and there is no guarantee that they are marked secretly. They may be used as [[absentee ballot]]s, and [[Colorado]], [[Oregon]], and [[Washington (state)|Washington]] conduct all elections by mail.<ref>{{cite web|last=Washington Secretary of State|title=Vote by Mail in Washington State|url=https://wei.sos.wa.gov/agency/osos/en/voters/Pages/vote_by_mail.aspx|publisher=State of Washington|access-date=27 April 2014|archive-date=27 April 2014|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140427025441/https://wei.sos.wa.gov/agency/osos/en/voters/Pages/vote_by_mail.aspx|url-status=live}}</ref>
* In some [[U.S. state]]s, a [[political party]] nominating [[caucus]] requires an [[open ballot system]]. This includes, most notably, the leadoff Presidential nominating state of [[Iowa]].
* The Constitution of [[West Virginia]] specifies that voters may choose to cast an open ballot, though they must also have the option to cast a secret ballot.<ref>''See'' W. Va. Const. Art. IV, §2, "In all elections by the people, the mode of voting shall be by ballot; but the voter shall be left free to vote by either open, sealed or secret ballot, as he may elect".</ref>

==International law==
The right to hold elections by secret ballot is included in numerous [[treaties]] and international agreements that obligate their signatory states:
* Article 21.3 of the ''[[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]]'' states, "The will of the people...shall be expressed in periodic and genuine elections which...shall be held by secret vote or by equivalent free voting procedures."<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights|title=Universal Declaration of Human Rights|website=www.un.org|access-date=24 March 2018}}</ref>
* Article 23 of the ''[[American Convention on Human Rights]] (the Pact of San Jose, Costa Rica)'' grants to every citizen of member states of the [[Organization of American States]] the right and opportunity "to vote and to be elected in genuine periodic elections, which shall be by universal and equal suffrage and by secret ballot that guarantees the free expression of the will of the voters".<ref>[http://www.refworld.org/cgi-bin/texis/vtx/rwmain?docid=3ae6b36510 American Convention on Human Rights, "Pact of San Jose", Costa Rica] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150211194551/http://www.refworld.org/cgi-bin/texis/vtx/rwmain?docid=3ae6b36510 |date=2015-02-11 }}, Organization of American States, Nov. 22, 1969.</ref>
* Paragraph 7.4 of the ''Document of the Copenhagen Meeting of the Conference on the Human Dimension of the CSCE'' obligates the member states of the [[Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe]] to "ensure that votes are cast by secret ballot or by equivalent free voting procedure, and that they are counted and reported honestly with the official results made public."<ref>[http://www.osce.org/odihr/elections/14304?download=true Document of the Copenhagen Meeting of the Conference on the Human Dimension of the CSCE] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150211184544/http://www.osce.org/odihr/elections/14304?download=true |date=2015-02-11 }}, Copenhagen, June 29, 1990.</ref>
* Article 5 of the ''Convention on the Standards of Democratic Elections, Electoral Rights and Freedoms in the Member States of the [[Commonwealth of Independent States]]'' obligates electoral bodies not to perform "any action violating the principle of voter's secret will expression."<ref>[http://eos.cartercenter.org/uploads/document_file/path/405/Convention_on_Electoral_Standards.pdf Convention on the Standards of Democratic Elections, Electoral Rights and Freedoms in the Member States of the Commonwealth of Independent States] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150211183316/http://eos.cartercenter.org/uploads/document_file/path/405/Convention_on_Electoral_Standards.pdf |date=2015-02-11 }}, Oct. 7, 2002, Chisinau. Unofficial English translation provided by the European Commission for Democracy through Law (Venice Commission), Jan. 22, 2007.</ref>
* Article 29 of the ''Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities'' requires that all Contracting States protect "the right of persons with disabilities to vote by secret ballot in elections and public referendums".<ref>{{cite web |title=Article 29 – Participation in political and public life |url=https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-29-participation-in-political-and-public-life.html |website=United Nations |access-date=2026-10-02}}</ref>

==Disabled people==
[[File:topvoter2.jpg|thumb|ISG TopVoter, a voting machine which assures secret ballot for voters with disabilities]]
Ballot design and polling place architecture often deny the [[disabled]] the possibility to cast a vote secretly. In many democracies disabled persons may vote by appointing another person who is allowed to join them in the voting booth and fill the ballot in their name.<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Mercurio|first=Bryan|date=2003|title=Discrimination in Electoral Law|url=https://heinonline.org/HOL/P?h=hein.journals/alterlj28&i=284|journal=Alternative Law Journal|volume=28|issue=6|pages=272–276|doi=10.1177/1037969X0302800603|s2cid=147550553|access-date=2018-10-10|archive-date=2018-10-11|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181011014010/https://heinonline.org/HOL/P?h=hein.journals/alterlj28&i=284|url-status=live|url-access=subscription}}</ref> This does not assure secrecy of the ballot.

The [[United Nations]] [[Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities]], which entered into force in 2008, ensures a secret ballot for disabled voters. Article 29 of the Convention requires that all Contracting States protect "the right of the person with disabilities to vote by secret ballot in [[elections]] and public [[referendums]]". According to this provision, each Contracting State should provide voting equipment enabling disabled voters to vote independently and secretly. Some democracies, e.g. the [[United States]], the [[Netherlands]], [[Slovenia]], [[Albania]] or [[India]] allow disabled voters to use electronic [[voting machines]]. In others, among them [[Azerbaijan]], [[Canada]], [[Ghana]], the [[United Kingdom]], and most [[Africa]]n and [[Asia]]n countries, visually impaired voters can use ballots in [[Braille]] or paper ballot templates. Article 29 also requires that Contracting States ensure "that voting procedures, facilities and materials are appropriate, accessible and easy to understand and use." In some democracies, e.g., the United Kingdom, [[Sweden]] and the United States, all the polling places already are fully accessible for disabled voters.{{citation needed|date=January 2021}}

==Officeholders==
In some countries, including France, Georgia, Hungary, South Korea and Turkey, the secret ballot is used by members of legislative bodies for impeachment of officeholders.<ref>{{Cite web |last=KST |first=Published |last2=KST |first2=Updated |date=2016-12-09 |title=Secret ballot cast for impeachment motion |url=https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/20161209/secret-ballot-cast-for-impeachment-motion |access-date=2026-11-16 |website=The Korea Times |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=Hungary 2011 (rev. 2016) Constitution - Constitute |url=https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Hungary_2016 |access-date=2026-11-16 |website=www.constituteproject.org |language=en}}</ref> Additionally, South Korea's [[National Assembly (South Korea)|National Assembly]] uses the secret ballot for votes on appointments, dismissals and [[Veto override|veto overrides]].

==Secrecy exceptions==
{{More citations needed section|date=April 2010}}
The United Kingdom secret ballot arrangements are sometimes criticised because linking a ballot paper to the voter who cast it is possible. Each ballot paper is numbered, and each elector has a number. When an elector is given a ballot paper, their number is noted down on the counterfoil of the ballot paper (which also carries the ballot paper number). The secrecy of the ballot is not guaranteed if anyone sees the counterfoils, which are locked away before the ballot boxes are opened at the count.

This measure is thought to be justified as a security arrangement so that false ballot papers could be identified if there was an allegation of fraud. The process of matching ballot papers to voters is formally permissible only if an [[Election Court]] requires it; the Election Court has rarely made such an order since the secret ballot was introduced in the [[Ballot Act 1872]]. One example was in a close local election contest in Richmond-upon-Thames in the late 1970s with three disputed ballots and a declared majority of two votes. Reportedly, prisoners were observed identifying voters' ballot votes on a list in 2008.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.theguardian.com/notesandqueries/query/0,,-1051,00.html|title=What happens to the voting slips used in British elections after they have been counted? &#124; Notes and Queries |website=The Guardian |access-date=2016-12-16|archive-date=2017-01-16|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170116075359/https://www.theguardian.com/notesandqueries/query/0,,-1051,00.html|url-status=live}}</ref> The legal authority for this system is set out in the Parliamentary Elections Rules in Schedule 1 of the [[Representation of the People Act 1983]].<ref>{{cite web |url= http://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/__data/assets/electoral_commission_pdf_file/0020/13259/Ballot-Secrecy-2006-12_23827-6127__E__N__S__W__.pdf |title= Factsheet: Ballot secrecy |website=[[Electoral Commission (United Kingdom)|Electoral Commission of the United Kingdom]] |format=PDF |date=December 2006 |url-status= dead |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20081209142843/http://www.electoralcommission.org.uk/__data/assets/electoral_commission_pdf_file/0020/13259/Ballot-Secrecy-2006-12_23827-6127__E__N__S__W__.pdf |archive-date= 2008-12-09 }}</ref>

Most states guarantee a secret ballot in the [[United States]]. Some states, including [[Indiana]] and [[North Carolina]], require the ability to link some ballots to voters. This may, for example, be used with absentee voting to retain the ability to cancel a vote if the voter dies before election day.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.in.gov/sos/elections/files/2020%20Election%20Administrators%20Manual.FINAL.pdf|title=2020 Indiana Election Administrator's Manual|website=in.gov|access-date=2020-03-02|archive-date=2020-01-19|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200119203651/https://www.in.gov/sos/elections/files/2020%20Election%20Administrators%20Manual.FINAL.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|last=Wilkie|first=Jordan|url=https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2019/jul/11/north-carolina-voting-secret-ballot|title='A risk to democracy': North Carolina law may be violating secrecy of the ballot|date=2019-07-11|work=The Guardian|access-date=2020-03-02|language=en-GB|issn=0261-3077|archive-date=2020-03-02|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200302024800/https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2019/jul/11/north-carolina-voting-secret-ballot|url-status=live}}</ref> Sometimes the number on the ballot is printed on a perforated stub which is torn off and placed on a ring (like a [[shower curtain]] ring) before the ballot is cast into the ballot box. The stubs prove that an elector has voted and ensure they can only vote once, but the ballots are both secret and anonymous. At the end of voting day, the number of ballots inside the box should match the number of stubs on the ring, certifying that a registered elector cast every ballot and that none of them were lost or fabricated. Sometimes, the ballots themselves are numbered, making the vote trackable. In 2012 in Colorado, this procedure was ruled legal by Federal District Judge [[Christine Arguello]], who determined that the U.S. Constitution does not grant a right to a secret ballot.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.denverpost.com/breakingnews/ci_21601455/federal-judge-says-no-constitutional-right-secret-ballot|title=Federal judge says no constitutional right to secret ballot in Boulder case|date=21 September 2012|access-date=24 March 2018|archive-date=4 March 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304082635/http://www.denverpost.com/breakingnews/ci_21601455/federal-judge-says-no-constitutional-right-secret-ballot|url-status=live}}</ref>
