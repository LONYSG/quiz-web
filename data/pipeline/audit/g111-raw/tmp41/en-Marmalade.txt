'''Marmalade''' is a sweet, tangy [[Fruit preserves|fruit preserve]] made from the juice and peel of [[citrus]] fruits boiled with sugar and water. The well-known version is made from [[bitter orange]], but other citrus fruits such as [[lemon]]s and [[Lime (fruit)|limes]] can also be used. Bitter orange is mostly used in marmalade because of its high [[pectin]] content, which gives a thick consistency to its marmalade. Bitter oranges also have the proper balance of [[acid]] and pectin to create the right consistency. To make marmalade from fruit with less pectin, pectin must be added. 

Historically, the term marmalade was often used for non-citrus preserves. [[Mango]], [[pineapple]], [[apricot]], and [[cocoa bean]]s have all been made into marmalade. In the 21st century, the term refers mainly to jam made with citrus fruits. [[White sugar]] ([[sucrose]]) is typically used to sweeten marmalade. [[Sugar substitute]]s such as [[sucralose]], [[aspartame]], or [[saccharin]] may be used. Artificial dyes and flavouring agents may be added to marmalade to enhance taste, flavour, and appearance. 

Originally marmalade was made from [[quince]], essentially creating the thick [[quince cheese]]. [[Mary Kettilby]]'s 1714 cookery book, ''[[A Collection of above Three Hundred Receipts]]'' (pages 78–79) discusses how to make marmalade. Modern marmalade was created in the 1700s when the Scots added water to make it less solid. The Scots made marmalade a breakfast item, and soon after the rest of Britain followed.  

The word ''marmalade'' in the English language comes from [[French_language|French]] {{Lang|fr|marmelade}} which came from the [[Portuguese language|Portuguese]] word {{Lang|pt|marmelada}}, starting with the [[Greek language|Greek]] word {{lang|grc-Latn|melimēlon}} that means 'sweet apple'.  

The preserve has been mentioned in various books and is the fictional character [[Paddington Bear]]'s favourite food. The 2014 movie ''[[Paddington (film)|Paddington]]'' slightly increased marmalade sales in the United Kingdom.

== Origins ==

=== Early history ===
{{Main|Quince cheese}}

In the 1500s, marmalade was made from [[quince]], and was imported to England from Spain and Italy.<ref name="Wilson-2000">{{Cite book |last=Wilson |first=C. Anne |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=4Jh0BAAAQBAJ |title=The Book of Marmalade |date=2 January 2000 |publisher=University of Pennsylvania Press |isbn=978-0-8122-1727-8 |pages= |language=en}}</ref>{{Reference page|page=38}} The quince jam or quince cheese, was a firm, sticky, sweet reddish hard paste made by slowly cooking quince fruit with sugar, and is still made today.<ref>{{cite web |title=Quince Jelly |url=https://www.bbcgoodfood.com/user/55876/recipe/quince-jelly |access-date=6 August 2025 |website=BBC Good Food |publisher=}}</ref>

=== Scottish and English influence ===
The Scots are credited with developing marmalade as a spread, with Scottish recipes in the 18th century using more water to produce a less solid preserve than before.<ref name="henry-2012">{{Cite book |last=Henry |first=Diana |url=http://archive.org/details/saltsugarsmokeho0000henr |title=Salt sugar smoke: how to preserve fruit, vegetables, meat and fish |date=2012 |publisher=London : Mitchell Beazley |others=Internet Archive |isbn=978-1-84533-675-2 |pages=38 |language=en}}</ref>

The Scots were the people who made marmalade a breakfast item. [[James Boswell]] and [[Samuel Johnson]] were given it at breakfast while in Scotland in 1773. In the 19th century, the English followed suit and began to eat marmalade in the morning. The American writer [[Louisa May Alcott]] visited Britain, and later described "a choice pot of marmalade and a slice of cold ham" to be "essentials of English table comfort".<ref name="Spread"/>

== Etymology ==

=== Actual ===

[[File:FollowsAndBateMarmaladeCutter.jpg|thumb|Antique marmalade cutter, used to cut citrus fruit peel into thin slices|alt=An antique brown and tan marmalade cutter used to cut citrus fruit peel into thin slices.]]

The word ''marmalade'' in the English language comes from French {{lang|fr|marmelade}} which, in turn, came from the [[Portuguese language|Portuguese]] word {{lang|pt|marmelada}}.<ref name="Online Etymology Dictionary-2020" /><ref>{{Cite web |title=Marmalade {{!}} Meaning & Definition for UK English |url=https://www.lexico.com/definition/marmalade |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210225022326/https://www.lexico.com/definition/marmalade |archive-date=25 February 2021 |access-date=26 May 2022 |website=Lexico.com}}</ref> According to José Pedro Machado's {{lang|pt|Dicionário Etimológico da Língua Portuguesa|italic=yes}}, the oldest known document where this Portuguese word is to be found is [[Gil Vicente]]'s play ''Comédia de Rubena'', written in 1521:

: {{lang|pt|Temos tanta marmelada}} ({{Translation|We have so much quince jelly.}})
: {{lang|pt|Que a minha mãe vai me dar um pouco}} ({{Translation|That my mother will give me some.}})<ref>Translation: We have so much quince jelly / That my mother will give me some. [http://www.quimera-editores.com/vicente/pdf/Rubena.pdf Maria João Amaral, ed. Gil Vicente, ''Rubena'' (Lisbon:Quimera) 1961 (e-book)]</ref>

The Portuguese word comes from the Latin {{lang|la|melimelum}} or "a sweet apple",<ref name="Online Etymology Dictionary-2020" /> in turn from [[Ancient Greek language|Greek]] {{lang|grc|μελίμηλον}} {{lang|grc-Latn|melimēlon}} 'sweet apple', from {{lang|grc|μέλη}} 'honey' + {{lang|grc|μῆλον}} {{lang|grc|mēlon}} 'apple, round fruit', became [[Galician-Portuguese language|Galician-Portuguese]] {{lang|pt|marmelo}} 'quince'.<ref name=Scott>[https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/cgi-bin/ptext?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0057%3Aentry%3D%2366024 Melimelon], Henry George Liddell, Robert Scott, ''A Greek–English Lexicon'', on Perseus Digital Library</ref> The name originated in the 16th century from [[Middle French]] {{lang|frm|marmelade}} and Portuguese, where {{lang|pt|marmelada}} applied to [[Quince cheese|quince jam]].<ref>{{harvnb|Wilson|1999}}</ref><ref name="Online Etymology Dictionary-2020">{{cite web |date=2020 |title=Marmalade |url=https://www.etymonline.com/search?q=marmalade |access-date=8 January 2020 |publisher=Online Etymology Dictionary, Douglas Harper}}</ref> The English recipe book of Eliza Cholmondeley, dated from 1677 and held at the Chester Record Office in the [[Cheshire]] county archives, has one of the earliest marmalade recipes ("Marmelet of Oranges") which produced a firm, thick dark paste.<ref name="henry-2012" /> The modern definition of marmalade is a jam made from citrus fruits rather than quince.<ref name="Legislative Services Branch-2002">{{cite web |author=Legislative Services Branch |date=12 January 2002 |title=EUR-lex |url=http://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?qid=1501588491296&uri=CELEX:32001L0113 |website=eur-lex.europa.eu}}</ref>

=== Folk ===
According to a Scottish legend, the creation of orange marmalade in the Scottish city of [[Dundee]] occurred by accident. The legend tells of a ship carrying a cargo of oranges that broke down in the port, resulting in some ingenious locals making marmalade out of the cargo.<ref name="scotsindependent.org">{{cite web |title=Features – Scottish Food, Traditions and Customs – Dundee Marmalade |url=http://scotsindependent.scot/oldsitearchive/scotind/features/food/dundee_marmalade.htm |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170216131459/http://scotsindependent.scot/oldsitearchive/scotind/features/food/dundee_marmalade.htm |archive-date=16 February 2017 |access-date=15 February 2017 |work=scotsindependent.org}}</ref><ref>C. Anne Wilson, ''The Book of Marmalade''. [[Constable & Robinson|Constable]], London. 1985. {{ISBN|0-09-465670-3}}.</ref> Since then, the city has had a long association with marmalade.<ref>{{cite web |title=Features – Scottish Food, Traditions and Customs – Dundee Marmalade |url=http://www.greatbritishkitchen.co.uk/recipebook/index.php?option=com_rapidrecipe&page=viewrecipe&recipe_id=418 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080129010234/http://www.greatbritishkitchen.co.uk/recipebook/index.php?option=com_rapidrecipe&page=viewrecipe&recipe_id=418 |archive-date=29 January 2008 |access-date=26 January 2017 |work=The GBK Cookbook |publisher=The British Food Trust}}</ref> However, this legend was "decisively disproved by food historians", according to a ''[[New York Times]]'' report.<ref>{{cite web |last=Apple Jr. |first=R. W. |date=27 March 2002 |title=This Blessed Plot, This Realm of Tea, This Marmalade |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2002/03/27/dining/this-blessed-plot-this-realm-of-tea-this-marmalade.html |access-date=13 January 2020 |work=[[The New York Times]]}}</ref>

A [[folk etymology]] asserts that [[Mary, Queen of Scots]] ate marmalade as a treatment for [[Motion sickness|seasickness]],<ref>{{Cite web |title=Marmalade myths – Recipes from Scotland – National Library of Scotland |url=https://digital.nls.uk/recipes/themes/preserves/myths.html |access-date=17 November 2023 |website=digital.nls.uk}}</ref> and that the name is derived from her maids' whisper of {{lang|fr|Ma'am est malade}} ('Ma'am is ill'), though the word's origin has nothing to do with Mary.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Marmalade |url=http://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-mar3.htm |access-date=13 July 2016 |website=World Wide Words}}</ref>

Marmalade is made from the juice and peel of citrus fruits boiled with sugar and water. == Commerce ==

=== Production companies ===
[[File:JamesRobertson.jpg|thumb|Scottish grocer [[Robertson's|James Robertson]] in 1874|alt=A black and white portrait of Scottish grocer James Robertson in 1874.|227x227px]]

[[Keiller's marmalade|James Keiller]] (died 1839) and his mother, Janet, ran a small sweet and preserves shop in the Seagate area of Dundee.<ref name="scotsindependent.org" /> They then began to produce "Dundee Marmalade". The business prospered, and remains a signature marmalade producer today.<ref>W.M. Matthew, ''The Keiller Dynasty 1800–1879'' narrates the history of Keillers; [https://www.bbc.co.uk/legacies/work/scotland/perth_tayside/article_2.shtml BBC News "Legacies: Keiller's: Sticky Success"]: offers an abbreviated version.</ref>

The [[Frank Cooper (marmalade manufacturer)|Frank Cooper Oxford Marmalade]] manufacturing business sold marmalade in 1874. It started out first for just for [[Oxford]] students, but the business expanded all over England and abroad.<ref>{{cite web |title=Frank Cooper Ltd. 1881–1992 |url=https://heritagesearch.oxfordshire.gov.uk/records/ |access-date=4 July 2025 |website=Heritage Search – Oxfordshire County Council}}</ref>

[[Robertson's]] was founded in 1864 by James and Marion Robertson in [[Paisley, Renfrewshire]]. The business was known for making Golden Shred marmalade. Today, it no longer exists, and is now owned by [[Premier Foods]], though they still make the same product Golden Shred.<ref>{{Cite web |date=7 February 2015 |title=A chance to take marmalade back to its Scottish roots |url=https://www.heraldscotland.com/life_style/arts_ents/13200554.chance-take-marmalade-back-scottish-roots/ |access-date=4 July 2025 |website=The Herald |language=en}}</ref>

=== Market ===
As of 2021, the international market for jams and preserves was US$8.46&nbsp;billion. Global value has been projected to increase in the future with almost US$9.90&nbsp;billion expected in 2027, with an average annual growth of about 3.5% between now and then.<ref name=":0" />

== Legal definitions ==

=== North America ===
Under the Canadian Food and Drug Regulations (C.R.C., c. 870), marmalade is a standardised food and defined as a food of jelly-like composition that consists of at least 65% water-soluble solids. The regulations permit the use of [[Acidity regulator|pH adjusting agents]] to prevent the marmalade from [[dehydration]], [[antifoaming agents]] to prevent blemishes on surface coatings and enable efficient filling of containers, and an acid ingredient to compensate for the natural acidity of the citrus fruit used. If [[pectin]] is added, the marmalade must contain at least 27% of peel, pulp, or juice of citrus fruit. Class II preservatives may also be used.<ref name="Canregs">{{cite web |url=https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/page-63.html#h-572471 |title=Marmalade |publisher=Consolidated Federal Laws of Canada, Food and Drug Regulations, Government of Canada |date=3 June 2019 |access-date=16 July 2019}}</ref>

The Canadian Food and Drug Regulations (C.R.C., c. 870) specify that [[pineapple]] or [[fig]] marmalade must be of jelly-like consistency, achieved by boiling the [[pulp (fruit)|pulp]] of juice of the fruit with water, and a [[sweetening]] ingredient. Pineapple or fig marmalade should contain at least 45% of the named fruit.<ref name=Canregs/>

In the United States, marmalade should contain at least 65 percent soluble solids, and have at least 70 points to be ranked U.S. Grade B or U.S. Choice.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Orange Marmalade Grades and Standards {{!}} Agricultural Marketing Service |url=https://www.ams.usda.gov/grades-standards/orange-marmalade-grades-and-standards |access-date=5 July 2025 |website=www.ams.usda.gov}}</ref>

=== Europe ===
In 1979, the [[European Economic Community]] (EEC) passed a [[Directive (European Union)|directive]] regulating labelling of fruit jams, jellies and marmalades, which defined marmalade as a gelled mixture of sugars and citrus fruit's [[Pulp (fruit)|pulp]], [[purée]], [[Fruit juice|juice]], [[Aqueous extract|aqueous extracts]] or [[Peel (fruit)|peel]]. Under the directive marmalade had to contain at least 20% citrus fruit of which 7.5% had to be obtained from the [[Fruit (plant structure)|endocarp]].<ref>{{Cite EU law|url=https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/1979/693/oj/eng|title=Council Directive 79/693/EEC of 24 July 1979 on the approximation of the laws of the Member States relating to fruit jams, jellies and marmalades and chestnut purée}}</ref> 
The strict definition mandating the use of citrus fruits is said to be a result of British lobbying, even though in some other European languages, the word [[cognate]] with English ''marmalade'' - such as {{lang|de|Marmelade}} in German or {{lang|it|marmellata}} in Italian - is commonly used to refer to other fruit jams, too.<ref name="Rawlinson2026">{{cite news |last1=Rawlinson |first1=Kevin |title=Keir Starmalade, anyone? Will marmalade really have to be rebranded in UK? |url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/apr/04/breakfast-reset-will-marmalade-really-be-renamed-in-post-brexit-food-deal |access-date=24 May 2026 |work=The Guardian |date=4 April 2026}}</ref><ref name=":1">{{Cite web |last=Seddon |first=Paul |date=3 April 2026 |title=Marmalade to be rebranded in post-Brexit food deal |url=https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c0e53x475qjo |access-date=24 May 2026 |website=BBC News |language=en-GB}}</ref>

The marmalade definition remained the same until June 2026, except that in 2004 Austria and Germany were granted a derogation to allow fruit jams to be sold as ''Marmelade'' at farmers' markets.<ref name="Rawlinson2026" /><ref name=":1" /><ref>{{Cite EU law|title=Council Directive 2004/84/EC of 10 June 2004 amending Directive 2001/113/EC relating to fruit jams, jellies and marmalades and sweetened chestnut purée intended for human consumption|url=https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2004/84/eng}}</ref> In 2024, the [[European Union]], from which the UK had withdrawn in 2020 ([[Brexit]]), agreed to broaden the definition by allowing member states to permit the label ''marmalade'' for other jams, reflecting the interchangeability of the terms in many European languages. The new directive is going to be applicable on 14 June 2026. To avoid consumer confusion, the label of citrus-based marmalades in the directive was renamed to ''citrus marmalade'', in which the word "citrus" may be replaced by the name of the citrus fruit used, e.g. ''orange marmalade''. The ingredient requirements for citrus marmalades remain the same as in the 1979 directive.<ref name="Rawlinson2026" /><ref name=":1" /><ref name=":2">{{Cite web |last=Lynch |first=David |date=4 April 2026 |title=Government steps in to clarify post-Brexit marmalade naming rules |url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/home-news/marmalade-brexit-naming-laws-europe-b2951848.html |access-date=24 May 2026 |website=The Independent |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite EU law|url=https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1438/eng|title=Directive (EU) 2024/1438 of the European Parliament and of the Council}}</ref><ref name="tetens-2024">{{Cite web |last=Tetens |first=Katrin |date=3 June 2024 |title=Are you ready for the updated EU Breakfast Directives? |url=https://www.intertek.com/blog/2024/06-03-updated-eu-breakfast-directives/ |access-date=7 July 2025 |website=www.intertek.com |language=en}}</ref>

The amendment also raises the minimum fruit content of jams: from the current 350 to 450 grams per kilogram, and to 500 grams for ‘extra’ products. The aim is to reduce the sugar content and promote a healthier composition.<ref>https://orf.at/#/stories/3432586/ (6 June 2026)</ref>

As of April 2026, it is expected that the directive could form part of the future EU–UK sanitary and phytosanitary agreement that is being negotiated under prime minister [[Keir Starmer]]'s "[[United Kingdom–European Union relations#EU–UK 'Reset' (2024–)|reset]]" of EU–UK relations, extending the EU rules on marmalades to the UK. The UK government expects the possible agreement to come into force after mid-2027.<ref>{{Cite web |last=White |first=Kevin |title=Burnt toast, marmalade and the politics of the EU reset |url=https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/comment-and-opinion/burnt-toast-marmalade-and-the-politics-of-the-eu-reset/717457.article |access-date=24 May 2026 |website=The Grocer |language=en}}</ref><ref name="Rawlinson2026" /><ref name=":1" /><ref name=":2" />

== In popular culture ==

=== Paddington Bear ===

[[File:"Bear Necessities" Paddington Bear, Great Russell Street - geograph.org.uk - 4262782.jpg|thumb|[[John Hurt]]'s marmalade-themed [[Paddington Bear]] statue in London, auctioned to raise funds for the [[National Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children|NSPCC]] |alt=John Hurt's marmalade-themed Paddington Bear statue in London holding his hat up off his head.|300x300px]]

The fictional character [[Paddington Bear]] is known for his liking of marmalade, particularly in sandwiches, since he keeps it in his hat wherever he goes.<ref>{{cite book |last=Bond |first=Michael |title=Paddington: My Book of Marmalade |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] Children's |others=Illustrated by Peggy Fortnum |year=2008 |isbn=978-0-00-726946-4}}</ref> Paddington Bear is now used on the label of the smaller peel ("shred") and clearer/milder Robertson's "Golden Shred" marmalade, in place of the previous icon, the "[[Golliwog]]", because it was considered racially offensive.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.theguardian.com/media/2001/aug/23/marketingandpr.raceintheuk |newspaper=The Guardian |title=Time runs out for Robertson's golly |first=Owen |last=Bowcott |date=23 August 2001 |access-date=6 September 2025}}</ref> The 2014 movie ''[[Paddington (film)|Paddington]]'' led to a slight increase in marmalade sales in the United Kingdom.<ref name="Grdn">{{cite news |last=Davies |first=Caroline |date=24 February 2017 |title=Marmalade in decline as Paddington struggles to lift sales |url=https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2017/feb/24/marmalade-in-decline-as-paddington-struggles-to-lift-sales |access-date=25 February 2017 |newspaper=[[The Guardian]]}}</ref>

=== Other mentions ===
In [[Agatha Christie]]'s 1953 detective novel ''[[A Pocket Full of Rye]]'', the first victim of the murderer is given poison hidden in orange marmalade consumed at breakfast.<ref>{{cite book |last=Baucekova |first=Silvia |title=Dining Room Detectives: Analysing Food in the Novels of Agatha Christie |publisher=[[Cambridge Scholars Publishing]] |year=2015 |isbn=978-1443877626}}</ref>

In [[Jane Austen]]'s 1811 novel ''[[Sense and Sensibility]]'', an over-indulgent mother feeds apricot marmalade to her fussy three-year-old child who has been slightly scratched by a pin in the mother's hair. Austen in irony of this states, "that she could taste no greater delight than in making a fillagree basket for a spoilt child".<ref>{{Cite web |last=Allen Ford |first=Susan |date=2022 |title=Thoughts on the Education of Daughters in Sense and Sensibility |url=https://jasna.org/publications-2/persuasions-online/volume-43-no-1/ford/ |access-date=6 July 2025 |website=jasna.org}}</ref>
