In [[golf]], '''par''' is the predetermined number of strokes that a proficient (scratch, or zero [[handicap (golf)|handicap]])<ref name=Block/> golfer should require to complete a hole, a round (the sum of the pars of the played holes), or a tournament (the sum of the pars of each round).<ref name=bbcacademy>{{cite news |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/sportacademy/hi/sa/golf/rules/par/newsid_3863000/3863533.stm |title=What is par? |website=BBC Sport Academy |date=September 22, 2004 |access-date=3 September 2020}}</ref><ref name=sn>{{cite web |url=https://www.sportingnews.com/us/other-sports/news/how-is-par-on-a-golf-course-determined/yln8cjdj9lne1trmiqwt8tv15 |title=How is par on a golf course determined? |website=Sporting News |access-date=3 September 2020}}</ref> For scoring purposes, a golfer's number of strokes is compared with the par score to determine how much the golfer was either "over par", "under par", or "even/level par".<ref name="liveabout">{{cite web |url=https://www.liveabout.com/what-does-over-par-mean-1560942 |title=The Meaning of 'Over Par' in Golf, With Scoring Examples |website=liveabout.com|date=24 May 2019|access-date=26 June 2022}}</ref>

Holes are generally assigned par values between three and five based on the distance from the [[teeing ground]] to the [[putting green]], and occasionally other factors such as terrain and obstacles.<ref name=usga/> A typical 18-hole [[golf course]] will have a total par around 72,<ref name=bbcacademy/> and a 9-hole par-3 course (where all holes are rated as par 3) will have a total par of 27.

==Determination of par==
Par is primarily determined by the playing length of each hole from the [[teeing ground]] to the [[putting green]]. Holes are generally assigned par values between three and five, which includes a regulation number of strokes to reach the green based on the average distance a proficient golfer hits the ball, and two [[Golf swing#Putt|putts]].<ref name=sn/> On occasion, factors other than distance are taken into account when setting the par for a hole; these include altitude, terrain and obstacles that result in a hole playing longer or shorter than its measured distance, e.g. route is significantly uphill or downhill, or requiring play of a stroke to finish short of a body of water before hitting over it.<ref name=usga/>

In general, par-3 holes for men will be under {{convert|260|yd|m|-1}} from the tee to the green, par-4 holes will be {{convert|240|-|490|yd|m|-1}}, par-5 holes will be {{convert|450|-|710|yd|m|-1}} and par-6 holes will be over {{convert|670|yd|m|-1}}. For women, par-3 holes will be under {{convert|220|yd|m|-1}} from the tee to the green, par-4 holes will be {{convert|200|-|420|yd|m|-1}}, par-5 holes will be {{convert|370|-|600|yd|m|-1}} and par-6 holes will be over {{convert|570|yd|m|-1}}<ref name=usga>{{cite web |url=https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/handicapping/roh/Content/rules/Appendix%20F%20Establishing%20Par.htm |title=Rules of Handicapping {{!}} Appendix F: Establishing Par |publisher=USGA |access-date=3 September 2020 |archive-date=January 20, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210120030036/https://www.usga.org/content/usga/home-page/handicapping/roh/Content/rules/Appendix%20F%20Establishing%20Par.htm |url-status=dead }}</ref> These boundaries are commonly extended or shortened for elite or non-elite tournament players, who will often encounter par-4 holes of {{convert|500|yd|m|-1}} or more; this is often the result of a normal par-5 hole being rated as a par-4 for them.<ref name=sn/> Par-6 holes are rarely seen in professional competitions, as these will typically rate any hole otherwise designated as a par-6 as a par-5. Some golf courses feature par-7 holes, {{fact|date=October 2024}} but these are not recognized by the [[United States Golf Association]].

==Course and tournament scores==
A golfer's score is compared with the par score.<ref name="liveabout"/> If a course has a par of 72 and a golfer takes 75 strokes to complete the course, the reported score is +3, or "three-over-par" and takes three shots more than par to complete the course. If a golfer takes 70 strokes, the reported score is −2, or "two-under-par".

Tournament scores are reported by totalling scores relative to par in each round (there are usually four rounds in professional tournaments). If each of the four rounds has a par of 72, the tournament par would be 288. For example, a golfer could record a 70 in the first round, a 72 in the second round, a 73 in the third round, and a 69 in the fourth round. That would give a tournament score of 284, or "four-under-par".

==Hole scores==
<!--Add info on the origin of the names Eagle, Albatross, and Condor.-->
Scores on each hole are reported in the same way that course scores are given. Names are commonly given to scores on holes relative to par.

===Par===
A hole score equal to the par of the hole is simply called a par. The term is thought to have originated from the [[stock exchange]], where it was used to describe the expected value of stocks. The transition to golf was made by writer A. H. Doleman prior to [[The Open Championship]] at [[Prestwick Golf Club|Prestwick]] in 1870, when he reported what had been described by [[David Strath]] and [[James Anderson (golfer)|James Anderson]] as the score resulting from "perfect play".<ref name="SGH"/>

===Bogey===
A score of one stroke more than par (+1) for a hole is known as a bogey, e.g. 4 strokes to complete a par 3 hole or 6 strokes on a par 5 hole.<ref name=bbcacademy/>

The original meaning of bogey in golf was the number of strokes a good golfer should take at each hole, and first came into use at the [[Great Yarmouth]] Golf Club in England in about 1890, based on the phrase "[[bogey man]]" and a popular [[music hall]] song "[[Hush, Hush, Hush, Here Comes the Bogeyman|Here Comes the Bogey Man]]".<ref name=SGH/> Players competed against [[Colonel Bogey]], an imaginary player, who scored a predetermined number of strokes on each hole. The winner of the competition was the player who had the best match-play score against Colonel Bogey. The term appeared in print in the 28 November 1891 issue of ''[[The Field (magazine)|The Field]]'', relating to competitions held at the United Services Golf Club, [[Gosport]].<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/bogey |title=bogey / boogie man |date=7 June 2020 |website=www.wordorigins.org}}</ref> The term gave the title to a 1914 British marching tune, "[[Colonel Bogey March]]".<ref>{{cite book |last1 = Harris |first1 = Ed |title = Golf Facts, Figures & Fun |volume = Illustrated |publisher = AAPPL |year = 2007 |isbn = 978-1-904332-65-7 |url-access = registration |url = https://archive.org/details/golf0000harr }}</ref> As golf became more standardised in the United States, par scores were tightened and recreational golfers found themselves scoring over par, with bogey changing meaning to one-over-par.

Scores in excess of one stroke more than par for a hole are known as '''double-bogey''' (two strokes more than par, +2), '''triple-bogey''' (three strokes more than par, +3), and so on. For higher hole scores it is more common for them to be referred to by the number of strokes, or strokes relative to par, rather than as a "''n''-tuple bogey".

It is considered an achievement to complete a bogey-free round. Completing four bogey-free rounds in professional tournament play is rare. Examples are [[Lee Trevino]] at the 1974 [[Greater New Orleans Open]]; [[David J. Russell (golfer)|David J. Russell]] at the 1992 [[Lyon Open V33]]; [[Jesper Parnevik]] at the 1995 [[Volvo Scandinavian Masters]]; [[Manuel Piñero]] at the 2002 [[GIN Monte Carlo Invitational]]; [[Diana Luna]] at the 2011 [[UniCredit Ladies German Open]]; and [[Jonas Blixt]] and [[Cameron Smith (golfer)|Cameron Smith]] at the 2017 [[Zurich Classic of New Orleans]] (a team event). Each of them won the tournament except Piñero, who finished third.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://ladieseuropeantour.com/bogey-free-luna-makes-history-in-germany/ |title=Bogey-free Luna makes history in Germany |publisher=Ladies European Tour |date=May 25, 2011}}</ref>

===Birdie===
A hole score of one stroke fewer than par (one under par, −1) is known as a birdie, e.g. 2 strokes to complete a par 3 hole or 4 strokes on a par 5 hole.<ref name=bbcacademy/> This expression was coined in 1899, at the [[Atlantic City Country Club]] in [[Northfield, New Jersey]]. According to a story that has been passed down, one day in 1899, three golfers, George Crump (who later built [[Pine Valley Golf Club]], about 45 miles away), William Poultney Smith (founding member of Pine Valley), and his brother Ab Smith, were playing together when Crump hit his second shot only inches from the cup on a par-four hole after his first shot had struck a bird in flight. Simultaneously, the Smith brothers exclaimed that Crump's shot was "a bird". Crump's short putt left him one-under-par for the hole, and from that day, the three of them referred to such a score as a "birdie". In short order, the entire membership of the club began using the term. As the Atlantic City Country Club, being a resort, had many out-of-town visitors, the expression spread and caught the fancy of all American golfers.<ref>{{cite book |title = The World of Golf |page = 124 |first = Charles |last = Price |year = 1962 }} Price's book is required reading for all membership applicants to the PGA.</ref>

The [[perfect round]] (score of 54 on a par-72 course) is most commonly described as scoring a birdie on all 18 holes,<ref>{{cite news |url = https://www.nytimes.com/1998/05/10/nyregion/atlantic-city-on-to-the-next-round.html |title = Atlantic Cite: On to the Next Round |newspaper = [[The New York Times]] |date = May 10, 1998 }}</ref><ref>{{cite magazine |url = http://www.golfdigest.com/courses/2008/06/19thhole_atlanticcity |title = Atlantic City Country Club; A golf landmark goes public |magazine = [[Golf Digest]] |date = June 2008 }}</ref> but no player has ever recorded a perfect round in a professional tournament. During the 2009 [[RBC Canadian Open]], [[Mark Calcavecchia]] scored nine consecutive birdies at the second round, breaking the [[PGA Tour]] record.<ref>{{cite news |url = http://www.golf.com/ap-news/calcavecchia-birdies-record-9-straight-holes |title = Calcavecchia birdies record 9 straight holes |work = Golf Magazine |agency = Associated Press |date = July 25, 2009 }}</ref>

===Eagle===
A hole score of two strokes fewer than par (two under par, −2) is known as an eagle, e.g. 2 strokes to complete a par 4 hole or 3 strokes on a par 5 hole.<ref name=bbcacademy/> The name "eagle" was used to represent a better score than a birdie due to it being a relatively [[eagle|large bird]].<ref name=SGH/> An eagle usually occurs when a golfer hits the ball far enough to reach the green with fewer strokes than expected. It most commonly happens on par-fives but can occur on short par-fours. A [[hole in one]] on a par-three hole also results in an eagle.

===Albatross===
{{see also|List of albatrosses in important tournaments}}
A hole score of three strokes fewer than par (three under par, −3) is known as an albatross (the [[albatross]] being one of the largest birds); also called a double eagle in the US, e.g. 2 strokes to complete a par 5 hole.<ref name=bbcacademy/> It is an extremely rare score and occurs most commonly on par-fives with a strong drive and a holed approach shot. Holes-in-one on par-four holes (generally short ones) are also albatrosses, as are scores of three on par-six holes. The first famous albatross was made by [[Gene Sarazen]] in 1935 on the 15th hole at [[Augusta National Golf Club]] during the final round of the [[1935 Masters Tournament|Masters Tournament]]. It vaulted him into a tie for first place and forced a playoff, which he won the next day. The sportswriters of the day termed it "the [[Shot heard 'round the world#Widespread idiomatic use|shot heard 'round the world]]."

Between 1970 and 2003, 84 such shots (averaging about 2.5 per year) were recorded on the [[PGA Tour]].<ref name="rarebird">{{cite magazine |first=Bill |last=Fields |url=http://www.golfdigest.com/majors/masters/index.ssf?/majors/masters/gw20040402albatross.html |title=The Rarest Bird: The albatross took flight at the 1935 Masters, but golf's most unlikely shot isn't easy to find |magazine=Golf World |date=April 2, 2004 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070305063301/http://www.golfdigest.com/majors/masters/index.ssf?%2Fmajors%2Fmasters%2Fgw20040402albatross.html |archive-date=March 5, 2007}}</ref>

Well-publicised albatrosses include those by [[Joey Sindelar]] at the [[2006 PGA Championship]], only the third in that competition's history;<ref>{{cite news |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/sport2/hi/golf/5267360.stm |title=Sindelar plunders rare albatross |date=19 August 2006 |work=BBC Sport |access-date=9 September 2018}}</ref> [[Miguel Ángel Jiménez]] while defending his [[BMW PGA Championship]] title in 2009;<ref>{{cite news |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/sport1/hi/golf/8066190.stm |title=Casey holds on for Wentworth win |date=24 May 2009 |work=BBC Sport |access-date=9 September 2018}}</ref> [[Paul Lawrie]] in the final round of the [[2009 Open Championship]];<ref>{{cite news |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/sport2/hi/golf/8157001.stm |work=BBC Sport |title=Lawrie enjoys albatross at Open |date=July 19, 2009 |access-date=May 21, 2010}}</ref> [[Shaun Micheel]] on the final day of the [[2010 U.S. Open (golf)|2010 U.S. Open]],<ref>{{cite news |url=http://uk.reuters.com/article/idUKN1911463820100620|archive-url=https://archive.today/20120715165424/http://uk.reuters.com/article/idUKN1911463820100620|url-status=dead|archive-date=July 15, 2012|title=Golf-Micheel records second ever U.S. Open albatross |work=Reuters |date=June 20, 2010}}</ref> only the second ever in that competition; [[Pádraig Harrington]] in the [[2010 WGC-HSBC Champions]];<ref>{{cite web |last=Keogh |first=Brian |url=http://www.irishgolfdesk.com/news-files/2010/11/6/albatross-not-enough-for-harrington.html |title=News: Albatross not enough for Harrington |publisher=Irish Golf Desk |date=November 6, 2010 |access-date=October 1, 2012 }}</ref> [[Louis Oosthuizen]] on the final day of the [[2012 Masters Tournament]], the fourth in that competition's history, the first to be televised, and the first on Augusta's par-five second hole;<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/sport/0/golf/17648976 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120408203350/http://www.bbc.co.uk/sport/0/golf/17648976 |url-status=dead |archive-date=April 8, 2012 |title=2012 Masters: Day four as it happened |work=BBC Sport |date=April 9, 2012 |access-date=October 1, 2012}}</ref> [[Rafa Cabrera-Bello]] at the [[2017 Players Championship]]; and [[Brooks Koepka]] at the [[2018 Players Championship]].<ref>{{cite news |url=http://www.espn.com/golf/story/_/id/23492948/brooks-koepka-ties-tpc-record-9-63-sunday |title=Brooks Koepka makes albatross, ties TPC record with 9-under 63 |work=ESPN |first=Michael |last=DiRocco |date=May 13, 2018 |access-date=May 13, 2018}}</ref>

[[Andrew Magee]] made the only par-four hole-in-one in PGA Tour history at the [[Phoenix Open]] in 2001.<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Myers |first=Alex |title=Did you know: There has only been one hole-in-one on a par 4 in PGA Tour history—and it was a real doozy |url=https://www.golfdigest.com/story/did-you-know-there-has-only-been-one-hole-in-one-on-a-par-4-in-pga-tour-history |magazine=Golf Digest |date=April 17, 2020 |access-date=21 December 2023}}</ref>

===Condor===
{{see also|Hole in one#Holes-in-one on par 5 (or higher) holes}}
Condor is an unofficial name for a hole score of four strokes fewer than par (four under par, −4). A condor is also known as a double albatross, or a triple eagle.<ref name=Condor01/><ref name=Condor02/> This is the lowest individual hole score ever made, relative to par. A condor would be a hole-in-one on a par-five (typically by cutting over a dogleg corner), a two on a par-six,<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.eastbaytimes.com/2021/01/12/east-bay-man-achieves-golfs-rarest-shot-ever/ |title=East Bay man achieves golf's rarest shot ever |newspaper=East Bay Times |date=January 12, 2021 |first=Jon |last=Becker |access-date=January 12, 2021}}</ref> or a three on a par-seven (which is not known to have been achieved).<ref name=Condor01/> Par-sixes are exceptionally rare,<ref name=Par6Kelley02>{{cite web |title=Par 6 (Par-6 Hole) |url=http://golf.about.com/od/golfterms/g/par-6-hole.htm |first=Brent |last=Kelley |publisher=[[About.com]] Golf |access-date=August 24, 2014 |quote=As noted, par-6 holes are rare, with most golf courses having only par-3, par-4 and par-5 holes. Most recreational golfers go their entire golfing careers without ever seeing a par 6. |archive-date=July 12, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140712221007/http://golf.about.com/od/golfterms/g/par-6-hole.htm |url-status=dead }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.worldgolf.com/course-reviews/north-carolina/farmstead-golf-links.htm |title=Farmstead Golf Links, Calabash |department=North Carolina Course Reviews |publisher=Worldgolf.com |date=January 29, 2002 |access-date=October 1, 2012 }}</ref> as are par-sevens.<ref name=par7-01a>{{Cite magazine |url=http://www.esquire.com/features/travel/ESQ0903_golf |title=The World's Longest Golf Hole |magazine=[[Esquire (magazine)|Esquire]] |first=Victoria |last=Robb |date=April 13, 2007 |access-date=December 25, 2014 |quote=According to ''The Guinness Book of World Records'', the Satsuki golf course in Sano, Japan, boasts the longest hole in the world—an exhausting 964-yard, par-7 humdinger.}}</ref><ref>{{Cite magazine |last=Dethier |first=Dylan |date=2018-11-16 |title=The longest hole on Earth is an 1100-yard par-7 at Gunsan Country Club |url=https://golf.com/travel/longest-golf-hole-par-seven-south-korea/ |access-date=2023-09-14 |magazine=Golf Magazine}}</ref>

As of May 2023, a condor had been recorded only five times on a par-5,<ref name=Condor03>{{cite web |url=https://thegolfnewsnet.com/golfnewsnetteam/2021/01/13/what-is-a-condor-in-golf-we-explain-the-rarest-of-birds-in-golf-121666/ |title=What is a condor in golf? We explain the rarest of birds in golf? |website=www.thegolfnewsnet.com |access-date=August 25, 2021}}</ref> once reportedly on a straight drive (a record {{convert|517|yd|m|0}}), aided by the thin air at high altitude in [[Denver]]).<ref name=Condor01>{{cite web |title=Condor |work=Golf Today |date=October 2008 |url=https://golftoday.co.uk/golf-a-to-z-the-condor/}}</ref> Another was reportedly achieved with a 3-iron club (in 1995 on a horseshoe-shaped par-5 hole).<ref name=Condor01/><ref name=Condor02>{{cite web |title=Has There Ever Been a Hole-in-One on a Par-5 Hole? |url=http://golf.about.com/od/faqs/f/par5aces.htm |first=Brent |last=Kelley |publisher=[[About.com]] Golf |access-date=August 24, 2014 |quote=One was even recorded with a 3-iron! That one was made by Shaun Lynch, playing at Teign Valley Golf Club in Christow, England, in 1995, on the 496-yard No. 17. According to a 2004 article in Golf World magazine, Lynch aimed straight toward the green on a '''horseshoe''' par-5, clearing a 20-foot-high hedge, then hitting a downslope on the other side. The downslope carried his ball to the green and into the cup. |archive-date=February 14, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170214061853/http://golf.about.com/od/faqs/f/par5aces.htm |url-status=dead }}</ref> In December 2020, a condor on a par-6 was recorded on the 18th hole at [[Lake Chabot Regional Park|Lake Chabot Golf Course]] in [[Oakland, California]].<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.mercurynews.com/2021/01/12/east-bay-man-achieves-golfs-rarest-shot-ever/ |title=East Bay man achieves golf's rarest shot ever |newspaper=Mercury News |date=January 12, 2021 |access-date=August 25, 2021}}</ref> A condor has never been achieved during a professional tournament.

==Scorecard==
