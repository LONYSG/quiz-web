[[Image:Booba-Kiki.svg|thumb|right|250px|alt=A spiky geometric shape (left) and a rounded geometric shape (right)|This picture is used as a test to demonstrate that people may not attach sounds to shapes arbitrarily. When given the names "kiki" and "bouba", many cultural and linguistic communities worldwide robustly tend to label the shape on the left "kiki" and the one on the right "bouba".]]

The '''bouba&ndash;kiki effect''' ({{IPAc-en|ˈ|b|u:|b|ə|_|ˈ|k|i:|k|i:}} {{respell|BOO|bə|_|KEE|kee}}), or '''takete&ndash;maluma''' '''phenomenon''',<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Nielsen |first1=Alan |last2=Rendall |first2=Drew |date=2011 |title=The sound of round: evaluating the sound-symbolic role of consonants in the classic Takete-Maluma phenomenon. |journal=Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology |volume=65 |issue=2 |pages=115–124|doi=10.1037/a0022268 |pmid=21668094 }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Occelli |first1=Valeria |last2=Esposito |first2=Gianluca |last3=Venuti |first3=Paola |last4=Arduino |first4=Giuseppe Maurizio |last5=Zampini |first5=Massimiliano |date=2013 |title=The Takete—Maluma Phenomenon in Autism Spectrum Disorders |url=https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p7357 |journal=Perception |language=en |volume=42 |issue=2 |pages=233–241 |doi=10.1068/p7357 |pmid=23700961 |issn=0301-0066|url-access=subscription }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Rogers |first1=Susan K |last2=Ross |first2=Abraham S |date=1975 |title=A Cross-Cultural Test of the Maluma—Takete Phenomenon |url=https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p040105 |journal=Perception |language=en |volume=4 |issue=1 |pages=105–106 |doi=10.1068/p040105 |pmid=1161435 |issn=0301-0066 |url-access=subscription |archive-date=12 February 2023 |access-date=12 September 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230212051355/https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p040105 |url-status=live }}</ref> is a non-arbitrary [[Association (psychology)|mental association]] between certain speech sounds and certain visual [[Shape|shapes]]. The most typical research finding is that people, when presented with [[nonsense word]]s, tend to associate certain "soft" sounds (like ''bouba'' and ''maluma'') with a rounded shape and other "hard" sounds (like ''kiki'' and ''takete'') with a spiky shape. Its discovery dates back to the 1920s, when psychologists [[Dimitri Uznadze|Uznadze]] and [[Wolfgang Köhler|Köhler]] documented experimental participants as connecting [[nonsense]] words to shapes in consistent ways.

There is a strong general tendency towards the effect worldwide. It has been robustly confirmed across a majority of cultures and languages in which it has been researched; examples include English-speaking American university students, [[Tamil language|Tamil]] speakers in India, speakers of certain languages with no [[Writing system|writing system]], young children, infants, and (though to a much lesser degree) the [[Congenital blindness|congenitally blind]]. It has also been shown to occur with familiar names. The effect has also been shown to extend to chickens and dogs. The bouba–kiki effect is one form of [[sound symbolism]].

==Research==
===Discovery===
This effect was first observed by Georgian psychologist [[Dimitri Uznadze]] in a 1924 paper.<ref name="usnadze">{{cite web |last=Usnadze |first=Dimitri |date=15 November 1923 |title=Ein experimenteller Beitrag zum Problem der psychologischen Grundlagen der Namengebung |trans-title=An Experimental Contribution to the Problem of the Psychological Foundations of Naming |url=https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/210861794.pdf |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221127165459/https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/210861794.pdf |archive-date=27 November 2022 |access-date=18 April 2023 |series=Psychologische Forschung, Vol. 5 |pages=24-43 |language=de |publication-date=1924 |via=core.ac.uk}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Glenney |first=Brian |url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/A_Pluralist_s_Guide_to_Solving_Molyneux/4a40EQAAQBAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&dq=Uznadze+bouba+kiki&pg=PA118&printsec=frontcover |title=A Pluralist's Guide to Solving Molyneux's Problem |date= |publisher=Taylor & Francis |year=2024 |isbn=978-1-040-29621-9 |page=118 |language=}}</ref> He conducted an experiment with 10 participants who were given a list with nonsense words, shown six drawings for five seconds each, then instructed to pick a name for the drawing from the list of given words. He describes the different "strategies" participants developed to match words to drawings and quotes their reasoning. He also describes situations where participants described very specific forms that they associated with a nonsense word, without reference to the shown drawings. He develops a theory of four factors that influence the way names for objects are decided.

In total, there were 42 words. For one particular drawing, 45% picked the same word. For three others, the percentages were 40%. Uznadze points out that this is significantly more overlap than one could expect, given the high number of possible words. He speculates that there must therefore be certain regularities "which the human soul follows in the process of name-giving".<ref name="usnadze"/>

German American psychologist [[Wolfgang Köhler]] referred to Uznadze's experiment in his 1929 book ''Gestalt Psychology''<ref name=Kohler1929>{{cite book |last=Köhler |first=Wolfgang |year=1929 |title=Gestalt Psychology. |url=https://archive.org/details/gestaltpsycholog0000kohl |url-access=registration |location=New York |publisher=Liveright}}</ref> which showed two forms and asked readers which shape was called "takete" and which was called "maluma". Although he does not say so outright, Köhler implies that there is a strong preference to pair the jagged shape with "takete" and the rounded shape with "maluma".<ref>{{cite book |last=Köhler |first=Wolfgang|year=1947 |title=Gestalt Psychology |url=https://archive.org/details/gestaltpsycholog00kh |url-access=registration |edition=2nd |location=New York |publisher=Liveright |page=[https://archive.org/details/gestaltpsycholog00kh/page/132 133]}}</ref>

In 2001, [[V. S. Ramachandran]] and Edward Hubbard at the [[University of California, San Diego]], presented the words "kiki" and "bouba" along with new figures as alternatives to the stimuli used by Köhler. They stated that when asked to guess, 95% of people will select the curvy shape as "bouba" and the jagged one as "kiki".<ref name="Rama2001">{{cite journal |last1=Ramachandran |first1=V.S. |last2=Hubbard |first2=E.M. |name-list-style=amp |year=2001 |title=Synaesthesia: A window into perception, thought and language |journal=[[Journal of Consciousness Studies]] |volume=8 |issue=12 |pages=3–34 |url=http://cbc.ucsd.edu/pdf/Synaesthesia%20-%20JCS.pdf |access-date=20 October 2011 |archive-date=13 August 2011 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110813064348/http://cbc.ucsd.edu/pdf/Synaesthesia%20-%20JCS.pdf }}</ref>

===Extension to other contexts===
====Cross-language contexts====

There is a strong general tendency towards the effect worldwide. It has been robustly confirmed across a majority of cultures and languages in which it has been researched. A 2022 study by Cwiek et al, that found evidence supporting the idea that the bouba/kiki effect is a [[Cross-cultural studies|cross-cultural phenomenon]].<ref name="Cwiek">{{cite journal |title=The ''bouba/Kiki'' effect is robust across cultures and writing systems |date=2022 |last1=Ćwiek |first1=Aleksandra |last2=Fuchs |first2=Susanne |last3=Draxler |first3=Christoph |last4=Asu |first4=Eva Liina |last5=Dediu |first5=Dan |last6=Hiovain |first6=Katri |last7=Kawahara |first7=Shigeto |last8=Koutalidis |first8=Sofia |last9=Krifka |first9=Manfred |last10=Lippus |first10=Pärtel |last11=Lupyan |first11=Gary |last12=Oh |first12=Grace E. |last13=Paul |first13=Jing |last14=Petrone |first14=Caterina |last15=Ridouane |first15=Rachid |last16=Reiter |first16=Sabine |last17=Schümchen |first17=Nathalie |last18=Szalontai |first18=Ádám |last19=Ünal-Logacev |first19=Özlem |last20=Zeller |first20=Jochen |last21=Perlman |first21=Marcus |last22=Winter |first22=Bodo |journal=Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences |volume=377 |issue=1841 |article-number=20200390 |doi=10.1098/rstb.2020.0390 |pmid=34775818 |pmc=8591387 }} The paper examines cross-linguistic data supporting the bouba/kiki effect and explores its potential implications for understanding iconicity in language.</ref> 917 participants speaking 25 different languages, with 10 different writing systems, maintain a higher than chance consistency in bouba/kiki identification, intuitively associating the "bouba" with a rounded shape and "kiki" with a sharp, pointed shape, regardless of their native language, though the effect is stronger in some languages than others. It also supports that [[Roman orthography]] is a factor that could enhance the bouba/kiki effect. However, this biasing effect of orthography is rather weak since the participants that speak languages with Roman orthography are only marginally more likely to show the bouba/kiki effect.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Hurford |first=James R. |url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_Origins_of_Grammar/ce11HQC-8NoC?hl=en&gbpv=1&dq=roman+orthography+bouba+kiki&pg=PA126&printsec=frontcover |title=The Origins of Grammar: Language in the Light of Evolution II |date= |publisher=Oxford University Press |year=2012 |isbn=978-0-19-920787-9 |location=New York |page=126 |language=}}</ref>

====Cross-age contexts====
[[Daphne Maurer]] and colleagues showed that even children as young as 2{{1/2}} years old may show this preference.<ref>{{cite journal |author-link=Daphne Maurer |last1=Maurer |first1=Daphne |last2=Pathman |first2=Thanujeni |last3=Mondloch |first3=Catherine J. |name-list-style=amp |year=2006 |title=The shape of boubas: Sound-shape correspondences in toddlers and adults |journal=Developmental Science |volume=9 |issue=3 |pages=316–322 |doi=10.1111/j.1467-7687.2006.00495.x |pmid=16669803 |s2cid=7297731 |url=http://psych.mcmaster.ca/maurerlab/Publications/Maurer_bouba.pdf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110723035944/http://psych.mcmaster.ca/maurerlab/Publications/Maurer_bouba.pdf |archive-date=23 July 2011 |access-date=19 June 2011 }}</ref>

Work by Özge Öztürk and colleagues in 2013 showed that even 4-month-old infants have the same sound–shape mapping biases as adults and toddlers.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Ozturk |first1=Ozge |last2=Krehm |first2=Madelaine |last3=Vouloumanos |first3=Athena |title=Sound symbolism in infancy: Evidence for sound–shape cross-modal correspondences in 4-month-olds |journal=Journal of Experimental Child Psychology |date=2013 |volume=114 |issue=2 |pages=173–186 |doi=10.1016/j.jecp.2012.05.004 |pmid=22960203 |s2cid=7274252 |url=http://www.psych.nyu.edu/niccl/publicationlinks/OzturkKrehmVouloumanos_JECP_InPress.pdf |access-date=24 September 2019 |archive-date=17 August 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200817164233/http://www.psych.nyu.edu/niccl/publicationlinks/OzturkKrehmVouloumanos_JECP_InPress.pdf }}</ref> Infants are able to differentiate between congruent trials (pairing an angular shape with "kiki" or a curvy shape with "bubu") and incongruent trials (pairing a curvy shape with "kiki" or an angular shape with "bubu"). Infants looked longer at incongruent pairings than at congruent pairings. Infants' mapping was based on the combination of [[Consonant|consonants]] and [[Vowel|vowels]] in the words, and neither consonants nor vowels alone sufficed for mapping. 

These results suggest that some sound–shape mappings precede [[Language acquisition|language learning]], and may in fact aid in language learning by establishing a basis for matching labels to referents and narrowing the hypothesis space for young infants. Adults in this study, like infants, used a combination of consonant and vowel information to match the labels they heard with the shapes they saw. However, this was not the only strategy that was available to them. Adults, unlike infants, were also able to use consonant information alone and vowel information alone to match the labels to the shapes, albeit less frequently than the consonant–vowel combination. When vowels and consonants were put in conflict, adults used consonants more often than vowels.

====Other contexts where the effect is present====
The effect has also been shown to emerge in other contexts, such as when words are paired with evaluative meanings (with "bouba" words associated with positive concepts and "kiki" words associated with negative concepts)<ref>{{cite conference|last=Bross |first=Fabian |date=2018 |title=The Good, the Bad, the Bouba, and the Kiki. Cross-Modal Correspondences Between Evaluative Meanings, Speech-Sounds, and Object Shapes|book-title=14th conference "Phonetics & Phonology in the German-Speaking World"|location=University of Vienna|doi=10.13140/RG.2.2.11463.14240}}</ref> or when the words to be paired are existing first names, suggesting that some familiarity with the linguistic stimuli does not eliminate the effect. A study showed that individuals will pair names such as "Molly" with round silhouettes, and names such as "Kate" with sharp silhouettes. Moreover, individuals will associate different personality traits with either group of names (e.g., easygoingness with "round names"; determination with "sharp names"). This may hint at a role of abstract concepts in the effect.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Sidhu|first1=David M.|last2=Pexman|first2=Penny M.|date=27 May 2015|title=What's in a Name? Sound Symbolism and Gender in First Names|journal=PLOS ONE|volume=10|issue=5|article-number=e0126809|doi=10.1371/journal.pone.0126809|issn=1932-6203|pmc=4446333|pmid=26016856|bibcode=2015PLoSO..1026809S|doi-access=free}}</ref>

==== Animals ====
A 2026 study at the [[University of Padua]] discovered the effect was also present in one- and three-day-old baby chickens.<ref name="npr26">{{cite news |last1=Greenfieldboyce |first1=Nell |title=Baby chicks link certain sounds with shapes, just like humans do |url=https://www.npr.org/2026/02/19/nx-s1-5717039/baby-chicks-link-certain-sounds-with-shapes-just-like-humans-do |access-date=19 February 2026 |work=NPR |date=19 February 2026}}</ref><ref name="sciamer26">{{cite news |last1=Cottier |first1=Cody |title='Mind-blowing' baby chick study challenges a theory of how humans evolved language |url=https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/baby-chicks-pass-the-bouba-kiki-test-challenging-a-theory-of-language/ |access-date=19 February 2026 |work=Scientific American |date=19 February 2026}}</ref> A 2026 study undertaken at the [[University of Parma]] showed that the effect was also seen in adult dogs.<ref name="dogs">{{Cite journal |last=Woodford |first=James |date=14 October 2026 |title=Dogs are susceptible to the bouba-kiki effect |url=https://www.newscientist.com/article/2593290-dogs-are-susceptible-to-the-bouba-kiki-effect/ |journal=[[New Scientist]]}}</ref>

===Contexts where the effect is smaller or absent===
Other research suggests that this effect does not occur in all communities,<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Rogers|first1=Susan K.|last2=Ross|first2=Abraham S.|date=1975|title=A cross-cultural test of the maluma–takete phenomenon|journal=Perception|volume=4|issue=1|pages=105–106|doi=10.1068/p040105|pmid=1161435|s2cid=30045028}}</ref> and it appears that the effect breaks if the sounds do not make licit words in the language.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Styles|first1=Suzy|last2=Gawne|first2=Lauren|date=2017|title=When Does Maluma/Takete Fail? Two Key Failures and a Meta-Analysis Suggest That Phonology and Phonotactics Matter|journal=i-Perception|volume=8 |issue=4|page=204166951772480|doi=10.1177/2041669517724807|pmid=28890777|pmc=5574486}}</ref> The bouba–kiki effect seems to be dependent on a long [[Critical period|sensitive period]], with high visual capacities in childhood being necessary for its typical development. Although the [[Congenital blindness|congenitally blind]] have been reported to show a bouba–kiki effect, they show a much smaller one for touched shapes than sighted individuals do for visual shapes.<ref name="Cwiek"/><ref name="Fryer2014">{{cite journal |last1=Fryer |first1=Louise |last2=Freeman |first2=Jonathan |last3=Pring |first3=Linda |name-list-style=amp |year=2014 |title=Touching words is not enough: How visual experience influences haptic–auditory associations in the "Bouba–Kiki" effect. |journal=Cognition |volume=132 |issue=2 |pages=164–173 |doi=10.1016/j.cognition.2014.03.015 |pmid=24809744 |s2cid=29605784 |url=http://research.gold.ac.uk/10499/1/PSY-Fryer-Freeman-Pring-2014.pdf |archive-date=2 July 2020 |access-date=30 November 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200702141426/http://research.gold.ac.uk/10499/1/PSY-Fryer-Freeman-Pring-2014.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Hamilton-Fletcher2018">{{cite journal |last1=Hamilton-Fletcher |first1=Giles |last2=Pisanski |first2=Katarzyna |last3=Reby |first3=David |last4=Stefańczyk |first4=Michał |last5=Ward |first5=Jamie |last6=Sorokowska |first6=Agnieszka |name-list-style=amp |year=2018 |title=The role of visual experience in the emergence of cross-modal correspondences. |journal=Cognition |volume=175 |pages=114–121 |doi=10.1016/j.cognition.2018.02.023 |pmid=29502009 |s2cid=3688492 |url=http://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/74197/1/__smbhome.uscs.susx.ac.uk_ellenaj_Desktop_SRO_after%20august_blnd%20correspondences%20cognition%20accepted%20version.pdf |archive-date=28 April 2019 |access-date=30 November 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190428142343/http://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/74197/1/__smbhome.uscs.susx.ac.uk_ellenaj_Desktop_SRO_after%20august_blnd%20correspondences%20cognition%20accepted%20version.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Languages with little observed effect===
A major 2022 study by Cwiek et al showed that certain languages, namely [[Mandarin Chinese]], [[Turkish language|Turkish]], [[Romanian language|Romanian]], and [[Albanian language|Albanian]], on average showed lower-than-50% matches for both associating bouba with roundedness and kiki with jaggedness. However, the authors consider their analysis conservative and not clear enough to confirm if these four definitively lacked the bouba&ndash;kiki phenomenon. For example, the phonetic structures of these languages or their participants' cultural associations with sound and shape could have led to the weaker correlations observed.<ref name="Cwiek" />

===Neuroscience===
In 2019, Nathan Peiffer-Smadja and Laurent Cohen published the first study using [[Functional magnetic resonance imaging|fMRI]] to explore the bouba–kiki effect.<ref name=":0">{{Cite journal|date=1 February 2019|title=The cerebral bases of the bouba-kiki effect|journal=NeuroImage|volume=186|pages=679–689|doi=10.1016/j.neuroimage.2018.11.033|issn=1053-8119|last1=Peiffer-Smadja|first1=Nathan|last2=Cohen|first2=Laurent|pmid=30503933|s2cid=54164828|doi-access=free}}</ref> They found that prefrontal activation is stronger to mismatching (bouba with spiky shape) than to matching (bouba with round shape) stimuli. A subsequent study by Kelly McCormick and colleagues reported a similar pattern of greater activation for mismatched word-shape stimuli, but with most activity in [[Parietal region|parietal regions]] including the [[intraparietal sulcus]] and [[supramarginal gyrus]], regions known to play a role in sensory association and perceptual-motor processing.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=McCormick|first1=Kelly|last2=Lacey|first2=Simon|last3=Stilla|first3=Randall|last4=Nygaard|first4=Lynne C.|last5=Sathian|first5=K.|date=11 August 2021|title=Neural Basis of the Sound-Symbolic Crossmodal Correspondence Between Auditory Pseudowords and Visual Shapes|journal=Multisensory Research|volume=-1|issue=aop|pages=29–78|doi=10.1163/22134808-bja10060|pmid=34384048|pmc=9196751 |s2cid=236998825|issn=2213-4794}}</ref> Peiffer-Smadja and Cohen also found that sound-shape matching also influences activations in the auditory and visual cortices, suggesting an effect of matching at an early stage in [[sensory processing]].<ref name=":0" />

===Implications for understanding language===
The bouba–kiki effect is one form of [[sound symbolism]].<ref>{{Cite journal |author=Margiotoudi Konstantina and Pulvermüller Friedemann |date=2020|title=Action sound–shape congruencies explain sound symbolism |journal=Scientific Reports |volume=10 |issue=1 |page=12706  |doi=10.1038/s41598-020-69528-4|doi-access=free |pmid=32728096 |pmc=7392762 |bibcode=2020NatSR..1012706M |url=https://www.proquest.com/docview/2428279185 |id={{ProQuest|2428279185}}}}</ref>

Ramachandran and Hubbard suggest that the kiki/bouba effect has implications for the evolution of language, because it suggests that the naming of objects is not completely arbitrary.<ref name = "Rama2001" />{{rp|17}} The rounded shape may most commonly be named "bouba" because the mouth makes a more rounded shape to produce that sound while a more taut, angular mouth shape is needed to make the sounds in "kiki".<ref>{{cite journal|last1=D'Onofrio|first1=Annette|title=Phonetic Detail and Dimensionality in Sound-shape Correspondences: Refining the ''Bouba-Kiki'' Paradigm|journal=Language and Speech|date=2013|volume=57|issue=3|pages=367–393|doi=10.1177/0023830913507694|s2cid=51937587}}</ref> Alternatively, the distinction may be between [[Coronal consonant|coronal]] or [[Dorsal consonant|dorsal]] consonants like {{IPAslink|k}} and [[Labial consonant|labial]] consonants like {{IPAslink|b}},<ref>{{Cite conference|last1=McCormick|first1=Kelly|last2=Kim|first2=Jee Young|last3=List|first3=Sara|last4=Nygaard|first4=Lynne C.|date=2015|title=Sound to Meaning Mappings in the Bouba-Kiki Effect|url=https://mindmodeling.org/cogsci2015/papers/0273/paper0273.pdf|book-title=Proceedings of the 37th Annual Conference of the Cognitive Science Society: Mind, Technology, and Society: Pasadena, California, 23–25 July 2015|location=Austin, TX|publisher=Cognitive Science Society|pages=1565–1570|isbn=978-0-9911967-2-2|access-date=8 February 2019|archive-date=9 February 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190209124406/https://mindmodeling.org/cogsci2015/papers/0273/paper0273.pdf}}</ref> or, as Fort and Schwartz suggest, the difference may be attributed to the noise a "bouba" shape makes when bounced (lower frequency and more continuous) in comparison to a spiked object.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Fort |first1=Mathilde |last2=Schwartz |first2=Jean-Luc |date=2022-11-10 |title=Resolving the bouba-kiki effect enigma by rooting iconic sound symbolism in physical properties of round and spiky objects |journal=Scientific Reports |language=en |volume=12 |issue=1 |page=19172 |doi=10.1038/s41598-022-23623-w  |doi-access=free|issn=2045-2322 |pmc=9649795 |pmid=36357511 |bibcode=2022NatSR..1219172F }}</ref> 

Additionally, it was shown that it is not only different consonants (e.g., voiceless versus voiced) and different vowel qualities (e.g., {{IPAslink|a}} versus {{IPAslink|i}}) that play a role in the effect, but also vowel quantity (long versus short vowels). In one study, participants rated words containing long vowels to refer to longer objects and short vowels to short objects, at least for languages that make a [[vowel length]] distinction.<ref>{{cite conference|last=Bross |first=Fabian |year=2018 |url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323749384 |title=Cognitive associations between vowel length and object size: A new feature contributing to a bouba/kiki effect |editor1-last=Belz |editor1-first=M. |editor2-last=Mooshammer |editor2-first=C. |editor3-last=Fuchs |editor3-first=S. |editor4-last= Jannedy |editor4-first= S. |editor5-last=Rasskazova |editor5-first=O. |editor6-last=Zygis |editor6-first=M. |book-title=Proceedings of the Conference on Phonetics & Phonology in German-Speaking Countries |volume=13|location=Berlin |publisher=Humbold University |pages=17–20}}</ref>  The presence of these "[[synesthesia]]-like mappings" suggest that this effect may be the neurological basis for [[sound symbolism]], in which sounds are non-arbitrarily mapped to objects and events in the world. Research has also indicated that the effect may be a case of [[ideasthesia]],<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Gómez Milán |first1=E. |last2=Iborra |first2=O. |last3=de Córdoba |first3=M.J. |last4=Juárez-Ramos |first4=V. |last5=Rodríguez Artacho |first5=M.A. |last6=Rubio |first6=J.L. |year=2013 |title=The Kiki-Bouba effect: A case of personification and ideaesthesia |journal=Journal of Consciousness Studies |volume=20 |issue=1–2 |pages=84–102 |url=https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/imp/jcs/2013/00000020/F0020001/art00005 |url-access=subscription |archive-date=24 February 2019 |access-date=23 February 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190224005242/https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/imp/jcs/2013/00000020/F0020001/art00005 |url-status=live }}</ref> a phenomenon in which activations of concepts (inducers) evoke perception-like experiences (concurrents). The name comes from the Greek ''idea'' and ''aisthesis'', meaning "sensing concepts" or "sensing ideas", and was introduced by in 2009 Danko Nikolić.<ref>{{cite conference|last1=Nikolić|first1=Danko|title=Is synaesthesia actually ideaestesia? An inquiry into the nature of the phenomenon|book-title=Proceedings of the Third International Congress on Synaesthesia, Science & Art|date=2009|url=http://www.danko-nikolic.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/09/Synesthesia2009-Nikolic-Ideaesthesia.pdf|access-date=23 February 2019|archive-date=12 July 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190712210332/http://www.danko-nikolic.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/09/Synesthesia2009-Nikolic-Ideaesthesia.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> However, it has been reported that the bouba-kiki effect is also exhibited by newly hatched chickens<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Loconsole| first1 = M. | last2 = Benavides-Varela | first2 = S. | last3 = Regolin | first3 = L. | year = 2026 |title= Matching sounds to shapes: Evidence of the bouba-kiki effect in naïve baby chicks. | journal = Science | volume = 391 | issue = 391| pages = 836–839 | doi = 10.1126/science.adq7188 }}</ref> and in adult dogs.<ref name="dogs"/> Therefore, such sound-shape correspondences may belong to a set of innate cross-modal associations that are shared across species, rather than being a speech-related phenomenon that is distinctive to humans.
