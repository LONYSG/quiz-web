{{Short description|Building material used in ancient Rome}}
[[File:Rome-Pantheon-Interieur1.jpg|right|thumb|upright=1.1|The [[Pantheon, Rome|Pantheon]] in [[Rome]] is an example of Roman concrete construction.|alt= picture showing the concrete underside of a large dome]]
[[File:Caesarea Concrete Bath.jpg|thumbnail|300px|[[Caesarea Maritima|Caesarea]] harbour in [[Roman Judaea]], an example of underwater Roman concrete technology on a large scale]]

'''Roman concrete''', also called '''{{lang|la|opus caementicium}}''', was used in construction in [[ancient Rome]]. Like its [[Concrete|modern equivalent]], Roman concrete was based on a [[hydraulic cement|hydraulic-setting cement]] added to an [[Construction aggregate|aggregate]]. 

Many buildings and structures still standing today, such as bridges, reservoirs and aqueducts, were built with this material, which attests to both its versatility and its durability. It was previously thought that its strength was enhanced by the incorporation of [[Pozzolana|pozzolanic ash]] where available (particularly in the [[Bay of Naples]]). The addition of ash prevented cracks from spreading. However, research in 2023 has shown that the incorporation of mixtures of different types of lime, forming conglomerate "clasts" allowed the concrete to self-repair cracks, which was more likely the case.<ref name=chandler>{{cite news|title=Riddle solved: Why was Roman concrete so durable? |first=David L.|last=Chandler |website=MIT News|url=https://news.mit.edu/2023/roman-concrete-durability-lime-casts-0106 |date=6 January 2023 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://archive.today/20230121175003/https://techxplore.com/news/2023-01-riddle-roman-concrete-durable.html |archive-date=21 January 2023}}</ref>

Roman concrete was in widespread use from about 150 BC;<ref name=":0">{{Cite web|title=National Pozzolan Association: The History of Natural Pozzolans|url=https://pozzolan.org/history-pozzolans.html|access-date=2021-02-21|website=pozzolan.org}}</ref> some scholars believe it was developed a century before that.<ref>{{cite book |author1-link=Axel Boëthius |last1=Boëthius |first1=Axel |author-link2=Roger Ling |last2=Ling |first2= Roger |author-link3=Tom Rasmussen |last3=Rasmussen |first3=Tom |chapter=Etruscan and Early Roman Architecture |pages=128–129 |title=Yale/Pelican history of art |date= 1978 |publisher= Yale University Press |isbn=978-0-300-05290-9}}</ref>

It was often used in combination with facings and other supports,<ref name="romanaqueducts.info">{{cite web |title=Aqua Clopedia, a picture dictionary on Roman aqueducts: Roman concrete / opus caementicium |website=romanaqueducts.info |url=http://www.romanaqueducts.info/picturedictionary/pd_onderwerpen/concrete2.htm |access-date=2023-01-24}}</ref> and interiors were further decorated by [[stucco]], [[fresco]] paintings, or colored marble. Further innovative developments in the material, part of the so-called [[Roman architectural revolution|concrete revolution]], contributed to structurally complicated forms. The most prominent example of these is the [[Pantheon, Rome|Pantheon]] dome, the world's largest and oldest unreinforced concrete dome.<ref name="Ancient Roman Concrete">{{cite web |last=Moore |first=David |url=http://www.romanconcrete.com/docs/spillway/spillway.htm |title=The Riddle of Ancient Roman Concrete |work=S Dept. of the Interior, Bureau of Reclamation, Upper Colorado Region |date=February 1993 |access-date=20 May 2013}}</ref>

Roman concrete differs from modern concrete in that the aggregates often included larger components; hence, it was laid rather than poured.<ref>{{cite book |editor-last=Henig |editor-first= Martin  |title=A Handbook of Roman Art |page=30 |publisher=Phaidon |date=1983 |isbn=0-7148-2214-0}}</ref> Roman concretes, like any hydraulic concrete, were usually able to set underwater, which was useful for bridges and other waterside construction.

==History==
[[File:Baia-Tempio di Mercurio.jpg|thumb|right|alt=Bare concrete dome interior today called the Temple of Mercury with two square windows halfway up the dome on the far side, a circular [[Oculus (architecture)|oculus]] at the top, and a water level that reaches up to the base of the dome|The "Temple of Mercury" in [[Baiae]], a Roman {{lang|la|[[frigidarium]]}} pool of a [[thermae|bathhouse]] built in the 1st century BC<ref>{{cite encyclopedia |url=https://www.britannica.com/topic/Temple-of-Mercury |title= Baiae, historic site, Italy |encyclopedia =[[Encyclopedia Britannica]]}}</ref> containing the oldest surviving [[Ancient Roman and Byzantine domes|concrete dome]],{{sfn|Lancaster|2009|page=40}} and largest one before the [[Pantheon, Rome|Pantheon]]<ref>{{cite journal| last1 = Mark| first1 = Robert| last2 = Hutchinson| first2 = Paul| date = March 1986| title = On the Structure of the Roman Pantheon| journal = The Art Bulletin| volume = 68| number = 1| publisher = College Art Association| location = New York, NY| jstor = 3050861| page = 24| doi=10.2307/3050861}}</ref>]]

[[Vitruvius]], writing around 25 BC in his [[De architectura|''Ten Books on Architecture'']], distinguished types of materials appropriate for the preparation of [[lime mortar]]s. For structural mortars, he recommended [[pozzolana]] ({{lang|la|pulvis puteolanus}} in Latin), the volcanic sand from the beds of [[Pozzuoli]], which are brownish-yellow-gray in colour in that area around Naples, and reddish-brown near Rome. Vitruvius specifies a ratio of 1 part lime to 3 parts pozzolana for mortar used in buildings and a 1:2 ratio for underwater work.{{sfn|Lechtman|Hobbs|1986}}<ref>{{cite book |author= [[Vitruvius]] |title=[[De Architectura]], Book II:v,1; Book V:xii2}}</ref>

The Romans first used hydraulic concrete in coastal underwater structures, probably in the harbours around [[Baiae]] before the end of the 2nd century BC.<ref>Oleson et al., 2004, The ROMACONS Project: A Contribution to the Historican and Engineering Analysis of the Hydrauilc Concrete in Roman Maritime Structures, International Journal of Nautical Archaeology 33.2: 199-229</ref> The harbour of [[Caesarea Maritima|Caesarea]] is an example (22-15 BC) of the use of underwater Roman concrete technology on a large scale,{{sfn|Lechtman|Hobbs|1986}} for which enormous quantities of pozzolana were imported from [[Puteoli]].<ref>Hohlfelder, R. 2007. "Constructing the Harbour of Caesarea Palaestina, Israel: New Evidence from ROMACONS Field Campaign of October 2005". International Journal of Nautical Archaeology 36:409-415.</ref>

For rebuilding Rome after the [[Great Fire of Rome|fire in 64 AD]] which destroyed large portions of the city, [[Nero]]'s new building code largely called for brick-faced concrete.{{cn|date=January 2023}} This appears to have encouraged the development of the brick and concrete industries.{{sfn|Lechtman|Hobbs|1986}}
[[File:OpusCaementiciumViaAppiaAntica.jpg|thumb|Example of {{lang|la|opus caementicium}} on a tomb on the ancient [[Appian Way]] in Rome. The original covering has been removed.]]

A building site from 79 CE was uncovered at Pompeii in 2025, which contained unmixed Roman concrete building materials as well as the concreted structures.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Vaserman |first=Ellie |last2=Weaver |first2=James C. |last3=Hayhow |first3=Claire |last4=Bergmann |first4=Kristin |last5=Grifa |first5=Celestino |last6=Scalesse |first6=Roberto |last7=Amoretti |first7=Valeria |last8=Russo |first8=Antonino |last9=Iovino |first9=Gennaro |last10=Zuchtriegel |first10=Gabriel |last11=Masic |first11=Admir |date=2025-12-09 |title=An unfinished Pompeian construction site reveals ancient Roman building technology |url=https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-66634-7 |journal=Nature Communications |language=en |volume=16 |issue=1 |pages=10847 |doi=10.1038/s41467-025-66634-7 |issn=2041-1723|doi-access=free }}</ref>

==Material properties==
Roman concrete, like any [[concrete]], consists of an [[construction aggregate|aggregate]] and hydraulic [[Mortar (masonry)|mortar]], a binder mixed with water that hardens over time. The composition of the aggregate varied, and included pieces of rock, [[ceramic]] tile, lime clasts, and brick rubble from the remains of previously demolished buildings. In Rome, readily available [[tuff]] was often used as an aggregate.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b05xxl4t |title=Rome's Invisible City |website=BBC One|access-date=6 July 2017}}</ref>

[[Gypsum]] and [[calcium oxide|quicklime]] were used as binders.<ref name=":0" /> Volcanic dusts, called [[pozzolana]] or "pit sand", were favoured where they could be obtained. Pozzolana makes the concrete more resistant to salt water than modern-day concrete.<ref>{{cite web| last1=Wayman |first1=Erin |url=https://www.smithsonian.com/history/the-secrets-of-ancient-romes-buildings-234992/ |title=The Secrets of Ancient Rome's Buildings |publisher=Smithsonian.com |date=16 November 2011 |access-date= 24 April 2012}}</ref> Pozzolanic mortar had a high content of [[alumina]] and [[silica]]. 

Research in 2023 found that lime clasts, previously considered a sign of poor aggregation technique, react with water seeping into any cracks. This produces reactive calcium, which allows new calcium carbonate crystals to form and reseal the cracks.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Seymour |display-authors=etal |first1=Linda |title=Hot mixing: Mechanistic insights into the durability of ancient Roman concrete |journal=Science Advances |year=2023 |volume=9 |issue=1 |article-number=eadd1602 |doi=10.1126/sciadv.add1602  |doi-access=free|pmid=36608117 |pmc=9821858 |bibcode=2023SciA....9D1602S |hdl=1721.1/147056 |s2cid=255501528 |hdl-access=free }}</ref> These lime clasts have a brittle structure that was most likely created in a "hot-mixing" technique with [[quicklime]] rather than traditional [[slaked lime]], causing cracks to preferentially move through the lime clasts, thus potentially playing a critical role in the self-healing mechanism.<ref name=chandler/>

Concrete and, in particular, the hydraulic mortar responsible for its cohesion, was a type of structural ceramic whose utility derived largely from its [[Rheology|rheological plasticity]] in the paste state. The setting and hardening of hydraulic cements derived from hydration of materials and the subsequent chemical and physical interaction of these hydration products. This differed from the setting of [[lime mortar|slaked lime mortars]], the most common cements of the pre-Roman world. Once set, Roman concrete exhibited little plasticity, although it retained some resistance to tensile stresses.[[File:Torbermorite_CSH_3D_Crystal_Structure_RasMol.gif|thumb|Crystal structure of [[tobermorite]]: elementary unit cell]]The setting of [[geopolymer|pozzolanic cements]] has much in common with setting of their modern counterpart, [[Portland cement]]. The high silica composition of Roman pozzolana cements is very close to that of modern cement to which blast furnace [[slag]], [[fly ash]], or [[silica fume]] have been added.  

The strength and longevity of Roman 'marine' concrete is understood to benefit from a reaction of [[seawater]] with a mixture of [[volcanic ash]] and quicklime to create a rare crystal called [[tobermorite]], which may resist fracturing. As seawater percolated within the tiny cracks in the Roman concrete, it reacted with [[phillipsite]] naturally found in the volcanic rock and created [[Aluminium|aluminous]] tobermorite crystals. The result is a candidate for "the most durable building material in human history". In contrast, modern concrete exposed to saltwater deteriorates within decades.<ref name="WashPost2017">{{cite news |last1=Guarino |first1=Ben |title=Ancient Romans made world's 'most durable' concrete. We might use it to stop rising seas |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/speaking-of-science/wp/2017/07/04/ancient-romans-made-worlds-most-durable-concrete-we-might-use-it-to-stop-rising-seas/ |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |date=4 July 2017 }}</ref><ref name="JacksonMulcahy2017">{{cite journal |last1=Jackson|first1=Marie D.|last2=Mulcahy|first2=Sean R. |last3=Chen|first3=Heng |last4=Li|first4=Yao |last5=Li|first5=Qinfei |last6=Cappelletti|first6=Piergiulio |last7=Wenk|first7=Hans-Rudolf |title=Phillipsite and Al-tobermorite mineral cements produced through low-temperature water-rock reactions in Roman marine concrete |journal=American Mineralogist |volume=102 |issue=7 |year=2017 |pages=1435–1450 |issn=0003-004X |doi=10.2138/am-2017-5993CCBY|bibcode=2017AmMin.102.1435J|doi-access=free }}</ref><ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/science-environment-40494248|title=Scientists explain ancient Rome's long-lasting concrete|first=Matt|last=McGrath|work=BBC News|date=4 July 2017|access-date=6 July 2017}}</ref>

The Roman concrete at the [[Tomb of Caecilia Metella]] is another variation higher in potassium that triggered changes that "reinforce interfacial zones and potentially contribute to improved mechanical performance".<ref>{{cite news |last1=Ouellette |first1=Jennifer |title=Noblewoman's tomb reveals new secrets of ancient Rome's highly durable concrete |url=https://arstechnica.com/science/2022/01/noblewomans-tomb-reveals-new-secrets-of-ancient-romes-highly-durable-concrete/ |access-date=5 January 2022 |work=[[Ars Technica]] |date=1 January 2022 |language=en-us}}</ref>

==Seismic technology==
[[File:Rome (Italy, October 2019) - 275 (50589571796).jpg|thumb|Exterior view of the Pantheon's concrete dome]]
For an environment as prone to [[earthquake]]s as the [[Italian Peninsula]], interruptions and internal constructions within walls and domes created discontinuities in the concrete mass. Portions of the building could then shift slightly when there was movement of the earth to accommodate such stresses, enhancing the overall strength of the structure. It was in this sense that bricks and concrete were flexible. It may have been precisely for this reason that, although many buildings sustained serious cracking from a variety of causes, they continue to stand to this day.{{sfn|MacDonald|1982|loc=fig. 131B}}{{sfn|Lechtman|Hobbs|1986}} 

Another technology used to improve the [[strength of materials|strength]] and stability of concrete was its gradation in domes. One example is the [[Pantheon, Rome|Pantheon]], where the aggregate of the upper dome region consists of alternating layers of light [[tuff]] and [[pumice]], giving the concrete a density of {{convert|1350|kg/m3|lb/cuft}}. The foundation of the structure used [[travertine]] as an aggregate, having a much higher density of {{convert|2200|kg/m3|lb/cuft}}.<ref>K. de Fine Licht, The Rotunda in Rome: A Study of Hadrian's Pantheon. Jutland Archaeological Society, Copenhagen, 1968, pp. 89–94, 134–35</ref>{{sfn|Lechtman|Hobbs|1986}}

==Modern use==
Scientific studies of Roman concrete since 2010 have attracted both media and industry attention.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://trebcap.com/fixing-canadas-infrastructure-with-volcanoes/ |title= Fixing Canada's Infrastructure with Volcanoes |date= 15 October 2015 |publisher=Trebuchet Capital Partners Research |access-date=19 August 2016}}</ref> Because of its unusual durability, longevity, and lessened environmental footprint, corporations and municipalities are starting to explore the use of Roman-style concrete in North America. This involves replacing the volcanic ash with coal [[fly ash]] that has similar properties. Proponents say that concrete made with fly ash can cost up to 60% less, because it requires less cement. It also has a reduced environmental footprint, due to its lower cooking temperature and much longer lifespan.<ref>{{cite web | last=Patrick | first=Neil | title=By 25 BC, ancient Romans developed a recipe for concrete specifically used for underwater work which is essentially the same formula used today| website=The Vintage News | date=6 September 2016 | url=https://www.thevintagenews.com/2016/09/06/priority-25-bc-ancient-romans-developed-recipe-concrete-specifically-used-underwater-work-essentially-formula-used-today/}}</ref> Usable examples of Roman concrete exposed to harsh marine environments have been found to be 2000 years old with little or no wear.<ref>M. D. Jackson, S. R. Chae, R. Taylor, C. Meral, J. Moon, S. Yoon, P. Li, A. M. Emwas, G. Vola, H.-R. Wenk, and P. J. M. Monteiro, [https://pubs.geoscienceworld.org/msa/ammin/article-abstract/98/10/1669/45726 "Unlocking the secrets of Al-tobermorite in Roman seawater concrete"], ''American Mineralogist'', Volume 98, pp. 1669–1687, 2013.</ref>
In 2013, the University of California Berkeley published an article that described for the first time the mechanism by which the suprastable calcium-aluminium-silicate-hydrate compound binds the material together.<ref>{{Cite journal |title=Material and Elastic Properties of Al-Tobermorite in Ancient Roman Seawater Concrete |url=https://ceramics.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jace.12407?_gl=1*rdplb2*_gcl_au*MTIyMjM2MDM4Ny4xNjk5MDc5NTQw |access-date=November 4, 2023 |journal=Journal of the American Ceramic Society|date=28 May 2013 | doi=10.1111/jace.12407 | last1=Jackson | first1=Marie D. | last2=Moon | first2=Juhyuk | last3=Gotti | first3=Emanuele | last4=Taylor | first4=Rae | last5=Chae | first5=Sejung R. | last6=Kunz | first6=Martin | last7=Emwas | first7=Abdul-Hamid | last8=Meral | first8=Cagla | last9=Guttmann | first9=Peter | last10=Levitz | first10=Pierre | last11=Wenk | first11=Hans-Rudolf | last12=Monteiro | first12=Paulo J. M. | volume=96 | issue=8 | pages=2598–2606 | url-access=subscription | hdl=11511/37952 | hdl-access=free }}</ref> During its production, less carbon dioxide is released into the atmosphere than any modern concrete production process.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Renaissance of Roman Concrete: Cutting carbon emissions |url=https://www.constructionspecifier.com/renaissance-of-roman-concrete-cutting-carbon-emissions/3/ |access-date=June 27, 2022 |website=constructionspecifier.com|date=29 December 2016 }}</ref> It is no coincidence that the walls of Roman buildings are thicker than those of modern buildings. However, Roman concrete was still gaining its strength for several decades after construction had been completed.<ref name="WashPost2017"/>
