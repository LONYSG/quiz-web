A '''haboob'''<ref>{{Britannica|250848}}</ref> ({{langx|ar|هَبوب|lit=blasting/drifting|translit=habūb}}) is an atmospheric [[density current]] forming an intense [[dust storm]] generated by strong winds from the downdrafts of [[thunderstorm]]s or, less commonly, weather fronts. These storms occur in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide, including West Asia, North Africa, Australia, and North America, and have also been observed on [[Mars]] and [[Titan (moon)|Titan]]. A haboob typically forms when cold air from a thunderstorm rushes downward, causing high winds at the surface which lift dust into the air.

In Khartoum, Sudan, haboobs have been historically documented as reaching up to {{cvt|2000|m|-3}} high, advancing at up to {{cvt|45|mph|km/h|-1|order=flip}}, and lasting as long as 6 1/2 hours.<ref name=IIP/> Haboobs can occasionally span distances as long as {{cvt|1000|km|-2}}.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Roberts|first1=AJ|first2=P|last2=Knippertz|year=2014|title=The formation of a large summertime Saharan dust plume: Convective and synoptic-scale analysis|journal=J. Geophys. Res. Atmos.|volume=119|pages=1766–1785|doi=10.1002/2013JD020667|pmc=4379907}}</ref>

== Etymology ==
The term "haboob" comes from the Arabic root "haab" meaning wind or blow.<ref name=glossary>{{cite web|url=https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/Haboob|title=Haboob|work=Glossary of Meteorology|publisher=American Meteorological Society|date= 8 June 2016}}</ref><ref name=npr>{{cite news|title=A haboob covered central Arizona in dust. But what exactly is it?|newspaper=NPR|date=August 26, 2025|first=Juliana|last=Kim|url=https://www.npr.org/2025/08/26/nx-s1-5518308/haboob-explained-phoenix-arizona}}</ref> The term was originally used to describe dust storms in [[Sudan]], and was first applied to North American dust storms in 1972 by Robert Ingram and co-authors.<ref name=azrepublic>{{cite news|title=This is why dust storms are called 'haboobs' in Arizona|newspaper=Arizona Republic|first=Richard|last=Ruelas|date=August 20, 2018|url=https://www.azcentral.com/story/news/local/arizona-weather/2018/08/20/haboob-definition-meaning-name-dust-storm-wind-monsoon/770406002/}}</ref><ref name=IIP>{{cite journal|url=https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/53/10/1520-0477_1972_053_0930_aah_2_0_co_2.xml?tab_body=pdf|title=An American Haboob|last1=Idso|first1=SB|first2=RS|last2=Ingram|first3=JM|last3=Pritchard|year=1972|journal=Bull. Amer. Meteor. Soc.|volume=53|pages=930–935}}</ref> The term came into common English usage in Arizona after 1999.<ref name=azrepublic/>

== Formation and characteristics  ==
{{more citations needed section|date=August 2025}}
When [[thunderstorms]] form, winds move in a direction opposite to the storm's travel, and they move from all directions into the thunderstorm. When the storm collapses and begins to release precipitation, wind directions reverse, gusting outward from the storm and generally gusting the strongest in the direction of the storm's travel.<ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.1002/qj.49706327111|title=Haboobs and instability in the sudan|year=1937|last1=Farquharson|first1=J. S.|journal=Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society|volume=63|issue=271|pages=393–414|bibcode = 1937QJRMS..63..393F }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal|doi=10.1002/j.1477-8696.1971.tb07402.x|title=Haboob Structure at Khartoum|year=1971|last1=Lawson|first1=T. J.|journal=Weather|volume=26|issue=3|pages=105–112|bibcode = 1971Wthr...26..105L }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal|doi=10.1002/j.1477-8696.1985.tb06877.x|title=A Gravity-Wave Haboob?|year=1985|last1=Membery|first1=D. A.|journal=Weather|volume=40|issue=7|pages=214–221|bibcode = 1985Wthr...40..214M }}</ref>

When this downdraft of cold, denser air, or [[downburst]], reaches the ground, it sweeps up dry, loose silt and clay (referred to collectively as dust) from the desert, forming a wall of airborne sediment that precedes the storm cloud. This wall of dust can be up to 100&nbsp;km (62&nbsp;mi) wide, and several kilometers high. During their peak intensity, haboob winds can reach speeds of 35–100&nbsp;km/h (22–62&nbsp;mph) and may approach suddenly with minimal warning. Rain often fails to reach the ground level as it evaporates in the hot, dry air (a phenomenon known as [[virga]]). The evaporation process further chills the rushing air and propels it forward. In some instances, persistent rain may carry a significant amount of dust, leading to what is termed as ''mud storms'' in severe cases.

== Safety ==
Eye and respiratory system protection is advisable for anyone who must be outside during a haboob. Moving to shelter is highly advised during a strong event.

While operating a vehicle, drivers are advised to pull over to the side of the road and turn off their lights to avoid confusing other drivers in conditions of poor visibility.<ref>{{Cite web |last=US Department of Commerce |first=NOAA |title=Dust Storms and Haboobs |url=https://www.weather.gov/safety/wind-dust-storm |access-date=2024-12-30 |website=www.weather.gov |language=EN-US}}</ref>

== Occurrence ==
=== West Asia ===
Haboobs have been observed across the [[Arabian Peninsula]], throughout [[Kuwait]], and in the most arid regions of [[Iraq]].<ref>{{Cite journal|doi=10.1002/qj.49705121305|title=Haboobs|year=1925|last1=Sutton|first1=L. J.|journal=Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society|volume=51|issue=213|pages=25–30|bibcode = 1925QJRMS..51...25S }}</ref> Haboob [[wind]]s in the Arabian Peninsula, Iraq, and Kuwait are frequently created by the collapse of a [[thunderstorm]].

=== North Africa ===
[[File:Haboob dust storm in Tozeur, southern Tunisia.jpg|thumb|Haboob dust storm in [[Tozeur]], southern [[Tunisia]].]]
African haboobs often occur in the [[Sahara]] and [[Sahel]] biogeographical regions, and most typically in [[Sudan]], where they were named and described. They result from the northward summer shift of the [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]] into [[North Africa]], bringing moisture from the [[Gulf of Guinea]].

=== Australia ===
Haboobs in Australia may be frequently associated with [[cold front]]s. The deserts of [[Central Australia]], especially near [[Alice Springs]], are particularly prone to haboobs, with sand and debris reaching several kilometers into the sky and leaving up to {{convert|30|cm|ft|0}} of sand in the haboob's path.

=== North America ===
{{See also|North American monsoon}}

As in West Asia, haboobs in North America are often created by the collapse of a thunderstorm. This is a local or [[Mesoscale meteorology|mesoscale]] event, and at times of extreme drought they can originate in agricultural regions. Some of the most famous dust storms of the [[Dust Bowl]] and similar conditions later were in fact [[synoptic scale]] events typically generated by a strong cold frontal passage, with storms on [[Great Blue Norther of November 11, 1911|11&nbsp;November 1911]], 9–11&nbsp;May 1934, 14&nbsp;April 1935, and 19&nbsp;February 1954 having been particularly vivid examples.

The arid and semiarid regions of [[North America]]—in fact, any dry region—may experience haboobs. In North America, these events are commonly also referred to as either [[dust storm]] or sandstorm. In the U.S., they frequently occur in the deserts of [[Arizona]], including around the cities of [[Yuma, Arizona|Yuma]], [[Tucson, Arizona|Tucson]] and [[Phoenix, Arizona|Phoenix]];<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Idso |first1=S.B. |last2=Ingram |first2=R.S. |last3=Pritchard |first3=J.M. |year=1972 |title=An American Haboob |journal=Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society |volume=53 |issue=10 |pages=930–935 |doi=10.1175/1520-0477(1972)053<0930:AAH>2.0.CO;2 |doi-access=free |bibcode=1972BAMS...53..930I}}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Idso |first=Carolyn W. |year=1973 |title=Haboobs in Arizona |journal=Weather |volume=28 |issue=4 |pages=154–155 |bibcode=1973Wthr...28..154I |doi=10.1002/j.1477-8696.1973.tb02253.x}}</ref> in [[New Mexico]], including [[Albuquerque, New Mexico|Albuquerque]]; eastern [[California]]; west [[Texas]];<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Chen |first1=W. |last2=Fryrear |first2=D.W. |year=2002 |title=Sedimentary characteristics of a haboob dust storm |journal=Atmospheric Research |volume=61 |issue=1 |pages=75–85 |bibcode=2002AtmRe..61...75C |doi=10.1016/S0169-8095(01)00092-8}}</ref> and the [[High Plains (United States)|High Plains]]. Per the [[Washington State Department of Ecology]], they also occur in the [[Columbia Plateau (ecoregion)|Columbia Basin]] of [[Eastern Washington]], and can impact cities such as [[Walla Walla, Washington|Walla Walla]]<ref name="SeattlePI2013">{{cite news |last1=Ellison |first1=Jake |title=Photos: Major 'haboob' – dust and windstorm – strikes eastern Washington |url=https://www.seattlepi.com/local/weather/article/Photos-Major-haboob-dust-and-windstorm-4825842.php |access-date=22 June 2023 |work=Seattle PI |date=18 September 2013}}</ref> and [[Spokane]].<ref name="Spokesman2021">{{cite news |last1=Loyd |first1=Nic |last2=Weiford |first2=Linda |title=Weathercatch: Dust-filled drama of past teaches lesson for future |url=https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2021/aug/12/weathercatch-dust-and-drama-in-the-inland-northwes/ |access-date=22 June 2023 |work=The Spokesman-Review |date=12 August 2021 |ref=Spokesman2021}}</ref> In Washington, improved farming practices have led to a decline in large dust storms and haboobs since the 1990s,<ref name="WaDoEc21">{{cite web archived |last1=Vaughn |first1=Kim |date=1 April 2021 |title=Get ready, it's haboob season |url=https://ecology.wa.gov/Blog/Posts/March-2021/Central-and-Eastern-Washington |archive-date=2021-04-01 |access-date=22 June 2023 |website=Department of Ecology State of Washington}}</ref> with the largest likelihood of formation between late March through April, corresponding to the beginning of field tilling in Eastern Washington.<ref name="WaDoEc23">{{cite web|url=https://ecology.wa.gov/Blog/Posts/March-2023/What-to-know-about-dust-storms-in-Washington |title=What you need to know about dust storms in Washington |last1=Vaughn |first1=Kim |website=Department of Ecology State of Washington |date=1 April 2021 |access-date=22 June 2023 }}</ref> In [[Mexico]], they occur in the northern part of the country in the [[Sonoran Desert|Sonoran]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Most recently, a haboob impacted the cities of [[Guaymas]], [[San Carlos Nuevo Guaymas|San Carlos]], and [[Empalme, Sonora|Empalme]], [[Sonora]] on 20 July 2023.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Grupo Healy |first= |date=20 July 2023 |title=Tormenta de arena cubre Guaymas; Protección Civil Sonora emite medidas para la población |language=ES |trans-title=Dust storm covers Guaymas; Protección Civil Sonora issues safety measures |work=El Imparcial |url=https://www.elimparcial.com/sonora/sonora/Tormenta-de-arena-cubre-Guaymas-Proteccion-Civil-Sonora-emite-medidas-para-la-poblacion-20230720-0043.html |access-date=21 July 2023}}</ref>

Usage of the Arabic term by local news media in some parts of the U.S. has sometimes attracted criticism by viewers and readers.<ref>{{cite news |last1=Samenow |first1=Jason |author1-link=Jason Samenow |title=Arabic weather term 'haboob' is apparently troubling for some Texans |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/capital-weather-gang/wp/2016/05/31/arabic-weather-term-haboob-is-apparently-troubling-for-some-texans/ |access-date=22 March 2025 |work=[[The Washington Post]] |date=31 May 2016}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |last1=Lacey |first1=Marc |author1-link=Marc Lacey |title='Haboobs' Stir Critics in Arizona |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2011/07/22/us/22haboob.html |access-date=22 March 2025 |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=22 July 2011}}</ref><ref>{{cite news |last1=Hauser |first1=Christine |last2=Jiménez |first2=Jesus |title=Haboob?
