The '''Morning Glory cloud''' is a rare [[List of meteorological phenomena|meteorological phenomenon]] consisting of a low-level atmospheric solitary wave and associated cloud, occasionally observed in different locations around the world.  The wave often occurs as an [[amplitude]]-ordered series of waves forming bands of [[Arcus cloud#Roll_cloud|roll cloud]]s.

The southern part of the [[Gulf of Carpentaria]] in [[Northern Australia]] is the only known location where it can be predicted and observed regularly due to the configuration of land and sea in the area.

== Description ==
Morning Glory clouds can be observed from [[Burketown, Queensland|Burketown]] from late September to early November.  The town attracts [[gliding|glider pilots]] intent on riding this phenomenon.<ref name="stg">{{cite web |url=http://www.abc.net.au/science/articles/2003/08/07/2045486.htm |title=Soaring the glory |author=Abbie Thomas |date=7 August 2003 |work=ABC Science |publisher=Australian Broadcasting Corporation |accessdate=30 August 2014 }}</ref>  There are generally only a handful of well formed spectacular clouds during this period at Burketown. During the 2012 season there were only four to be seen from there, but quite a few ragged unspectacular cloud lines were seen. Often they start to break up before arriving at Burketown or pass to the north and only stay well formed over water. In an aircraft there is a significantly better chance of sighting the cloud.

A Morning Glory cloud is a [[roll cloud]], or [[arcus cloud]], that can be up to {{convert|1000|km}} long,<ref>[http://www.dropbears.com/brough/index.html Morning Glory Clouds of the Gulf of Carpentaria]. Dropbears.com. Retrieved on 28 December 2012.</ref>{{convert|1|to|2|km}} high, often only {{convert|100|to|200|m}} above the ground.  The cloud often travels at the rate of 10 to 20 metres per second.<ref name="esf">{{cite book |title=Environmental Stratified Flows |last=Grimshaw |first=Roger |author2=James W. Rottman |year=2002 |editor1-first=Roger |editor1-last=Grimshaw |chapter=Atmospheric Internal Solitary Waves |publisher=Springer Science & Business Media |isbn=0792376056 |pages=67, 69 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=EB17Mj902isC |accessdate=30 August 2014}}</ref> Sometimes there is only one cloud, sometimes there are up to ten consecutive roll clouds.<ref name="wrc">{{cite magazine |url=https://www.wired.com/2009/09/clouds/ |title=Weird, Rare Clouds and the Physics Behind Them |author=Betsy Mason |date=29 September 2009 |magazine=Wired |publisher=Condé Nast |access-date=30 August 2014 }}</ref> Three distinct types of Morning Glory clouds have been identified.<ref name="esf"/>

The Morning Glory is often accompanied by sudden wind squalls, intense low-level [[wind shear]], a rapid increase in the vertical displacement of air parcels, and a sharp pressure jump at the surface.<ref name="tcl">{{cite web |url=http://www.meteo.physik.uni-muenchen.de/~roger/Tropics/Tropclds-o.htm |title=Tropical Cloud Lines |author=Roger K. Smith |publisher=Meteorological Institute Munich |accessdate=30 August 2014 }}</ref>  Cloud is continuously formed at the leading edge while being eroded at the trailing edge.<ref name="wrc"/>  Showers or thunderstorms may develop in its wake.  In the front of the cloud, there is strong vertical motion that transports air up through the cloud and creates the rolling appearance, while the air in the middle and rear of the cloud becomes turbulent and sinks.  The cloud quickly dissipates over land where the air is drier.<ref name="esf"/>

The cloud can also be described as a [[John Russell (engineer)#The wave of translation|solitary wave]] or a [[soliton]] or an [[undular bore]], which is a wave that has a single crest and moves without changing speed or shape. They have been called "the biggest waves on the planet".<ref name="twbw">{{cite web |url=http://www.geographical.co.uk/Magazine/Morning_Glory_-_May_10.html |title=The world's biggest wave |date=May 2010 |work=Geographical Magazine |accessdate=30 August 2014 |url-status=dead |archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20140903081524/http://www.geographical.co.uk/Magazine/Morning_Glory_-_May_10.html |archivedate=3 September 2014 |df=dmy-all }}</ref>  The wave may occur without the appearance of any clouds.<ref name="esf"/>

== History of exploration ==
Unusual cloud formations have been noticed here since ancient times. The local [[Garrwa people|Garrwa]] [[Australian Aborigines|Aboriginal people]] called it ''kangólgi''.<ref name=wondermondo>{{cite web|url=http://www.wondermondo.com/Countries/Au/AU/Queensland/CarpentariaMornGlory.htm|title=Morning Glory of Carpentaria Gulf|date=24 August 2010|publisher=Wondermondo}}</ref> [[Royal Australian Air Force]] pilots first reported this phenomenon in 1942.

The Morning Glory cloud of the Gulf of Carpentaria has been studied by multiple teams of scientists since the early 1970s. The first studies were published by Reg H. Clarke ([[University of Melbourne]]).<ref name=wondermondo/> Multiple studies have followed since then, proposing diverse mathematical models explaining the complex movements of air masses in the region.

== Atmospheric conditions ==
One of the main contributors for the formation of the morning glory clouds is the [[Mesoscale meteorology|mesoscale]] circulations associated with a difference in sea breezes that develop over the Peninsula and the Gulf. On the large scale, Morning Glories are usually associated with [[Weather front|frontal systems]] crossing central Australia and high pressure in northern Australia. Locals have noted that the Morning Glory is likely to occur when the [[humidity]] in the area is high, which provides moisture for the cloud to form, and when strong sea breezes have blown the preceding day.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://media.bom.gov.au/social/blog/2272/whats-the-science-behind-these-spectacular-weather-photos/ | title=What's the science behind these spectacular weather photos? - Social Media Blog - Bureau of Meteorology }}</ref>

The following is a summary of the conditions that cause the Morning Glory cloud to form in the Gulf of Carpentaria (after hypothesis of R.H.Clarke, as described in 1981).<ref name=wondermondo/> First, [[Cape York Peninsula|Cape York]], which is the peninsula that lies to the east of the gulf, is large enough that sea breezes develop on both sides. During the day, the breeze from the Coral Sea coast blows in from the east and the breeze from the gulf blows in from the west. The two breezes meet in the middle of the peninsula, forcing the air to rise there and form a line of clouds over the spine of the peninsula. When night comes, the air cools and descends and at the same time a surface [[inversion (meteorology)|inversion]] (where air temperature increases with height) forms over the gulf. The densities in this stable layer are different above and below the inversion. The air descending from the peninsula to the east goes underneath the inversion layer and this generates a series of waves or rolling cylinders which travel across the gulf. These cylinders of air roll along the underside of the inversion layer, so that the air rises at the front of the wave and sinks at the rear. In the early morning, the air is saturated enough so that the rising air in the front produces a cloud, which forms the leading edge of the cylinder, and evaporates in the back, hence forming the Morning Glory cloud. The cloud lasts until the surface inversion disappears with the heating of the day.

There are other ways in which Morning Glory clouds form, especially in rarer cases in other parts of the world, but these are far less understood.{{citation needed|date=February 2013}}

Local weather lore in the area suggests that when the [[Refrigerator|fridges]] frost over and the café tables' corners curl upwards at the Burketown Pub, there is enough moisture in the air for the clouds to form. Reportedly, all winds cease at ground level as the cloud passes over.<ref>{{cite book| author=Gavin Pretor-Pinney | title=The Cloudspotter's Guide|publisher= Hodder & Stoughton| year=2006 |isbn= 0-340-89589-6}}  Chapter 13, ''The Morning Glory'' the cloud that glider pilots surf, pp 283–305</ref>

== Other reported occurrences ==
Although the Morning Glory clouds over the southern part of the Gulf of Carpentaria are the most frequent and predictable, similar phenomena have occasionally been observed elsewhere, ''e.g.'', over central [[United States]], Canada, in the [[English Channel]], [[Munich]],<ref name="wrc"/> [[Berlin]], eastern [[Russia]], and other maritime regions of Australia. 

Morning Glory clouds have occasionally been reported on Cape Cod and in the [[Gulf of California]] off the [[Mexico|Mexican]] coast. The phenomenon has also been observed from [[Sable Island]], 180&nbsp;km southeast of [[Nova Scotia]]. A Morning Glory also passed through [[Yarmouth, Nova Scotia]], in April 2009, as well as several communities in Newfoundland in May 2022. In contrast to the Gulf of Carpentaria where the Morning Glory is visible in the morning, those in the Maritimes have all occurred during the evening.
