'''Sprites''' or '''red sprites''' are large-scale [[electric discharge]]s that occur in the [[mesosphere]], high above [[thunderstorm]] clouds, or [[cumulonimbus]], giving rise to a varied range of visual shapes flickering in the night sky. They are usually triggered by the discharges of [[positive lightning]] between an underlying [[thundercloud]] and the ground.

== Summary ==
Sprites appear as luminous red-orange flashes. They often occur in clusters above the [[troposphere]] at an altitude range of {{convert|50|-|90|km|mi|abbr=on}}. Sporadic visual reports of sprites go back at least to 1886.<ref name="Rodger1999">{{Cite journal | doi=10.1029/2001JA000283| title=Red sprites, upward lightning, and VLF perturbations| journal=Reviews of Geophysics| volume=37| year=1999| issue= 3| pages = 317–336| last1= Rodger| first1=C. J.| doi-access=}}</ref> They were first photographed on July 4, 1989,<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.nasa.gov/mission_pages/sunearth/news/seeing-sprites.html | title=NASA - Heliophysics Nugget: Seeing Sprites }}</ref> by [[John R. Winckler|scientists]] from the [[University of Minnesota]] and have subsequently been captured in video recordings thousands of times.

Sprites are sometimes inaccurately called [[upper-atmospheric lightning]]. However, they are cold [[plasma (physics)|plasma]] phenomena that lack the hot channel temperatures of [[Troposphere|tropospheric]] lightning, so they are more akin to [[fluorescent tube]] discharges than to lightning discharges. Sprites are associated with various other upper-atmospheric [[Atmospheric optics|optical phenomena]] including [[blue jets]] and [[ELVES]].<ref name="Rodger1999" />

== History ==
The earliest known report is by Toynbee and Mackenzie in 1886.<ref name="samTM">{{cite journal |last=Toynbee |first=Henry |title=Meteorological phenomena (letter) |journal=Nature |volume=33 |issue=846 |page=245 |date=14 January 1886 |doi=10.1038/033245d0|s2cid=4128139 |url=https://zenodo.org/record/1698249|doi-access=free }}</ref> Nobel laureate [[Charles Thomson Rees Wilson|C. T. R. Wilson]] had suggested in 1925, on theoretical grounds, that electrical breakdown could occur in the upper atmosphere, and in 1956 he witnessed what possibly could have been a sprite. They were first documented photographically on July 6, 1989, when scientists from the [[University of Minnesota]], using a low-light video camera, accidentally captured the first image of what would subsequently become known as a sprite.<ref name="aaas">{{Cite journal |doi=10.1126/science.249.4964.48 | title=Television Image of a Large Upward Electrical Discharge Above a Thunderstorm System | date=1990 | first1=R.C.|last1=Franz | first2=R.J.|last2=Nemzek | first3=J.R.|last3=Winckler | journal=Science|volume=249|issue=4964|pages=48–51| pmid=17787625 | bibcode=1990Sci...249...48F | s2cid=9343018}}</ref>

Several years after their discovery they were named "sprites" (air spirits) after [[Sprite (creature)|their namesake mythological entity]] based on their elusive nature.<ref name="sentman">{{cite journal | doi = 10.1029/95GL00583 | last1 = Sentman | first1 = D.D. | last2 = Wescott | first2 = E. M. | last3 = Osborne | first3 = D. L. | last4 = Hampton | first4 = D. L. | last5 = Heavner | first5 = M. J.| title = Preliminary results from the Sprites94 aircraft campaign: 1. Red Sprites | journal = Geophys. Res. Lett. | volume = 22 | issue = 10 | pages = 1205–1208| date = 1995 | bibcode=1995GeoRL..22.1205S}}</ref> Since the 1989 video capture, images of sprites have been made from the ground, from aircraft, and from space, and have become the subject of intensive investigations. A high-speed video captured by [[Thomas Ashcraft]], Jacob L Harley, Matthew G McHarg, and Hans Nielsen in 2019 at about 100,000 frames per second provided some observations on how sprites develop. However, according to the NASA APOD blog, despite being recorded in photographs and videos for more than 30 years, the "root cause" of sprite lightning remains unknown, "apart from a general association with positive cloud-to-ground lightning". NASA also notes that not all storms exhibit sprite lightning.<ref name=APOD.NASA>{{cite web |title=Sprite Lightning at 100,000 Frames Per Second |url=https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap210104.html |website=APOD.NASA.gov |publisher=NASA's APOD (Astronomy Picture of the Day blog) |access-date=19 July 2022}}</ref> 

In 2016, sprites were observed during [[Hurricane Matthew]]'s passage through the Caribbean.<ref name="vqwzi">{{cite news|newspaper=National Geographic|date=October 3, 2016|access-date=October 3, 2016|title=Rare, Colorful Lightning Sprites Dance Over Hurricane Matthew|url=http://news.nationalgeographic.com/2016/10/lightning-sprites-form-over-hurricane-matthew/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161004171420/http://news.nationalgeographic.com/2016/10/lightning-sprites-form-over-hurricane-matthew/|archive-date=October 4, 2016}}</ref> The role of sprites in the tropical cyclones is presently unknown.<ref name="CIMSSOct2">{{cite web|publisher=University of Wisconsin–Madison|work=Cooperative Institute for Meteorological Satellite Studies|date=October 7, 2016|access-date=November 3, 2016|title=Hurricane Matthew and the Day/Night Band|url=http://cimss.ssec.wisc.edu/goes/blog/archives/date/2016/10/02}}</ref>

Since 2022 [[NASA]] operates a [[citizen science]] website and database called ''Spritacular'' collecting and studying images of sprites by citizens.<ref name="b515">{{cite web | last=Kaiser | first=Colleen | title=Spritacular | website=NASA Science | date=2023-12-26 | url=https://science.nasa.gov/citizen-science/spritacular/ | access-date=2026-05-24}}</ref>

Sprites have been observed over [[North America]],<ref name="9uiOo">Kathy Berry (1994). [http://umbra.nascom.nasa.gov/spd/pressrel_94-124.html Spectacular Color Flashes Recorded Above Electrical Storms.] [[NASA]]. Retrieved on 2009-02-18.</ref> [[Central America]], [[South America]],<ref name="DsGuW">Don Savage and Kathy Berry (1995). [http://umbra.nascom.nasa.gov/spd/pressrel_95-84.html Sprites Confirmed Over Storms Outside U.S. For First Time.] [[NASA]]. Retrieved on 2009-02-18.</ref> [[Europe]],<ref name="Ui7Qf">{{Cite web |url=http://star.arm.ac.uk/press/2013/sprite.html |title=Rare Atmospheric Phenomenon Observed from Armagh |access-date=2013-08-21 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130905165731/http://star.arm.ac.uk/press/2013/sprite.html |archive-date=2013-09-05 }}</ref> [[Central Africa]] ([[Zaire]]), [[Australia]], the [[Sea of Japan]], and [[Asia]] and are believed to occur during most large thunderstorm systems.

Rodger (1999) categorized three types of sprites based on their visual appearance.<ref name="Rodger1999" />
* Jellyfish sprite – very large, up to {{convert|50|by|50|km|mi|abbr=on}}
* Column sprite (C-sprite) – large-scale electrical discharges above the earth that are still not totally understood
* Carrot sprite – a column sprite with long tendrils

Sprites are colored reddish-orange<ref name="sentman" /> in their upper regions, with bluish hanging [[tendril]]s below, and may be preceded by a reddish halo. They last longer than normal lower stratospheric discharges, which last typically a few milliseconds, and are usually triggered by the discharges of positive lightning between the thundercloud and the ground,<ref name="boccippio">{{cite journal | last1 = Boccippio | first1 = D. J. | title = Sprites, ELF Transients, and Positive Ground Strokes| journal = Science | volume = 269 | pages = 1088–1091 |date=August 1995 | doi = 10.1126/science.269.5227.1088 | pmid = 17755531 | last2 = Williams | first2 = ER | last3 = Heckman | first3 = SJ | last4 = Lyons | first4 = WA | last5 = Baker | first5 = IT | last6 = Boldi | first6 = R | issue = 5227 | bibcode=1995Sci...269.1088B| s2cid = 8840716}}</ref> although sprites generated by negative ground flashes have also been observed.<ref name="ncg">{{cite journal |doi=10.1029/2011JD016890|title=Lightning morphology and impulse charge moment change of high peak current negative strokes|journal=Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres|volume=117|issue=D4|year=2012|last1=Lu|first1=Gaopeng|last2=Cummer|first2=Steven A|last3=Blakeslee|first3=Richard J|last4=Weiss|first4=Stephanie|last5=Beasley|first5=William H|article-number=2011JD016890 |pages=n/a|bibcode=2012JGRD..117.4212L|author-link2=Steven Cummer}}</ref> They often occur in clusters of two or more, and typically span the altitude range {{convert|50|to|90|km|mi}}, with what appear to be tendrils hanging below, and branches reaching above.<ref name="sentman" />

Optical imaging using a 10,000 frame-per-second [[high speed camera]] showed that sprites are clusters of small, decameter scale, ({{convert|10|–|100|m|ft|abbr=on|disp=or}}) balls of ionization that are launched at an altitude of about {{convert|80|km|mi|sp=us|abbr=on}} and then move downward at speeds of up to ten percent the [[speed of light]], followed a few milliseconds later by a separate set of upward moving balls of ionization.<ref name="CuTdQ">{{Cite journal| last1 =Stenbaek-Nielsen | first1 =H. C.| last2 = McHarg | first2 = M.G. | last3 = Kanmae | first3 = T.| last4 = Sentman | first4 = D.D. | title = Observed emission rates in sprite streamer heads | journal =Geophys. Res. Lett.| volume = 34 | issue =11 | date =June 6, 2007 | doi = 10.1029/2007GL029881| id = L11105 | pages = L11105 | bibcode=2007GeoRL..3411105S| doi-access = free}}</ref> Sprites may be horizontally displaced by up to {{convert|50|km|mi|sp=us|abbr=on}} from the location of the underlying lightning strike, with a time delay following the lightning that is typically a few milliseconds, but on rare occasions may be up to 100 milliseconds.

[[file:Red Sprite.ogv|thumb|upright=1.3|This footage from the [[International Space Station|ISS]] shows a red sprite over East Asia immediately before 0:07, directly above the large lightning flash toward the upper right of the frame.]]

In order to film sprites from Earth, special conditions must be present: {{convert|150|-|500|km|mi|abbr=on}} of clear view to a powerful thunderstorm with positive lightning between cloud and ground, red-sensitive recording equipment, and an unlit black sky.<ref name="jgr">Grønne, Jesper. [http://www.dmi.dk/dmi/foerste_danske_red_sprites_fanget_fra_silkeborg "Første danske 'red sprites' fanget fra Silkeborg"] {{webarchive |url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120822234958/http://www.dmi.dk/dmi/foerste_danske_red_sprites_fanget_fra_silkeborg |date=August 22, 2012}} ''[[Danish Meteorological Institute]]'', 20 August 2012. Retrieved: 20 August 2012.</ref>

== Mechanism ==
Sprites occur near the top of the [[mesosphere]] at about 80 km altitude in response to the electric field generated by lightning flashes in underlying thunderstorms. When a sufficiently large positive lightning strike carries [[Electric charge|charges]] to the ground, the cloud top is left with a strongly negative net charge. This may be modeled as a quasi-static electric dipole and for less than 10 milliseconds a strong [[electric field]] is generated in the region above the thunderstorm. In the low pressure of the upper mesosphere the [[breakdown voltage]] is drastically reduced, allowing for an [[electron avalanche]] to occur.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Sonnenfeld |first1=Richard G. |last2=Hager |first2=William W. |date=2013-05-01 |title=Electric Field Reversal in Sprite Electric Field Signature |url=https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/mwre/141/5/mwr-d-12-00220.1.xml |journal=Monthly Weather Review |language=en |volume=141 |issue=5 |pages=1731–1735 |doi=10.1175/MWR-D-12-00220.1 |bibcode=2013MWRv..141.1731S |issn=1520-0493}}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Pasko |first1=V. P. |last2=Inan |first2=U. S. |last3=Bell |first3=T. F. |last4=Taranenko |first4=Y. N. |date=March 1997 |title=Sprites produced by quasi-electrostatic heating and ionization in the lower ionosphere |url=https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/96JA03528 |journal=Journal of Geophysical Research: Space Physics |language=en |volume=102 |issue=A3 |pages=4529–4561 |doi=10.1029/96JA03528 |bibcode=1997JGR...102.4529P |issn=0148-0227|url-access=subscription }}</ref> Sprites get their characteristic red color from excitation of nitrogen in the low pressure environment of the upper mesosphere. At such low pressures [[Quenching (fluorescence)|quenching]] by atomic oxygen is much faster than that of nitrogen, allowing for nitrogen emissions to dominate, despite no difference in composition.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Sentman |first1=D. D. |last2=Stenbaek-Nielsen |first2=H. C. |last3=McHarg |first3=M. G. |last4=Morrill |first4=J. S. |date=2008-06-16 |title=Plasma chemistry of sprite streamers |url=https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2007JD008941 |journal=Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres |language=en |volume=113 |issue=D11 |article-number=2007JD008941 |doi=10.1029/2007JD008941 |bibcode=2008JGRD..11311112S |issn=0148-0227|url-access=subscription }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Liu |first1=Ningyu |last2=Pasko |first2=Victor P. |date=March 2005 |title=Molecular nitrogen LBH band system far-UV emissions of sprite streamers |url=https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2004GL022001 |journal=Geophysical Research Letters |language=en |volume=32 |issue=5 |article-number=2004GL022001 |doi=10.1029/2004GL022001 |bibcode=2005GeoRL..32.5104L |issn=0094-8276|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

== Sprite halo ==
Sometimes sprites are preceded, by approximately 1 millisecond, by a sprite ''[[Halo (optical phenomenon)|halo]]'', a pancake-shaped region of weak, transient [[optical]] [[light|emissions]] approximately {{convert|50|km|mi}} across and {{convert|10|km|mi}} thick. The halo is centered at approximately {{convert|70|km|mi}} altitude above the initiating lightning strike. These halos are thought to be produced by the same physical process that produces sprites, but for which the ionization is too weak to cross the threshold required for streamer formation. They may be mistaken for [[Upper-atmospheric lightning#ELVES|ELVES]], due to their visual similarity and short duration.<ref name="oEWY3">Rina Miyasato, Hiroshi Fukunishi, Yukihiro Takahashi, Michael J. Taylor, Hans. C. Stenbaek-Nielsen (2002). [https://web.archive.org/web/20090225025031/http://wwwsoc.nii.ac.jp/jepsjmo/cd-rom/2002cd-rom/pdf/j019/j019-011_e.pdf Characteristics of Lightning-induced Sprite Halos and Their Generation Mechanisms.] Academic Society Home Village. Retrieved on 2009-02-18. {{Dead link|date=December 2016}}</ref><ref name="b4rSg">Christopher Barrington Leigh (2000). [http://www-star.stanford.edu/~vlf/publications/theses/cpblThesis/node30.html Sprite halos.] {{webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080917210859/http://www-star.stanford.edu/~vlf/publications/theses/cpblThesis/node30.html |date=2008-09-17}} [[Stanford University]]. Retrieved on 2008-02-18.</ref><ref name="BLetal2001JGR">Barrington-Leigh, C. P., U. S. Inan, and M. Stanley, [http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2000JA000073/full "Identification of Sprites and Elves with Intensified Video and Broadband Array Photometry", J. Geophys. Res. 106, No. 2, February, 2001.]</ref>

Research carried out at [[Stanford University]] in 2000 indicates that, unlike sprites with bright vertical columnar structure, occurrence of sprite halos is not unusual in association with normal (negative) lightning discharges.<ref name="BLetal2001JGR" />
Research in 2004 by scientists from [[Tohoku University]] found that [[very low frequency]] emissions occur at the same time as the sprite, indicating that a discharge within the cloud may generate the sprites.<ref name="agu">{{Cite journal |doi=10.1029/2004GL021943 |title=VLF/ELF sferic evidence for in-cloud discharge activity producing sprites |date=2005 |first1=A. |last1=Ohkubo |first2=H. |last2=Fukunishi |first3=Y. |last3=Takahashi |first4=T. |last4=Adachi |s2cid=53059204 |journal=Geophysical Research Letters|volume=32 |issue=4 |pages=L04812 |bibcode = 2005GeoRL..32.4812O |doi-access=}}</ref>

== Related aircraft damage ==
Sprites have been blamed for otherwise unexplained incidents involving high altitude vehicular operations above thunderstorms. One example of this is the malfunction of a [[NASA]] [[stratosphere|stratospheric]] balloon launched on June 6, 1989, from [[Palestine, Texas]]. The balloon suffered an uncommanded payload release while flying at {{convert|120000|ft|m}} above a thunderstorm near [[Graham, Texas]]. Months after the incident, an investigation concluded that a "bolt of lightning" traveling upward from the clouds provoked the incident.<ref name="mun0s">{{cite web|title=Data of the stratospheric balloon launched on 6/5/1989 from Columbia Scientific Balloon Facility, Palestine, Texas, US for Molecules observation made fluorescent with a Laser |url=http://stratocat.com.ar/fichas-e/1989/PAL-19890605.htm | date=2009 |author=STRATOCAT |access-date=2009-02-18}}</ref> The attribution of the incident to a sprite was made retroactively, since this term was not coined until late 1993.
