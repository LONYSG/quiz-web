{{Short description|Air-borne movement of spiders and other invertebrates}}
[[File:Ballooning spiderlings.jpg|thumb|Spiderlings ballooning in the [[Santa Cruz Mountains]] of the San Francisco Peninsula]]
[[File:Ballooning spider positiv.png|thumb|Image from an observational study of ballooning in large spiders depicting stages of ballooning take off]]
'''Ballooning''', sometimes called '''kiting''', is a process by which [[spider]]s, and some other small [[invertebrate]]s, move through the air by releasing one or more [[Gossamer (spider silk)|gossamer threads]] to catch the wind, causing them to become airborne at the mercy of [[air current]]s and [[electric field]]s. A 2018 study concluded that electric fields provide enough force to lift spiders in the air, and possibly elicit ballooning behavior.<ref name="thefactsource">{{cite web |title=Can spiders fly? They are found 3 miles above ground. |url=https://thefactsource.com/can-spiders-fly-they-are-found-3-miles-above-ground/ |website=The Fact Source |date=3 July 2020 |access-date=2020-08-27 |archive-date=2020-08-06 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200806021233/https://thefactsource.com/can-spiders-fly-they-are-found-3-miles-above-ground/ |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Morley |first1=Erica L. |last2=Robert |first2=Daniel |title=Electric Fields Elicit Ballooning in Spiders |journal=Current Biology |date=July 2018 |volume=28 |issue=14 |pages=2324–2330.e2 |doi=10.1016/j.cub.2018.05.057 |pmid=29983315 |pmc=6065530 |bibcode=2018CBio...28E2324M }}</ref> This is primarily used by spiderlings to disperse; however, larger individuals have been observed doing so as well. The spider climbs to a high point and takes a stance with its abdomen to the sky, releasing fine silk threads from its [[spinneret]] until it becomes aloft. Journeys achieved vary from a few metres to hundreds of kilometres. Even atmospheric samples collected from balloons at five kilometres altitude and ships mid-ocean have reported spider landings. Ballooning can be dangerous due to predators and the unpredictable nature of long-distance ballooning, which may bring individuals to an unfavorable environment.

Ballooning is observed in many species of [[spider]]s, such as ''[[Erigone atra]]'', ''[[Cyclosa turbinata]]'', as well as in [[spider mite]]s (''[[Tetranychidae]]'') and in larvae of 31 species of [[lepidoptera]], distributed in 8 suborders. Bell and his colleagues put forward the hypothesis that ''ballooning'' first appeared in the Cretaceous.<ref>Bell, JR, DA Bohan, EM Shaw & GS Weyman. 2005. Ballooning dispersal using silk: world fauna, phylogenies, genetics and models <small> [ archive ]</small> . ''Bulletin of Entomological Research'' 95: 69-114.</ref> A 5-year-long research study in the 1920s–1930s revealed that 1 in every 17 invertebrates caught mid-air is a spider. Out of 28,739 specimens, 1,401 turned out to be spiders.<ref name="thefactsource" />

==Description==
[[File:Xysticus audax tiptoeing.ogv|thumb|''Xysticus audax'' tiptoeing, in preparation for ballooning]]
Ballooning is a behavior in which spiders and some other invertebrates use airborne dispersal to move between locations.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Heinrichs |first1=Ann |title=Spiders |date=2004 |publisher=Compass Point Books |isbn=978-0-7565-0590-5 |oclc=54027960 |url-access=registration |url=https://archive.org/details/spiders0000hein }}{{pn|date=November 2019}}</ref><ref name=instar>{{cite journal|last=Valerio |first=C.E. |year=1977 |title=Population structure in the spider ''Achaearranea Tepidariorum'' (Aranae, Theridiidae) |journal=Journal of Arachnology |volume=3 |issue=3 |pages=185–190 |jstor=3704941 }}</ref> A spider (usually limited to individuals of a small species), or spiderling after hatching,<ref>{{cite thesis |last1=Bond |first1=Jason Edward |title=Systematics and Evolution of the Californian Trapdoor Spider Genus Aptostichus Simon (Araneae: Mygalomorphae: Euctenizidae) |date=22 September 1999 |hdl=10919/29114 |citeseerx=10.1.1.691.8754 }}</ref> will climb as high as it can, stand on raised legs with its abdomen pointed upward ("tiptoeing"),<ref name=lab>{{cite journal |last=Weyman |first=G.S. |year=1995 |title=Laboratory studies of the factors stimulating ballooning behavior by Linyphiid spiders (Araneae, Linyphiidae) |journal=Journal of Arachnology |volume=23 |issue=2 |pages=75–84 |jstor=3705494 }}</ref> and then release several silk threads from its [[Spinneret (spider)|spinnerets]] into the air. These automatically form a triangular shaped parachute<ref name=anchor/> which carries the spider away on updrafts of winds where even the slightest of breezes will disperse the arachnid.<ref name=lab/><ref name=anchor/> The Earth's [[static electric field]] may also provide lift in windless conditions.<ref>{{cite arXiv|last=Gorham|first=Peter|title=Ballooning spiders: The case for electrostatic flight |date=Sep 2013|eprint=1309.4731 |class=physics.bio-ph}}</ref><ref>{{citation|title = Ballooning in spiders using multiple silk threads|author1-last= Habchi|author1-first= Charbel |author2-last=Jawed|author2-first=Mohammad K.|journal = Phys. Rev. E|volume = 105|issue = 3|  article-number = 034401|date=March 4, 2022| publisher = American Physical Society| doi = 10.1103/PhysRevE.105.034401|pmid= 35428095|arxiv= 2112.10981|bibcode= 2022PhRvE.105c4401H|s2cid= 245353548}}</ref>  Ballooning behavior may be triggered by favorable electric fields.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Morley |first1=Erica L. |last2=Robert |first2=Daniel |title=Electric Fields Elicit Ballooning in Spiders |journal=[[Current Biology]] |volume=28 |issue=14 |pages=2324–2330.e2 |date=5 July 2018 |doi=10.1016/j.cub.2018.05.057 |pmid=29983315 |pmc=6065530 |bibcode=2018CBio...28E2324M }}</ref><ref>{{cite news |last1=Young |first1=Ed |title=Spiders Can Fly Hundreds of Miles Using Electricity |url=https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/07/the-electric-flight-of-spiders/564437/ |access-date=5 July 2018 |work=[[The Atlantic]] |date=5 July 2018 |archive-date=5 July 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180705204140/https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/07/the-electric-flight-of-spiders/564437/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

Many spiders use especially fine [[Spider silk|silk]] called ''gossamer''<ref>Oxford English Dictionary 2nd ed.: Gossamer, noun and adjective: fine filmy substance, consisting of cobwebs, spun by small spiders, which is seen floating in the air in calm weather, esp. in autumn, or spread over a grassy surface: occas. with a and pl., a thread or web of gossamer.</ref> to lift themselves off a surface, and silk also may be used by a windblown spider to anchor itself to stop its journey.<ref name=anchor>{{cite journal |last=Schneider |first=J.M. |author2=Roos, J. |author3=Lubin, Y. |author4=Henschel, J.R. |date=October 2001 |title=Dispersal of ''Stegodyphus Dumicola'' (Araneae, Eresidae): They do balloon after all! |journal=Journal of Arachnology |volume=29 |issue=1 |pages=114–116 |doi=10.1636/0161-8202(2001)029[0114:DOSDAE]2.0.CO;2 |s2cid=4707752 |url=https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/226550 |access-date=2022-01-29 |archive-date=2022-10-09 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221009192733/https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/226550 |url-status=live }}</ref> The term "gossamer" is used [[metaphor]]ically for any exceedingly fine thread or fabric. Biologists also apply the term "balloon silk" to the threads that mechanically lift and drag systems.{{elucidate|date=September 2014}}

It is generally thought that most spiders heavier than 1&nbsp;[[milligram|mg]] are unlikely to use ballooning.<ref name="Suter">{{cite journal|author=Suter, R.B.|year=1999|title=An aerial lottery: The physics of ballooning in a chaotic atmosphere |journal=Journal of Arachnology |volume=27|issue=1|pages=281–293 |jstor=3705999 }}</ref>  Because many individuals die during ballooning, adults are less likely to balloon than spiderlings. However, adult females of several social ''[[Stegodyphus]]'' species (''S. dumicola'' and ''S. mimosarum'') weighing more than 100&nbsp;mg and with a body size of up to {{convert|14|mm|in|sp=us}} have been observed ballooning using rising [[thermal]]s on hot days without wind. These spiders use tens to hundreds of silk strands, which form a triangular sheet with a length and width of about {{convert|1|m|in|sp=us}}.<ref name=anchor />
[[File:Pardosa spp. ballooning.jpg|alt=Pardosa spp. attempting to balloon|thumb|Pardosa spp. attempting to balloon]]
In Australia, in 2012 and in May 2015, millions of spiders were reported to have ballooned into the air, making the ground where they landed seem snow-covered with their silk.<ref>{{Cite web|title = Millions of Spiders Rain Down on Australia—Why?|url = http://news.nationalgeographic.com/2015/05/150518-spiders-australia-silk-webs-animals-environment|archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20150521022229/http://news.nationalgeographic.com/2015/05/150518-spiders-australia-silk-webs-animals-environment/|archive-date = May 21, 2015|website = National Geographic News|access-date = 2015-05-19|first1 = Christine|last1 = Dell'Amore|date=May 18, 2005}}</ref>

==Distance and height achieved==
[[File:Threads of silk following a mass spider ballooning.jpg|thumb|left|Threads of silk following a mass spider ballooning]]
Most ballooning journeys end after just a few meters of travel, although depending on the spider's mass and posture,<ref>{{cite journal |last=Suter |first=R.B. |year=1992 |title=Ballooning: Data from spiders in freefall indicate the importance of posture |journal=Journal of Arachnology |volume=XX |issue= 2|pages=107–113 |jstor=3705774 }}</ref> a spider might be taken up into a [[jet stream]]. The trajectory further depends on the [[convection]] [[air current]]s and the [[Drag (physics)|drag]] of the silk and parachute to float and travel high up into the [[upper atmosphere]].<ref>{{cite journal |last=Greenstone |first=M.H. |author2=Morgan, C.E. |author3=Hultsh, A.-L. |year=1987 |title=Ballooning spiders in Missouri, USA, and New South Wales, Australia: Family and mass distributions |journal=Journal of Arachnology |volume=15 |issue=2 |pages=163–170 |jstor=3705725 }}</ref>

Many sailors have reported spiders being caught in their ship's sails over {{convert|1000|mi|km|sp=us}}<ref name=hawaii>{{cite journal |last=Hormiga |first=G. |year=2002 |title=Orsonwells, a new genus of giant linyphild spiders (Araneae) from the Hawaiian Islands |journal=Invertebrate Systematics |volume=16 |issue=3 |pages=369–448 |doi=10.1071/IT01026 }}</ref> from land (Heimer 1988). They have even been detected in atmospheric data balloons collecting air samples at slightly less than {{convert|5|km|ft|sp=us}} above sea level.<ref>{{cite web |last=VanDyk |first=J.K. |title=Entomology 201 - Introduction to insects |url=http://www.ent.iastate.edu/dept/courses/ent201/arthropoda/classarachnidasilk.html |date=2002–2009 |publisher=Department of Entomology, Iowa State University |access-date=18 July 2009 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090608172729/http://www.ent.iastate.edu/dept/courses/ent201/arthropoda/classarachnidasilk.html |archive-date=8 June 2009 }}</ref> Evidently, ballooning is the most common way for spiders to invade isolated islands and mountaintops.<ref name=hawaii/><ref>{{cite journal |last=Bilsing |first=S.W. |date=May 1920 |title=Quantitative studies in the food of spiders |journal=The Ohio Journal of Science |volume=20 |issue=7 |pages=215–260 |hdl=1811/867 }}</ref> Spiderlings are known to survive without food while travelling in air currents of jet streams for 25 days or longer.<ref name=instar/>

Some [[mite]]s and some [[caterpillar]]s also use silk to disperse through the air.<ref name="Clotuche Mailleux Astudillo Fernández Deneubourg p=e18854">{{cite journal | last1=Clotuche | first1=Gwendoline | last2=Mailleux | first2=Anne-Catherine | last3=Astudillo Fernández | first3=Aina | last4=Deneubourg | first4=Jean-Louis | last5=Detrain | first5=Claire | last6=Hance | first6=Thierry | editor-last=Planque | editor-first=Robert | title=The Formation of Collective Silk Balls in the Spider Mite Tetranychus urticae Koch | journal=PLOS ONE | publisher=Public Library of Science (PLoS) | volume=6 | issue=4 | date=2011-04-14 | issn=1932-6203 | doi=10.1371/journal.pone.0018854 | article-number=e18854| pmid=21533150 | pmc=3077419 | bibcode=2011PLoSO...618854C | doi-access=free }}</ref><ref name="Rosenwald Lill Lind Weiss pp. 833–842">{{cite journal | last1=Rosenwald | first1=Laura C. | last2=Lill | first2=John T. | last3=Lind | first3=Eric. M. | last4=Weiss | first4=Martha R. | title=Dynamics of host plant selection and host-switching by silver-spotted skipper caterpillars | journal=Arthropod-Plant Interactions | publisher=Springer Science and Business Media LLC | volume=11 | issue=6 | date=2017-05-10 | issn=1872-8855 | doi=10.1007/s11829-017-9538-0 | pages=833–842| bibcode=2017APInt..11..833R | s2cid=37474805 }}</ref>

A close association has been found between ballooning behaviors and the ability for a species of spiders to survive afloat on water. Water-repellent legs keep them alive on both fresh and salt water, enabling them to survive waves up to 0.5 meters in height. In wind many species raised their legs or abdomens to use as sails, propelling themselves across the water's surface. Many species of spiders also drop silk to anchor themselves in place while afloat. Said spiders did not show these behaviors on land, suggesting that they are adaptations to water.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Hayashi |first1=Morito |last2=Bakkali |first2=Mohammed |last3=Hyde |first3=Alexander |last4=Goodacre |first4=Sara L. |title=Sail or sink: novel behavioral adaptations on water in aerially dispersing species |journal=BMC Evolutionary Biology |date=3 July 2015 |volume=15 |issue=1 |page=118 |doi=10.1186/s12862-015-0402-5 |pmid=26138616 |pmc=4490750 |doi-access=free |bibcode=2015BMCEE..15..118H }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Cressey |first1=Daniel |title=Airborne spiders can sail on seas |journal=Nature |date=3 July 2015 |doi=10.1038/nature.2015.17906 |s2cid=186944562 }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |title=Flying spiders also sail on water |journal=Nature |date=8 July 2015 |volume=523 |issue=7559 |pages=130–131 |doi=10.1038/523130d |s2cid=4405075 |doi-access=free }}</ref>{{clear}}

== History ==
Although this phenomenon has been known since the time of Aristotle, the first precise observations were published by the [[arachnologist]] [[John Blackwall]] in 1827. Several studies have since made it possible to analyze this behavior. One of the most important and extensive studies exploring ballooning was funded by [[U.S. Department of Agriculture]] and performed between 1926 and 1931 by a group of scientists. The findings were published in 1939 in a 155-page bulletin compiled by P. A. Glick.<ref name="thefactsource" /><ref name="bulletin1">{{cite journal |last1=Glick |first1=P. A. |title=The distribution of insects, spiders and mites in the air |journal=Technical Bulletin of United States Department of Agriculture |date=May 1939 |issue=673 |url=https://naldc.nal.usda.gov/download/CAT86200667/PDF |access-date=28 August 2020 |archive-date=9 October 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20221009192729/https://naldc.nal.usda.gov/download/CAT86200667/PDF }}</ref>
