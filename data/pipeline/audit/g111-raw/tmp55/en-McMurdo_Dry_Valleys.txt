The '''McMurdo Dry Valleys''' are a series of largely [[Antarctic oasis|snow-free desert valleys]] on the continental coastline of [[Victoria Land]], [[Antarctica]], east of the [[East Antarctic Ice Sheet]] and west of [[McMurdo Sound]] and the [[Ross Sea]].<ref>{{cite news |last=Rejcek |first=Peter |title=In the cold of the night |work=[[The Antarctic Sun]] |date=29 November 2007 |url=http://antarcticsun.usap.gov/science/contenthandler.cfm?id=1283 |access-date=2008-01-13}}</ref> The Dry Valleys are the coldest and driest desert in the world, and the largest ice-free region in Antarctica; as a result, the Valleys have become a hotbed for scientific research in the Antarctic region. The Dry Valleys' climate is due to their location nestled within the [[Transantarctic Mountains]] along with high-pressure [[katabatic wind]]s that scour away snowfall and humidity across the region.  Surrounding mountains prevent the flow of ice from nearby [[glacier]]s. The region's geology is primarily [[granite]]s and [[gneiss]]es, and glacial [[till]]s dot the [[bedrock]] landscape, with loose gravel covering the ground. The Valleys are one of the driest places on Earth,<ref>{{Cite web|last=Cain|first=Fraser|date=2008-06-12|title=What is the Driest Place on Earth?|url=https://www.universetoday.com/15031/driest-place-on-earth/|access-date=2021-04-12|website=Universe Today|language=en-US}}</ref> though there are several anecdotal accounts of rainfall within them.<ref name="rain1">{{cite book |last1=Keys |first1=Harry (J.R.) |others=Additional credits on p. 51 |date=September 1980 |chapter=5.5 Precipitation—Other forms of precipitation |title=Air temperature, wind, precipitation and atmospheric humidity in the McMurdo Region, Antarctica |url=https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/antarctic/publications/antarctic-data-series/ADS10.pdf |url-status=live |series=Antarctic Data Series |volume=9 |publisher=[[Victoria University of Wellington]], Department of Geology |page=41 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220308063113/https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/antarctic/publications/antarctic-data-series/ADS10.pdf |archive-date=8 March 2022 |access-date=31 December 2023 }}</ref><ref name="rain2">{{Cite journal |last1=Obryk |first1=M. K. |last2=Doran |first2=P. T. |author-link2= Peter Doran |last3=Fountain |first3=A. G. |last4=Myers |first4=M. |last5=McKay |first5=C. P. |author-link5= Christopher McKay |date=16 July 2020 |title=Climate From the McMurdo Dry Valleys, Antarctica, 1986–2017: Surface Air Temperature Trends and Redefined Summer Season |url=https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2019JD032180 |journal=Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres |language=en |volume=125 |issue=13 |page=1 |doi=10.1029/2019JD032180 |bibcode=2020JGRD..12532180O |s2cid=219738421 |issn=2169-897X|url-access=subscription }}{{source-attribution}}</ref>

The region includes many features, including [[Lake Vida]], a saline lake, and the [[Onyx River]], a meltwater stream and Antarctica's longest river. Although no living organisms have been found in the [[permafrost]] here, [[Endolithic lichen|endolithic]] [[Photosynthesis|photosynthetic]] bacteria have been found living in the relatively moist interior of rocks. [[Anaerobic bacteria]], with a metabolism based on iron and sulfur, have also been found under the [[Taylor Glacier]].

Despite their extreme aridity, the McMurdo Dry Valleys contain a small but distinctive hydrological system. Summer melting from glaciers and seasonal snow can produce short-lived streams that feed lakes whose surfaces remain ice-covered for much of the year. Because evaporation and sublimation greatly exceed precipitation, dissolved salts can become concentrated in closed basins, producing saline and hypersaline waters. These features make the valleys an unusual combination of desert conditions, frozen lakes, episodic meltwater flow, and salt-rich soils.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Beverly |first=Damon N. |date=2025-12-22 |title=McMurdo Dry Valleys |url=https://desertsoftheworld.com/mcmurdo-dry-valleys/ |access-date=2026-10-14 |website=DesertsOfTheWorld.com |language=en-US}}</ref>

The valleys are located within the McMurdo Valleys [[Antarctic Specially Managed Area]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.ats.aq/devPH/apa/ep_protected_detail.aspx?type=3&id=75&lang=e |archive-url=https://archive.today/20130814202854/http://www.ats.aq/devPH/apa/ep_protected_detail.aspx?type=3&id=75&lang=e |archive-date=2013-08-14|title=ASMA 2: Mcmurdo Dry Valleys, Southern Victoria Land |publisher=[[Secretariat of the Antarctic Treaty]]}}</ref>

==Climate==
[[Image:Landsat7 dry valley lrg.jpg|thumb|McMurdo Dry Valleys, [[Landsat 7]] imagery acquired on December 18, 1999]]
The Dry Valleys are one of the most extreme habitats on Earth, and are classified as a [[cold desert]]. At approximately {{convert|4500|km2|mi2}},<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Levy |first=Joseph |date=February 2013 |title=How big are the McMurdo Dry Valleys? Estimating ice-free area using Landsat image data |url=https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0954102012000727/type/journal_article |journal=Antarctic Science |language=en |volume=25 |issue=1 |pages=119–120 |doi=10.1017/S0954102012000727 |issn=0954-1020|url-access=subscription }}</ref> the valleys constitute around 0.03% of the continent and form its largest ice-free region.

The region's low precipitation is the result of the [[Rain shadow|snow shadow]] that is created from the [[Transantarctic Mountains]] the valleys are situated in; the highest peaks in the region are some {{Convert|3000|m|ft}}.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Paerl |first=H.W. |last2=Priscu |first2=J.C. |date=1998-11-01 |title=Microbial Phototrophic, Heterotrophic, and Diazotrophic Activities Associated with Aggregates in the Permanent Ice Cover of Lake Bonney, Antarctica |url=https://doi.org/10.1007/s002489900109 |journal=Microbial Ecology |language=en |volume=36 |issue=3 |pages=221–230 |doi=10.1007/s002489900109 |issn=1432-184X|url-access=subscription }}</ref> As a result, much of the valley floors remain exposed and covered with loose gravel,  in which [[ice wedge]] [[patterned ground#Polygons|polygonal patterned ground]] may be observed.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Bockheim |first=J. G. |date=2002 |title=Landform and Soil Development in the McMurdo Dry Valleys, Antarctica: A Regional Synthesis |journal=[[Arctic, Antarctic, and Alpine Research]] |volume=34 |issue=3 |pages=308–317 |bibcode=2002AAAR...34..308B |doi=10.2307/1552489 |jstor=1552489 |doi-access=free}}</ref>

The conditions in the Dry Valleys are caused, in part, by [[katabatic wind]]s; these occur when cold, dense air is pulled downhill by the force of gravity. The dry wind evaporates the snow rapidly and little melts into the soil. During the summer, this process can take only hours.

Another important factor is a lack of precipitation. Precipitation averages around {{convert|100|mm|in|0}} per year over a century of records, almost exclusively in the form of snow.<ref name="rain1" /><ref name="rain2" /> This contributes to the low humidity of the area.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Hund |first1=Andrew J. |title=Antarctica and the Arctic Circle: A Geographic Encyclopedia of the Earth's Polar Regions |date=2014 |publisher=ABC-CLIO |isbn=9781610693936 |page=74 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=wRvFBAAAQBAJ&pg=PA474 |access-date=24 April 2019}}</ref>

Despite the lack of precipitation and surface-level ice, the Dry Valley region is home to over 6000 lakes and ponds.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Hawes |first=Ian |last2=Howard-Williams |first2=Clive |last3=Gilbert |first3=Neil |last4=Joy |first4=Kurt |date=2021-04-01 |title=Towards an Environmental Classification of Lentic Aquatic Ecosystems in the McMurdo Dry Valleys, Antarctica |url=https://doi.org/10.1007/s00267-021-01438-1 |journal=Environmental Management |language=en |volume=67 |issue=4 |pages=600–622 |doi=10.1007/s00267-021-01438-1 |issn=1432-1009|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

For several weeks in the summer, the temperature increases enough to allow for glacial melt, which causes small freshwater streams to form.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/2140/mcmurdo-dry-valleys |title=NASA: McMurdo Dry Valleys |author=<!--Not stated--> |date=26 January 2002 |website=NASA Earth Observatory |publisher=NASA |access-date=29 March 2022 }}</ref> These streams feed the lakes at the base of the valleys, which do not have outflows to the sea, causing them to become highly saline.{{Citation needed|date=October 2024}}

The maximum and minimum air temperatures recorded in the Dry Valleys are {{cvt|12.0|C|F}} at Taylor Glacier and {{cvt|−65.7|C|F}} at Lake Vida, respectively. The mean annual air temperature on the valleys floors ranges between {{cvt|−14.7|C|F}} at Lake Bonney to {{cvt|−29.6|C|F}} at Lake Vida. Within Taylor Valley, which has eight stations, the valley bottom mean annual temperatures ranges from {{cvt|−14.7|C|F}} to {{cvt|−23.0|C|F}}, in contrast to the four glaciers stations that range from {{cvt|−15.1|C|F}} to {{cvt|−19.4|C|F}}.<ref name="JGR">{{cite journal |last1=Obryk |first1=M. K. |last2=Doran |first2=P. T. | last3=Fountain | first3=A. G. | last4=Myiers | first4=M. | last5=McKay | first5=C. P. |date= 29 May 2020 |title= Climate From the McMurdo Dry Valleys, Antarctica, 1986–2017: Surface Air Temperature Trends and Redefined Summer Season |url=https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019JD032180 |journal=[[Journal of Geophysical Research]]: Atmospheres |volume= 125|issue=13 |publisher= |pages=1–14 |doi= |access-date=25 October 2025}}</ref>

High elevation sites ranged from {{cvt|−20.7|C|F}} to {{cvt|−25.2|C|F}}. The order of mean annual temperatures among the valleys, using valley bottom stations, warmest to coldest is Miers Valley {{cvt|−16.5|C|F}}, Taylor Valley {{cvt|−18.5|C|F}}, Wright Valley {{cvt|−19.8|C|F}} and Victoria Valley {{cvt|−26.8|C|F}}.<ref name="JGR" />

The McMurdo Oasis is bounded by the coastline of south [[Victoria Land]] and the [[Polar Plateau]]. The [[Taylor Valley|Taylor]] and [[Wright Valley]]s are major ice-free valleys within the [[Transantarctic Mountains]]. These "dry valleys" include [[hummocky]] [[moraine]]s, with frozen lakes, saline ponds, sand dunes, and meltwater streams. [[basement (geology)|Basement rocks]] include the Late [[Precambrian]] or Early [[Palaeozoic]] Skelton [[group (geology)|Group]] [[metamorphic rock]]s, primarily the Asgard [[formation (geology)|Formation]], which is a medium-high-grade marble and calc [[schist]]. The Palaeozoic [[Granite Harbour]] [[intrusive rock|intrusive]]s include [[granitoid]] [[pluton]]s and [[dike (geology)|dykes]], which intruded into the metasedimentary Skelton Group in the [[Late Cambrian]]–[[Early Ordovician]] during the [[Ross orogeny]]. The basement complex is overlain by the [[Jurassic]] [[Beacon Supergroup]], which is itself intruded by [[Karoo-Ferrar#Ferrar Supergroup|Ferrar Dolerite]] sheets and [[sill (geology)|sills]]. The [[McMurdo Volcanic Group]] intrudes, or is [[interbedding|interbedded]] with, the Taylor and Wright Valleys' moraines as [[basalt]]ic [[cinder cone]]s and [[lava flow]]s.  These basalts have ages between 2.1 and 4.4 [[Megaannum|Ma]]. The Dry Valley Drilling Project (1971–75) determined the [[Pleistocene]] layer within the Taylor Valley was between 137 and 275 m thick, and composed of interbedded [[sandstone]]s, pebble [[conglomerate (geology)|conglomerates]], and laminated silty [[mudstone]]s. This Pleistocene layer [[disconformity|disconformably]] overlies [[Pliocene]] and [[Miocene]] [[diamictite]]s.<ref>{{cite book |last1=McKelvey |first1=B. C. |editor1-last=Tingey |editor1-first=Robert |title=The Cainozoic glacial record in south Victoria Land: a geological evaluation of the McMurdo Sound drilling projects, in The Geology of Antarctica |date=1991 |publisher=Clarendon Press |location=Oxford |isbn=0198544677 |pages=434–454}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last1=Adams |first1=C. J. |last2=Whitla |first2=P. F. |editor1-last=Thomson |editor1-first=M. R. A. |editor2-last=Crame |editor2-first=J. A. |editor3-last=Thomson |editor3-first=J. W. |title=Precambrian ancestry of the Asgard Formation (Skelton Group): Rb-Sr age of basement metamorphic rocks in the Dry Valley region, Antarctica, in Geological Evolution of Antarctica |date=1991 |publisher=Cambridge University Press |location=Cambridge |isbn=9780521372664 |pages=129–135}}</ref>

==Research==

[[File:Mummified Seal Carcas in McMurdo Dry Valleys.jpg|[[Mummy|Mummified]] seal carcass|thumb]]
 
Since the Valleys' discovery in the early 1900s and the establishment of [[McMurdo Station]] (United States) and [[Scott Base]] (New Zealand) as research stations on nearby [[Ross Island]] in the 1950s, the Dry Valleys region has been extensively studied by scientists. The valleys' hyper-arid, cold-desert climate was soon identified as a high-fidelity terrestrial analogue for the environment of Mars, and geologists and astrophysicists have subsequently used the Valleys as a testing ground for analog-based studies since [[NASA]]'s [[Viking program|Viking Program]] in the 1970s.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=McKay |first=Christopher P. |last2=Andersen |first2=Dale |last3=Davila |first3=Alfonso |date=2017-07-03 |title=Antarctic environments as models of planetary habitats: University Valley as a model for modern Mars and Lake Untersee as a model for Enceladus and ancient Mars |url=https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2154896X.2017.1383705 |journal=The Polar Journal |language=en |volume=7 |issue=2 |pages=303–318 |doi=10.1080/2154896X.2017.1383705 |issn=2154-896X|url-access=subscription }}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=NASA Astrobiology |url=https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/missions/viking-1-and-2/#:~:text=Relevance%20to%20Astrobiology,NASA%20Astrobiology%20Involvement |access-date=2025-08-29 |website=astrobiology.nasa.gov |language=en-EN}}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Horowitz |first=N. H. |last2=Cameron |first2=Roy E. |last3=Hubbard |first3=Jerry S. |date=1972-04-21 |title=Microbiology of the Dry Valleys of Antarctica: Studies in the world's coldest and driest desert have implications for the Mars biological program. |url=https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.176.4032.242 |journal=Science |language=en |volume=176 |issue=4032 |pages=242–245 |doi=10.1126/science.176.4032.242 |issn=0036-8075|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

In 1985, the first continuously operating meteorological station was installed at [[Lake Hoare]], and in 1992, [[Taylor Valley]], the southernmost of the three major Dry Valleys, was selected as a [[National Science Foundation]] [[Long Term Ecological Research Network|Long Term Ecological Research]] (LTER) site, enabling for long-term scientific investigation of ecological processes over decadal timescales.<ref>{{Cite web |title=McMurdo Dry Valleys LTER Overview {{!}} McMurdo Dry Valleys LTER |url=https://mcm.lternet.edu/about |access-date=2025-08-29 |website=mcm.lternet.edu}}</ref> {{As of|2025|August}}, there are a total of 26 real-time meteorological stations, stream gauges, and observing platforms and a total of 1028 unique scientific publications affiliated or supported by the LTER program.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Sites Archive |url=https://lternet.edu/site/ |access-date=2025-08-29 |website=LTER |language=en-US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=MCM LTER Bibliography {{!}} McMurdo Dry Valleys LTER |url=https://mcm.lternet.edu/biblio |access-date=2025-08-29 |website=mcm.lternet.edu}}</ref>

Irish and American researchers conducted a field expedition in 2013 to [[University Valley]] in order to examine the microbial population and to test a drill designed for sampling on Mars in the permafrost of the driest parts of the valleys, the areas most analogous to the Martian surface. They found no living organisms in the permafrost, making University Valley the first location on the planet visited by humans with no detected active microbial life.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Goordial |first1=Jacqueline |last2=Davila |first2=Alfonso |last3=Lacelle |first3=Denis |last4=Pollard |first4=Wayne |last5=Marinova |first5=Margarita M. |last6=Greer |first6=Charles W. |last7=DiRuggiero |first7=Jocelyn |last8=McKay |first8=Christopher P. |last9=Whyte |first9=Lyle G. |date=19 January 2016 |title=Nearing the cold-arid limits of microbial life in permafrost of an upper dry valley, Antarctica |journal=[[The ISME Journal]] |volume=10 |issue=7 |pages=1613–1624 |bibcode=2016ISMEJ..10.1613G |doi=10.1038/ismej.2015.239  |doi-access=free|pmc=4918446 |pmid=27323892}}</ref>

A 2025 study of permafrost cores found diverse bacterial communities in lower Wright Valley and Pearse Valley, but almost no amplifiable DNA at the high-elevation Friis Hills (1,296 m); the New Zealand scientists suggested this reflects either a limit to DNA-based detection or a “[[Microbial treeline|microbiological treeline]]” above about 1,000 m in the Transantarctic Mountains.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Anderson |first=Jacob T.H. |last2=Marshall |first2=Alexis J. |last3=Richards-Babbage |first3=Roanna |last4=McDonald |first4=Ian R. |last5=Wilson |first5=Gary S. |last6=Abramov |first6=Andrey |last7=Demidov |first7=Nikita |last8=Cary |first8=S. Craig |date=2025-09-01 |title=Characterizing Microbial Communities in >7000- and >180,000-Year-Old Antarctic Permafrost Using a Low-Biomass Decontamination Protocol |url=https://doi.org/10.1177/15311074251369770 |journal=Astrobiology |language=EN |publisher=SAGE Publications |volume=25 |issue=9 |pages=633–647 |doi=10.1177/15311074251369770 |issn=1531-1074|doi-access=free }}</ref>

Similarly, much research has been done on analyzing the various ice-covered lakes and streams in the valleys. The lakes are unique physically due to a climate-induced balance between the maintenance of a thick, permanent ice cover due to low temperatures, the loss of ice to sublimation, and the replacement of water into the lakes themselves from seasonal glacial meltwater inflow.<ref>{{Citation |last=Wilson |first=A. T. |chapter=A review of the geochemistry and lake physics of the Antarctic dry areas |date=1981 |title=Antarctic Research Series |volume=33 |pages=185–192 |editor-last=McGinnis |editor-first=Lyle D. |chapter-url=http://www.agu.org/books/ar/v033/AR033p0185/AR033p0185.shtml |access-date=2025-08-29 |place=Washington, D. C. |publisher=American Geophysical Union |language=en |doi=10.1029/ar033p0185 |isbn=978-0-87590-177-0|chapter-url-access=subscription }}</ref><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Clow |first=Gary D. |last2=McKay |first2=Christopher P. |last3=Simmons |first3=George M. |last4=Wharton |first4=Robert A. |date=July 1988 |title=Climatological Observations and Predicted Sublimation Rates at Lake Hoare, Antarctica |url=http://journals.ametsoc.org/doi/10.1175/1520-0442(1988)0012.0.CO;2 |journal=Journal of Climate |language=en |volume=1 |issue=7 |pages=715–728 |doi=10.1175/1520-0442(1988)001<0715:COAPSR>2.0.CO;2 |issn=0894-8755|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

Research has discovered the presence of [[microbial mat]]s along glacial-melt streams, lakes, wetlands, and ice in the region. Dry Valley microbial mats are unique due to the constant scouring from katabatic winds.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Andriuzzi |first=Walter S. |last2=Stanish |first2=Lee F. |last3=Simmons |first3=Breana L. |last4=Jaros |first4=Chris |last5=Adams |first5=Byron J. |last6=Wall |first6=Diana H. |last7=McKnight |first7=Diane M. |date=2018-10-01 |title=Spatial and temporal patterns of microbial mats and associated invertebrates along an Antarctic stream |url=https://doi.org/10.1007/s00300-018-2331-4 |journal=Polar Biology |language=en |volume=41 |issue=10 |pages=1911–1921 |doi=10.1007/s00300-018-2331-4 |issn=1432-2056|url-access=subscription }}</ref>

[[Endolith]]ic [[bacteria]] have been found living in the Dry Valleys, sheltered from the dry air in the relatively moist interior of rocks.  Summer [[meltwater]] from the glaciers provides the primary source of [[soil nutrient]]s.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Barrett|first1=J. E.|last2=Virginia|first2=R. A.|last3=Lyons|first3=W. B.|last4=McKnight|first4=D. M.|last5=Priscu|first5=J. C.|last6=Doran|first6=P. T.|last7=Fountain|first7=A. G.|last8=Wall|first8=D. H.|last9=Moorhead|first9=D. L.|date=2007-03-01|title=Biogeochemical stoichiometry of Antarctic Dry Valley ecosystems|journal=Journal of Geophysical Research: Biogeosciences|language=en|volume=112|issue=G1|pages=G01010|doi=10.1029/2005JG000141|bibcode=2007JGRG..112.1010B|issn=2156-2202|url=https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1015&context=geog_fac|doi-access=free}}</ref>

[[Anaerobic organism|Anaerobic]] bacteria whose metabolism is based on iron and sulfur live in sub-freezing temperatures under the [[Taylor Glacier]].

It was previously thought that algae were staining the red ice emerging at [[Blood Falls]], but it is now known that the staining is caused by high levels of [[iron oxide]].<ref>{{cite web |first=John |last=Timmer |url=https://arstechnica.com/science/2009/04/ancient-frozen-ecosystem-produces-blood-red-ice-flows/ |title=Ancient, frozen ecosystem produces blood-red ice flows |website=[[Ars Technica]] |date=16 April 2009 |access-date=17 April 2009}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Mikucki |first1=Jill A. |first2=Ann |last2=Pearson |first3=David T. |last3=Johnston |first4=Alexandra V. |last4=Turchyn |first5=James |last5=Farquhar |first6=Daniel P. |last6=Schrag |first7=Ariel D. |last7=Anbar |first8=John C. |last8=Priscu |first9=Peter A. |last9=Lee |title=A Contemporary Microbially Maintained Subglacial Ferrous "Ocean" |journal=Science |volume=324 |issue=5925 |pages=397–400 |date=17 April 2009 |doi=10.1126/science.1167350 |pmid=19372431|bibcode=2009Sci...324..397M |s2cid=44802632}}</ref>

In 2014, drones were used in the McMurdo Dry Valleys by a team of scientists from [[Auckland University of Technology (AUT) Library|Auckland University of Technology (AUT)]] to create baseline maps of the vegetation. In 2015, the New Zealand Antarctic Research Institute granted funding to AUT to develop methods for operating [[unmanned aerial vehicle]]s. Over successive summer seasons in Antarctica, the AUT team created three dimensional maps with sub-centimeter resolution, which are now used as baselines.<ref>{{Cite journal|date=2019|title=Mapping biodiversity in a changing Antarctica|journal=Antarctic Magazine, NZ Antarctic Society|volume=37|pages=20–21|issn=0003-5327}}</ref>

Part of the Valleys was designated an environmentally protected area in 2004.{{Citation needed|date=October 2024}}

==Major geographic features==

===Valleys===
* [[Alatna Valley]] (sometimes called ''Atlanta Valley'')<ref>{{cite map |website=Antarctica New Zealand |url=http://www.antarcticanz.govt.nz/downloads/environment/Map%20A%20McMurdo%20Dry%20Valleys%20Area.pdf |title=Map A: Map of the McMurdo Dry Valleys Area |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090327024059/http://www.antarcticanz.govt.nz/downloads/environment/Map%20A%20McMurdo%20Dry%20Valleys%20Area.pdf |archive-date=2009-03-27}}</ref> is the northernmost, north of [[Benson Glacier]].

From north to south, the three main valleys are
* [[Victoria Valley]] (between [[Saint Johns Range|St. Johns Range]] in the north and [[Olympus Range]] in the south)
* [[Wright Valley]] (between [[Olympus Range]] in the north and [[Asgard Range]] in the south)
* [[Taylor Valley]] (between [[Asgard Range]] in the north and [[Kukri Hills]] in the south)

West of Victoria Valley are, from north to south,
* [[Barwick Valley]]
* [[Balham Valley]]
* [[McKelvey Valley]]

Stretching south from Balham Valley are, from west to east:
* [[Priscu Valley]]
* [[Wall Valley]]
* [[Virginia Valley]]
* [[Stuiver Valley]]

===Lakes===
Some of the lakes of the Dry Valleys rank among the world's most saline lakes, with a higher salinity than [[Lake Assal (Djibouti)|Lake Assal]] or the [[Dead Sea]]. The most saline of all is small [[Don Juan Pond]].
* [[Lake Vida]] (Victoria Valley)
* [[Lake Thomas]] (Victoria Valley)
* [[Lake Vanda]] (Wright Valley)
* [[Lake Brownworth]] (Wright Valley) (freshwater)
* [[Don Juan Pond]] (Wright Valley)
* [[Lake Fryxell]] (Taylor Valley)
* [[Lake Hoare]] (Taylor Valley)
* [[Lake Chad (Antarctica)|Lake Chad]] (Taylor Valley)
* [[Parera Pond]] (Taylor Valley) (freshwater)
* [[Lake Bonney (Antarctica)|Lake Bonney]] (Taylor Valley)
* [[Lake Joyce]] (Pearse Valley)
* [[Lake Garwood]] (Garwood Valley)
