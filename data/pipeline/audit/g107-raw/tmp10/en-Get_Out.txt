'''''Get Out''''' is a 2017 American <!--The consensus is to list the main genre only in lead-->[[psychological horror film]] written, co-produced, and directed by [[Jordan Peele]] in his [[List of directorial debuts|directorial debut]]. It stars [[Daniel Kaluuya]], [[Allison Williams (actress)|Allison Williams]], [[Lil Rel Howery]], [[LaKeith Stanfield]], [[Bradley Whitford]], [[Caleb Landry Jones]], [[Stephen Root]], [[Catherine Keener]], and [[Betty Gabriel]]. The plot follows a young black man (Kaluuya), who uncovers shocking secrets when he meets the family of his white girlfriend (Williams).

==Plot==
On a suburban street at night, a Black man walks alone, talking on his phone. After a car approaches him, the man, sensing trouble, attempts to escape, but is subdued, dragged into the car and driven away.

Six months later, Black photographer Chris Washington travels to [[upstate New York]] for a weekend getaway to meet the family of his white girlfriend, Rose Armitage. Upon arrival, he has uncomfortable conversations with Rose's father Dean, a [[Neurosurgery|neurosurgeon]], and mother Missy, a psychiatrist. Later, Rose's brother Jeremy arrives, and Chris observes eerie behavior from the family's Black staff, Georgina and Walter.

Missy manipulates Chris into an individual hypnotherapy session, using the sound of a spoon stirring in a teacup as a hypnotic trigger, ostensibly to cure his smoking habit. While entranced, he reveals that his mother died in a [[Hit and run|hit-and-run]] when he was 11 years old; he feels partially responsible, as he delayed calling for help. At Missy's prompting, his consciousness falls into a dark void she dubs the "sunken place" where his body becomes temporarily paralyzed. The next morning, he finds that he no longer has a desire to smoke.

Dozens of wealthy white guests arrive for the Armitages' annual get-together and continually make uncomfortable remarks toward Chris. Chris meets blind art dealer Jim Hudson, who takes an interest in his photography, and a Black man named Logan King. Like Walter and Georgina, Logan behaves unnaturally, and is married to a white woman thirty years his senior. When Chris photographs Logan with flash on, Logan lunges toward Chris, frantically telling him to "get out." Dean removes him, later explaining the sudden erratic behavior as a [[seizure]].

Chris confides in Rose about his worries, and both ultimately agree to return home. Meanwhile, as the party guests have a [[silent auction#Other features|silent auction]] for a photo of him, Chris sends the photo of Logan to his friend, [[Transportation Security Administration|TSA]] officer Rod Williams. Rod recognizes Logan as Andre Hayworth, a missing man from Brooklyn (and the man who was taken in the opening scene). Chris discovers photos of Rose dating numerous Black individuals, including Walter and Georgina, and he tries to leave, but Jeremy and Dean block the doors. Rose, revealing herself as an accomplice, refuses to give him his keys, and Missy hypnotizes him back to the sunken place.

Awakening strapped to an armchair in the basement, Chris views a video in which Dean's father Roman explains that the Armitage family [[brain transplant|transplants the brains]] of wealthy white people into Black bodies to acquire their physical characteristics, leaving the host's consciousness trapped in the "sunken place". Jim then reveals that he is going to be transferred into Chris's body, but maintains that he does not care about the color of Chris's skin and only covets a body with working eyesight. Meanwhile, Rod consults the police, who laugh off his theory about the Armitages.

In another room, Dean begins the transplantation process, removing the top of Jim's skull. Jeremy arrives to retrieve Chris, who has seemingly been hypnotized by a clip of the teacup sound earlier, but Chris awakens and bludgeons him—having blocked the trigger by plugging his ears with cotton stuffing from the armchair. Chris kills Dean, who sets the operating room ablaze, then Missy, then Jeremy when he attacks again. Attempting to escape in Jeremy's Porsche, Chris accidentally hits Georgina, who possesses the brain of Rose's grandmother Marianne. Reminded of his own mother's death, Chris guiltily takes her into the car, but she reawakens and attacks him, causing the car to crash and kill her.

Rose and Walter, who possesses Roman's brain, arrive to capture Chris. During a scuffle, Chris uses his phone's flash to free Walter from Roman's control. Walter shoots Rose with her rifle, then fatally shoots himself. Chris begins strangling Rose, but relents when she manipulatively pleads for her life and promises she loves him. A police car arrives; Rose cries out for help, but Rod emerges from the car and picks up Chris, leaving Rose to bleed out on the road.<!-- DO NOT CHANGE based on whether you believe she dies or not. ===Development===
[[File:Jordan Peele Peabody 2014 (cropped).jpg|thumb|left|214x214px|''Get Out'' is Jordan Peele's directorial debut.]]
''Get Out'' is the directorial debut of [[Jordan Peele]], who had previously worked in comedy, including the sketch show ''[[Key & Peele]]''.<ref name="Forbes">{{cite web|last1=Mendelson|first1=Scott|title=Jordan Peele Talks 'Get Out' And His Love For Horror Movies|url=https://www.forbes.com/sites/scottmendelson/2016/10/05/interview-jordan-peele-discusses-get-out-and-his-love-for-the-horror-genre/|website=Forbes.com|access-date=February 10, 2017|archive-date=September 14, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180914170615/https://www.forbes.com/sites/scottmendelson/2016/10/05/interview-jordan-peele-discusses-get-out-and-his-love-for-the-horror-genre/|url-status=live}}</ref> Peele has said that horror and comedy share similar mechanics (such as timing, rhythm and surprise), and that his comedy background helped inform the film’s approach to tension and reveals.<ref name="Forbes" /> He cited ''[[The Stepford Wives (1975 film)|The Stepford Wives]]'' (1975) as an influence on its satirical premise.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://editorial.rottentomatoes.com/article/key-and-peele-star-talks-about-his-upcoming-horror-movie/|website=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|author=Ricard, Maria|date=March 9, 2015|title=Key & Peele Star Talks About His Upcoming Horror Movie|access-date=October 7, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260713062304/https://editorial.rottentomatoes.com/article/key-and-peele-star-talks-about-his-upcoming-horror-movie|archive-date=July 13, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> As the film deals with racism, Peele has said the story is personal to him, though not autobiographical.<ref name="Forbes" />

Peele was introduced to producer [[Sean McKittrick]] by comedy partner [[Keegan-Michael Key]] in 2013.<ref name="vulture">{{cite web|url=http://www.vulture.com/2018/02/making-get-out-jordan-peele.html|title=The First Great Movie of the Trump Era|last=Yuan|first=Jada|author2=Hunter Harris|date=February 22, 2018|publisher=[[New York (magazine)#Digital|Vulture.com]]|access-date=March 21, 2018|quote=Williams: My instinct is that Rose [...] would she keep doing this?" and he said, 'Yeah.'|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200926020613/https://www.vulture.com/2018/02/making-get-out-jordan-peele.html|archive-date=September 26, 2020|url-status=live}}</ref> After Peele pitched the story during a meeting in New Orleans, McKittrick committed to producing and commissioned Peele to write the screenplay; Peele completed the first draft in about two months.<ref name="vulture" />

Although filmed in Alabama, Peele has said the story was not intended to read as set in the South; he wanted to avoid familiar regional stereotypes and instead place the film's racism within a more outwardly "liberal" social environment.<ref>{{Cite web|last1=Butler|first1=Bethonie|date=February 23, 2017|title=Jordan Peele made a woke horror film|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/lifestyle/style/jordan-peele-made-a-woke-horror-film/2017/02/22/5162f21e-f549-11e6-a9b0-ecee7ce475fc_story.html|access-date=July 14, 2021|website=WashingtonPost|language=en-US|archive-date=November 28, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201128224056/https://www.washingtonpost.com/lifestyle/style/jordan-peele-made-a-woke-horror-film/2017/02/22/5162f21e-f549-11e6-a9b0-ecee7ce475fc_story.html|url-status=live}}</ref> A contemporary report described the film as set in [[Upstate New York]].<ref>{{Cite web|last1=Herbert|first1=Geoff|date=January 4, 2017|title=Hit new movie 'Get Out' turns racial issues into horror story in Upstate NY|url=https://www.syracuse.com/entertainment/2017/02/get_out_movie_jordan_peele_upstate_ny.html|access-date=July 14, 2021|website=Syracuse|language=en-US|archive-date=July 14, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210714155534/https://www.syracuse.com/entertainment/2017/02/get_out_movie_jordan_peele_upstate_ny.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

Peele has described developing the concept of the "sunken place" from the sensation of falling as one drifts to sleep, and from the idea of being trapped as an observer behind one's own eyes while one's body is controlled by someone else.<ref name="Nast 2019">{{cite magazine | title=The 25 Most Influential Movie Scenes of the Past 25 Years | magazine=Vanity Fair | date=January 25, 2019 | url=https://www.vanityfair.com/hollywood/2019/01/25-best-movie-scenes | access-date=February 1, 2019 | archive-date=November 7, 2020 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201107233809/https://www.vanityfair.com/hollywood/2019/01/25-best-movie-scenes | url-status=live }}</ref> In the same interview, he connected the idea to themes of abduction and to a metaphor for the [[Prison–industrial complex|prison industrial complex]].<ref name="Nast 2019" />

[[Lil Rel Howery]] has said the film's allegory draws on the historical fears and traumas experienced by African Americans, and he cited events such as [[Racial segregation in the United States|racial segregation]] and the murder of [[Emmett Till]] as part of that cultural context.<ref>{{cite news |last=Mistry |first=Anupa |url=https://www.thefader.com/2017/03/03/lil-rel-get-out-interview |title=Meet Lil Rel, The Scene-Stealing TSA Agent From Get Out |work=[[The Fader]] |date=March 3, 2017 |access-date=August 10, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260114132433/https://www.thefader.com/2017/03/03/lil-rel-get-out-interview |archive-date=January 14, 2026 |url-status=live }}</ref> Peele also expressed concerns before release about whether White audiences would resist being implicated as villains, and whether Black audiences would want to see Black characters placed in peril.<ref name="LAPhenom">{{cite news|url=http://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-get-out-box-office-phenomenon-20170321-story.html|title=Jordan Peele on how 'Get Out' defied the odds to become a full-blown cultural phenomenon|last1=Rottenberg|first1=Josh|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|access-date=August 8, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260316151751/https://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-get-out-box-office-phenomenon-20170321-story.html|archive-date=March 16, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref>

==== Alternative endings ====
In the original ending, Chris kills Rose and is arrested by the police; Rod then visits him in jail, where Chris insists the threat has been stopped.<ref name="DofG2023">{{cite web |last1=Crow |first1=David |date=October 10, 2023 |title=Get Out's Original Ending Was a Lot Bleaker (and More Truthful) |url=https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/get-out-original-ending-bleaker-truthful/ |access-date=October 12, 2023 |website=[[Den of Geek]] |publisher=Den of Geek World Limited |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260625155217/https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/get-out-original-ending-bleaker-truthful/ |archive-date=June 25, 2026 |url-status=live}}</ref> Peele has said he originally intended the ending to reflect the realities of racism, but later shifted approach after considering the cultural moment and audience reactions at [[test screening]]s, opting for a more hopeful resolution while still preserving a brief moment where viewers might fear Chris will be arrested.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.slashfilm.com/get-out-alternate-ending/|title=Jordan Peele's 'Get Out' Almost Had a Much More Bleak Ending|last=Anderton|first=Ethan|work=Slash Film|date=March 5, 2017|access-date=March 12, 2017|archive-date=March 8, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210308024205/https://www.slashfilm.com/get-out-alternate-ending/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://collider.com/get-out-alternate-ending-explained/|title=Get Out Filmmakers Explain Why They Changed The Ending|last=Chitwood|first=Adam|work=Collider|date=February 22, 2018|access-date=April 4, 2018|archive-date=April 5, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180405024632/http://collider.com/get-out-alternate-ending-explained/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Peele considered several other endings, including one in which Rod finds Chris at the estate but realizes Chris is no longer himself.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.yahoo.com/movies/jordan-peele-reveals-get-alternate-ending-095942268.html|title=Jordan Peele Reveals 'Get Out' Has Alternate Ending|last=Paschal|first=Nicholas|work=Yahoo|date=May 15, 2017|access-date=May 16, 2017|archive-date=October 21, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171021111437/https://www.yahoo.com/movies/jordan-peele-reveals-get-alternate-ending-095942268.html|url-status=live}}</ref>

Peele has said he wanted the music to foreground Black voices and musical traditions, while avoiding genre shorthand such as a "voodoo" motif, and Abels incorporated [[Swahili language|Swahili]] vocals along with a [[blues]] influence.<ref name="Pulliam-Moore">{{cite news |last=Pulliam-Moore |first=Charles |url=https://splinternews.com/the-hidden-swahili-message-in-get-out-the-country-needs-1793858917 |title=The hidden Swahili message in 'Get Out' the country needs to hear |work=Splinter News |date=March 1, 2017 |access-date=March 17, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230801142431/https://splinternews.com/the-hidden-swahili-message-in-get-out-the-country-needs-1793858917 |archive-date=August 1, 2023 |url-status=dead }}</ref> The refrain "Sikiliza Kwa Wahenga" is Swahili for "listen to your ancestors", and Peele said it functions as a warning directed at Chris and echoes the film's title and premise.<ref name="GQinterview">{{cite web|last1=Weaver|first1=Caity|title=Jordan Peele on the Most Terrifying Horror Story: Being Black in America|url=https://www.gq.com/story/jordan-peele-get-out-interview|website=Gq.com|date=February 3, 2017|access-date=February 11, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210518081857/https://www.gq.com/story/jordan-peele-get-out-interview|archive-date=May 18, 2021|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="Pulliam-Moore"/>

The song "[[Redbone (song)|Redbone]]" by [[Donald Glover|Childish Gambino]] appears at the beginning of the film.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://hiphopdx.com/news/id.42476/title.jordan-peele-explains-why-childish-gambinos-redbone-was-perfect-for-get-out|title=Jordan Peele Explains Why Childish Gambino's 'Redbone' Was Perfect for 'Get Out'|website=HipHopDX.com|access-date=April 1, 2017|language=en-US|archive-date=March 31, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170331154540/http://hiphopdx.com/news/id.42476/title.jordan-peele-explains-why-childish-gambinos-redbone-was-perfect-for-get-out|url-status=live}}</ref> Other songs in the film include "[[Run, Rabbit, Run]]" by [[Flanagan and Allen]] and "[[(I've Had) The Time of My Life]]" by [[Bill Medley]] and [[Jennifer Warnes]].<ref>{{cite web |title=Get Out soundtrack and songs list |url=https://www.insoundtrack.com/movie/get-out-2017 |access-date=September 26, 2022 |website=insoundtrack.com |date=April 26, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260512163836/https://www.insoundtrack.com/movie/get-out-2017 |archive-date=May 12, 2026 |url-status=live }}</ref>

== Themes and interpretations ==
''Get Out'' has been described as critical of [[post-racial America]], the concept of "colorblindness", and [[neoliberalism]].<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Train |first1=Emma |title=What is not real can be felt into being: affective threat in Jordan Peele's Get Out |journal=New Review of Film and Television Studies |date=October 2, 2021 |volume=19 |issue=4 |pages=439–461 |doi=10.1080/17400309.2021.1919481 |s2cid=236588530 |url=https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17400309.2021.1919481 |access-date=April 28, 2023 |issn=1740-0309|url-access=subscription }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Landsberg |first1=Alison |title=Horror vérité: politics and history in Jordan Peele's Get Out (2017) |journal=Continuum |date=September 3, 2018 |volume=32 |issue=5 |pages=629–642 |doi=10.1080/10304312.2018.1500522 |s2cid=150219385 |url=https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10304312.2018.1500522 |access-date=April 28, 2023 |issn=1030-4312 |url-access=subscription |hdl=1920/11816 |hdl-access=free |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230501202949/https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10304312.2018.1500522 |archive-date=May 1, 2023 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="sundown">{{cite journal |last1=Patton |first1=Elizabeth A. |title=Get Out and the legacy of sundown suburbs in post-racial America |journal=New Review of Film and Television Studies |date=July 3, 2019 |volume=17 |issue=3 |pages=349–363 |doi=10.1080/17400309.2019.1622889 |s2cid=195386323 |issn=1740-0309|doi-access=free }}</ref> Lanre Bakare in ''[[The Guardian]]'' notes, "The villains here aren't [[Southern United States|southern]] rednecks or [[neo-Nazi]] skinheads, or the so-called '[[alt-right]]'. They're middle-class White liberals. [...] The thing ''Get Out'' does so well—and the thing that will rankle with some viewers—is to show how, however unintentionally, these same people can make life so hard and uncomfortable for black people. It exposes a liberal ignorance and hubris that has been allowed to fester. It's an attitude, an arrogance which in the film leads to a horrific final solution, but in reality leads to a complacency that is just as dangerous."<ref>{{cite news|first=Lanre|last=Bakare|url=https://www.theguardian.com/film/2017/feb/28/get-out-box-office-jordan-peele|title=Get Out: the film that dares to reveal the horror of liberal racism in America|newspaper=[[The Guardian]]|date=February 28, 2017|access-date=April 28, 2023}}</ref>

Peele stated that the character of Hudson, who literally is colorblind due to his physical blindness, "still plays a part in the system of racism", due to his belief that the eyesight of a black photographer will give him an "advantage".<ref name="RS 2019">{{cite magazine |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-features/director-jordan-peele-new-movie-cover-story-782743/ |title=The All-American Nightmares of Jordan Peele |last=Hiatt |first=Brian |date=January 29, 2019 |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |access-date=February 1, 2019 |archive-date=January 31, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190131042023/https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/movie-features/director-jordan-peele-new-movie-cover-story-782743/ |url-status=live }}</ref> Hudson distances himself from the racial context of taking Chris's body, claiming to be only interested in his eyesight and reducing him to an aesthetic.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Jarvis |first1=Michael |title=Anger translator: Jordan Peele's Get Out |journal=Science Fiction Film and Television |date=2018 |volume=11 |issue=1 |pages=97–109 |doi=10.3828/sfftv.2018.9 |s2cid=194008250 |url=https://muse.jhu.edu/article/686936 |access-date=April 28, 2023 |issn=1754-3789 |url-access=subscription |archive-date=June 23, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240623212033/https://muse.jhu.edu/article/686936 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="afropess"/>

Scholar Thai-Catherine Matthews draws parallels between Chris and [[Barack Obama]], noting their "suspension" between racial and social identities. Matthews says Obama comes to the conclusion that this "suspension" can foster positive relations in his memoir ''[[Dreams from My Father]]'', while ''Get Out'' "views suspension as the ultimate hell".<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Matthews |first1=Thai-Catherine |title=Screaming with Laughter: How Jordan Peele's Get Out Rereads Obama by Rewriting the Black Messiah |journal=Black Camera |date=2022 |volume=13 |issue=2 |pages=139–161 |doi=10.2979/blackcamera.13.2.07 |s2cid=258154708 |url=https://muse.jhu.edu/article/851872 |access-date=April 28, 2023 |issn=1947-4237 |url-access=subscription |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250504070254/https://muse.jhu.edu/article/851872 |archive-date=May 4, 2025 |url-status=live }}</ref> Ryan Poll cited the film as an example of [[Afropessimism (philosophy)|Afro-pessimism]].<ref name="afropess">{{cite journal |last1=Poll |first1=Ryan |title=Can One "Get Out?" The Aesthetics of Afro-Pessimism |journal=The Journal of the Midwest Modern Language Association |date=2018 |volume=51 |issue=2 |pages=69–102 |jstor=45151156 |url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/45151156 |access-date=April 28, 2023 |issn=0742-5562 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250815142821/https://www.jstor.org/stable/45151156 |archive-date=August 15, 2025 |url-status=live}}</ref>

The film also depicts the lack of attention on missing black Americans compared to [[Missing white woman syndrome|missing White girls and women]]. ''[[Slate (magazine)|Slate]]''{{'}}s [[Damon Young (writer)|Damon Young]] stated the film's premise was "depressingly plausible ... Although black people only comprise 13 percent of America's population, they are 34 percent of America's missing, a reality that exists as the result of a mélange of racial and socioeconomic factors rendering black lives demonstratively less valuable than the lives [of] our White counterparts."<ref>{{cite web |first=Damon |last=Young |title=The Disturbing Truth That Makes ''Get Out'' Depressingly Plausible |url=http://www.slate.com/blogs/browbeat/2017/03/10/get_out_and_america_s_many_missing_black_people.html |department=Browbeat (blog) |website=[[Slate (website)|Slate]] |date=March 10, 2017 |access-date=April 23, 2017 |archive-date=April 24, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170424182346/http://www.slate.com/blogs/browbeat/2017/03/10/get_out_and_america_s_many_missing_black_people.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

Peele wrote Rose as a subversion of the [[White savior]] trope, and in particular, of films where most White characters are evil, but one is good.<ref>{{cite web|author=Ramos, Dino-Day|url=https://deadline.com/2017/10/jordan-peele-get-out-film-independent-forum-keynote-speaker-diversity-inclusion-1202192699/|title='Get Out' Director Jordan Peele On Divisiveness, Black Identity & The "White Savior"|website=[[Deadline Hollywood]], Penske Business Media, LLC|date=October 22, 2017|access-date=March 21, 2018|quote=With the character of Rose (Allison Williams), the audience expects to see her come out as the White savior to save Chris, [...] He takes a beat and jokes, 'Sometimes all White people are evil — ''sometimes'' — but not ''all'' the time.'|archive-date=March 22, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180322142817/http://deadline.com/2017/10/jordan-peele-get-out-film-independent-forum-keynote-speaker-diversity-inclusion-1202192699/|url-status=live}}</ref> Peele and Williams stated that Rose behaved like a teenager as her emotional development was delayed.<ref name=YamatoJencreep>{{cite news|last=Yamato|first=Jen|url=http://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-get-out-milk-horror-jordan-peele-allison-williams-20170301-story.html|title=Jordan Peele explains 'Get Out's' creepy milk scene, ponders the recent link between dairy and hate|newspaper=[[Los Angeles Times]]|date=March 1, 2017|access-date=March 21, 2018|quote="She and Peele even coined a name for Rose's true self. 'We started to refer to her as Ro Ro,' Williams said. [...]" and "'Oh my God. Well, I intended that,' he joked of the uncannily prescient timing to milk's sudden surge in popularity with White supremacists.|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260628230102/https://www.latimes.com/entertainment/movies/la-et-mn-get-out-milk-horror-jordan-peele-allison-williams-20170301-story.html|archive-date=June 28, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> Williams believed that Rose was not a victim of indoctrination, hypnotism, or [[Stockholm syndrome]], but simply evil.<ref name="vulture"/><ref>{{cite news|author=Branigan, Anne|url=https://www.theroot.com/why-2017-was-the-year-of-rose-armitage-1821137750|title=Why 2017 Was the Year of Rose Armitage|work=[[The Root (magazine)|The Root]]|date=December 24, 2017|access-date=March 21, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260430142030/https://www.theroot.com/why-2017-was-the-year-of-rose-armitage-1821137750|archive-date=April 30, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> After Rose's intentions are revealed, her previous "soft and welcoming" appearance becomes a "vision of cold, meticulous elitism", with hunting [[jodhpurs]], a white dress shirt, and a "sleek ponytail"; she hangs photographs of her ex-partners on her wall like hunting trophies.<ref name=YamatoJencreep /> 

==Reception==
===Box office===
''Get Out'' grossed $176&nbsp;million in the United States and Canada and $79.4&nbsp;million in other territories for a worldwide gross of $255.5&nbsp;million, against a production budget of $4.5&nbsp;million.<ref name=BOM /> ''[[Deadline Hollywood]]'' calculated the net profit of the film to be $124.8&nbsp;million, when factoring together all expenses and revenues, making it the 10th most profitable release of 2017.<ref name=profit>{{cite magazine|url=https://deadline.com/2018/03/get-out-box-office-profit-2017-1202345412/|title=Deadline's 2017 Most Valuable Movie Blockbuster Tournament Gets Underway: No. 10 'Get Out'|first=Anthony|last=D'Alessandro|magazine=[[Deadline Hollywood]]|date=March 19, 2018|access-date=March 19, 2018|archive-date=November 12, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201112020308/https://deadline.com/2018/03/get-out-box-office-profit-2017-1202345412/|url-status=live}}</ref> ''[[Vulture (website)|Vulture]]'' described ''Get Out''{{'}}s 5.3 [[multiple (film)|multiple]] (total divided by opening weekend gross) as "staggering".<ref name="lincoln20170920">{{cite news |last=Lincoln |first=Kevin |title=What the 19 Movies to Ever Receive an 'F' CinemaScore Have in Common |url=http://www.vulture.com/2017/09/here-are-the-only-19-movies-to-ever-receive-an-f-cinemascore.html |work=[[vulture.com]] |date=September 20, 2017 |access-date=May 8, 2018 |archive-date=September 24, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170924050456/http://www.vulture.com/2017/09/here-are-the-only-19-movies-to-ever-receive-an-f-cinemascore.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

In North America, ''Get Out'' was released on February 24, 2017, alongside ''[[Collide (2016 film)|Collide]]'' and ''[[Rock Dog]]'', and was expected to gross $20–25&nbsp;million from 2,773 theaters in its opening weekend.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://deadline.com/2017/02/get-out-1201940270/ |title=Audiences Plan To Get Into 'Get Out' – Weekend B.O. Preview |website=Deadline Hollywood |first=Anthony |last=D'Alessandro |date=February 22, 2017 |access-date=February 22, 2017 |archive-date=December 16, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191216132223/https://deadline.com/2017/02/get-out-1201940270/ |url-status=live }}</ref> The film made $1.8&nbsp;million from Thursday night previews and $10.8&nbsp;million on its first day. It went on to open for $33.4&nbsp;million, finishing first at the box office. 38% of the film's opening-weekend audience was African American, while 35% was White, with Georgia being its most profitable market.<ref name="opening">{{cite web |url=https://deadline.com/2017/02/get-out-collide-rock-dog-lego-batman-fifty-shades-darker-box-office-1201964096/ |title=Universal/Blumhouse's 'Get Out' Now Grabbing A $30M+ Opening |first=Anthony |last=D'Alessandro |website=Deadline Hollywood |date=February 27, 2017 |access-date=February 27, 2017 |archive-date=June 12, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180612163604/https://deadline.com/2017/02/get-out-collide-rock-dog-lego-batman-fifty-shades-darker-box-office-1201964096/ |url-status=live }}</ref> 90% of its opening weekend ticket sales were purchased at the theater (versus in advance).<ref>{{cite news|last=D'Alessandro|first=Anthony|title=Jordan Peele's 'Us' To Leave 'Captain Marvel' In The Dust With $45M-$50M Opening|url=https://deadline.com/2019/03/us-jordan-peele-movie-box-office-captain-marvel-1202578460/|access-date=March 19, 2019|work=Deadline Hollywood|date=March 19, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260429223233/https://deadline.com/2019/03/us-jordan-peele-movie-box-office-captain-marvel-1202578460/|archive-date=April 29, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> In its second weekend, the film finished in second at the box office behind new release ''[[Logan (film)|Logan]]'' ($88.4&nbsp;million), grossing $28.3&nbsp;million, for a drop of 15.4%. Horror films tend to drop at least 60% in their second weekend, so this was above average.<ref>{{cite web|title='Logan's $85.3M Debut Breaks Records For Wolverine Series & Rated R Fare; Beats 'Fifty Shades' & 'Passion Of The Christ'|url=https://deadline.com/2017/03/logan-box-office-opening-get-out-the-shack-1202035207/|website=Deadline Hollywood|access-date=March 5, 2017|archive-date=June 18, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180618102415/https://deadline.com/2017/03/logan-box-office-opening-get-out-the-shack-1202035207/|url-status=live}}</ref> In its third weekend, the film grossed $21.1&nbsp;million, dropping just 25% from its previous week, and finished third at the box office behind newcomer ''[[Kong: Skull Island]]'' and ''Logan''.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://deadline.com/2017/03/kong-skull-island-beating-box-office-projections-logan-get-out-1202041809/|title='Kong' Beats His Chest On Saturday: Weekend Opening Now At $61M – Sunday AM Update|first=Anthony|last=D'Alessandro|website=Deadline Hollywood|date=March 12, 2017|access-date=March 12, 2017|archive-date=March 12, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170312111607/http://deadline.com/2017/03/kong-skull-island-beating-box-office-projections-logan-get-out-1202041809/|url-status=live}}</ref>

In March 2017, three weeks after its release, ''Get Out'' crossed the $100&nbsp;million mark domestically, making Peele the first black writer-director to do so with his debut movie.<ref name="auto">{{cite web|url=http://www.thefader.com/2017/04/04/get-out-original-screenplay-record|title=''Get Out'' Is Now The Highest Grossing live-action Debut Film Based On An Original Screenplay In History|publisher=TheFader.com|first=David|last=Renshaw|date=April 4, 2017|access-date=April 23, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260429081114/http://www.thefader.com/2017/04/04/get-out-original-screenplay-record|archive-date=April 29, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> On April 8, 2017, the film became the highest-grossing film domestically directed by a black filmmaker, beating out [[F. Gary Gray]]'s ''[[Straight Outta Compton (film)|Straight Outta Compton]],'' which grossed $162.8&nbsp;million domestically in 2015. Gray reclaimed the record two weeks later when ''[[The Fate of the Furious]]'' grossed $173.3&nbsp;million on its fourteenth day of release on April 27.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://shadowandact.com/2017/04/11/get-out-is-now-the-highest-grossing-film-domestically-by-a-black-director-but-not-for-long/|title='Get Out' Is Now the Highest Grossing Film Domestically by a Black Director (But Not for Long)|publisher=ShadowAndAct.com|first=Sergio|last=Mims|date=April 11, 2017|access-date=April 23, 2017|archive-date=April 24, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170424180037/http://shadowandact.com/2017/04/11/get-out-is-now-the-highest-grossing-film-domestically-by-a-black-director-but-not-for-long/|url-status=dead}}</ref> Domestically, ''Get Out'' is also the highest-grossing [[List of directorial debuts|debut film]] based on an original screenplay in Hollywood history, beating the two-decade-long record of 1999's ''[[The Blair Witch Project]]'' ($140.5&nbsp;million).<ref name="auto" /> By the end of March, ''Los Angeles Times'' had declared the film's success a "cultural phenomenon" noting that in addition to its box office success, "moviegoers have shared countless 'sunken place' Internet memes and other ''Get Out''-inspired [[fan art]] across social media." Josh Rottenberg, the editor of the piece, attributed the film's success to the fact that it was released "at one of the most politically charged moments in memory."<ref name="LAPhenom" />

===Critical response===
[[File:Daniel Kaluuya (2017).jpg|thumb|upright|[[Daniel Kaluuya]]'s performance garnered universal acclaim and he received his first [[Academy Awards|Academy Award]], [[British Academy Film Awards|BAFTA]], [[Golden Globe Awards|Golden Globe]], [[Screen Actors Guild Awards|SAG]], and [[Critics' Choice Movie Awards|Critics' Choice Award]] nominations.]]

On review aggregation website [[Rotten Tomatoes]], the film has an approval rating of 98% based on 403 reviews, and an average rating of 8.30/10.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/get_out |title=Get Out (2017) |work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]] |publisher=[[Fandango Media]] |access-date=June 25, 2021 |archive-date=June 12, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180612012318/https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/get_out/ |url-status=live }}</ref> The critical consensus reads, "Funny, scary, and thought-provoking, ''Get Out'' seamlessly weaves its trenchant social critiques into a brilliantly effective and entertaining horror/comedy thrill ride." The film was the highest rated wide release of 2017 on the site.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://editorial.rottentomatoes.com/guide/best-movies-2017-wide-release/|title=Top 100 Movies of 2017|work=[[Rotten Tomatoes]]|publisher=[[Fandango Media]]|access-date=January 3, 2018|date=January 3, 2018|archive-date=June 16, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180616235713/https://editorial.rottentomatoes.com/guide/best-movies-2017-wide-release/|url-status=live}}</ref> On [[Metacritic]], the film has an average weighted score of 85 out of 100, based on 48 critics, indicating "universal acclaim".<ref>{{cite web|url=https://metacritic.com/movie/get-out|title=Get Out Reviews|work=[[Metacritic]]|publisher=[[CBS Interactive]]|access-date=February 23, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260722131044/https://www.metacritic.com/movie/get-out/|archive-date=July 22, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> Audiences polled by [[CinemaScore]] gave the film an average grade of "A−" on an A+ to F scale, while [[PostTrak]] reported filmgoers gave an 84% overall positive score and a 66% "definite recommend".<ref name="opening" />

Greg Falla's book, ''Sado Mass Essayist: Certified Loverboy, Certified Cinephile'', noted Peele's movie as the start of a new era of horror he dubbed "Nu-Wave American Cerebral Horror". Falla explained that the movement is categorized by absurdist premises framing social narratives.{{citation needed|date=August 2026}}

[[Richard Roeper]] gave the film {{frac|3|1|2}} stars, saying: "the real star of the film is writer-director Jordan Peele, who has created a work that addresses the myriad levels of racism, pays homage to some great horror films, carves out its own creative path, has a distinctive visual style—and is flat-out funny as well."<ref>{{cite news |date=February 22, 2017 |author=Richard Roeper |author-link=Richard Roeper |url=http://chicago.suntimes.com/entertainment/get-out-earns-its-laughs-while-honoring-horror-traditions/ |title='Get Out' earns its laughs while honoring horror traditions |newspaper=[[Chicago Sun-Times]] |access-date=January 9, 2020 |archive-date=November 11, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181111223838/https://chicago.suntimes.com/entertainment/get-out-earns-its-laughs-while-honoring-horror-traditions/ |url-status=live }}</ref> Keith Phipps of ''[[Uproxx]]'' praised the cast and Peele's direction, saying, "That he brings the technical skill of a practiced horror master is more of a surprise. The final thrill of ''Get Out''—beyond the slow-building sense of danger, the unsettling atmosphere, and the twisty revelation of what's really going on—is that Peele's just getting started."<ref>{{cite web|last=Phipps|first=Keith|url=http://uproxx.com/movies/get-out-review/|title=Jordan Peele's 'Get Out' Uses 21st Century Racism To Make A Masterful Horror Movie|website=Uproxx.com|date=February 22, 2017|access-date=February 23, 2017|archive-date=February 24, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170224133313/http://uproxx.com/movies/get-out-review/|url-status=live}}</ref> Mike Rougeau of [[IGN]] gave the film 9/10, and wrote, "''Get Out''{{'}}s whole journey, through every tense conversation, A-plus punchline and shocking act of violence, feels totally earned. And the conclusion is worth each uncomfortable chuckle and moment of doubt."<ref>{{cite web|last=Rougeau|first=Mike|title=''Get Out'' Review|url=https://ign.com/articles/2017/02/23/get-out-review-2|access-date=February 25, 2017|website=[[IGN]]|date=February 22, 2017}}</ref> [[Peter Travers]] of ''[[Rolling Stone]]'' gave ''Get Out'' a 3.5 out of 4, and called it a "jolt-a-minute horrorshow laced with racial tension and stinging satirical wit."<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Travers |first=Peter |title=''Get Out'' Review: Scares Meet Racially Charged Satire in Instant Horror Classic |url=https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/reviews/peter-travers-get-out-movie-review-w468003 |access-date=February 25, 2017 |magazine=[[Rolling Stone]] |date=February 22, 2017 |archive-date=February 24, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170224164627/http://www.rollingstone.com/movies/reviews/peter-travers-get-out-movie-review-w468003 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Scott Mendelson of ''[[Forbes]]'' said the film captured the [[zeitgeist]] and called it a "modern American horror classic".<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.forbes.com/sites/scottmendelson/2017/02/21/review-jordan-peeles-get-out-is-a-new-horror-classic/ |first=Scott |last=Mendelson |title=Review: Jordan Peele's 'Get Out' Is a New Horror Classic |work=[[Forbes]] |date=February 27, 2017 |access-date=March 14, 2017 |archive-date=March 15, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170315005243/https://www.forbes.com/sites/scottmendelson/2017/02/21/review-jordan-peeles-get-out-is-a-new-horror-classic/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

Critic [[Armond White]] gave a negative review in ''[[National Review]]'', referring to the film as a "Get-Whitey movie" and stating that it "[reduces] racial politics to trite horror-comedy ... it's an [[Barack Obama|Obama]] movie for [[Quentin Tarantino|Tarantino]] fans."<ref>{{cite web |last1=White |first1=Armond |title=Return of the Get-Whitey Movie |url=https://www.nationalreview.com/2017/02/jordan-peeles-get-out-trite-get-whitey-movie/ |website=National Review |date=February 24, 2017 |access-date=December 1, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260727232213/https://www.nationalreview.com/2017/02/jordan-peeles-get-out-trite-get-whitey-movie/ |archive-date=July 27, 2026 |url-status=live}}</ref> The ''[[The New York Observer|New York Observer]]'' critic [[Rex Reed]] included the film on his list of 10 Worst Films of 2017,<ref>{{cite web|url=http://observer.com/2017/12/rex-reeds-10-best-movies-and-10-worst-movies-of-2017/|title=Rex Reed's 10 Best Movies and 10 Worst Movies of 2017|work=Observer|date=December 26, 2017|access-date=April 6, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260718190836/https://observer.com/2017/12/rex-reeds-10-best-movies-and-10-worst-movies-of-2017/|archive-date=July 18, 2026|url-status=live}}</ref> and later sardonically stated in a ''[[CBS Sunday Morning]]'' interview, "I didn't care if all the black men are turned into robots." In 2018, ''[[IndieWire]]'' writers ranked the script the third best American screenplay of the 21st century, with Chris O'Falt arguing that Peele "walked a narrative tightrope that required as much craft as insight [...] the audience's understanding of what Chris (Daniel Kaluuya) is thinking and feeling is always clear. [[Alfred Hitchcock|Hitchcock]]-like in its execution, and playing off genre and audience expectation (especially about how racial dynamics are traditionally portrayed on screen), the twists and turns of ''Get Out'' are not only gasp-inducing; each one reveals a new layer to its exploration of systematic racist belief systems."<ref>{{Cite web|last1=Dry|first1=Jude|last2=O'Falt|first2=Chris|last3=Erbland|first3=Kate|last4=Kohn|first4=Eric|last5=Sharf|first5=Zack|last6=Marotta|first6=Jenna|last7=Thompson|first7=Anne|last8=Earl|first8=William|last9=Nordine|first9=Michael|last10=Ehrlich|first10=David|date=April 20, 2018|title=The 25 Best American Screenplays of the 21st Century, From 'Eternal Sunshine' to 'Lady Bird'|url=https://www.indiewire.com/2018/04/best-screenplays-scripts-american-1201952200/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210119174929/https://www.indiewire.com/2018/04/best-screenplays-scripts-american-1201952200/|archive-date=January 19, 2021|access-date=February 4, 2021|website=IndieWire|language=en|url-status=live}}</ref>

===Accolades===
{{Main|List of accolades received by Get Out{{!}}List of accolades received by ''Get Out''}}
At the [[90th Academy Awards]], the film won an Oscar for [[Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay|Best Original Screenplay]] and was nominated in three other categories: [[Academy Award for Best Picture|Best Picture]], [[Academy Award for Best Director|Best Director]], and [[Academy Award for Best Actor|Best Actor]] for [[Daniel Kaluuya]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=The 90th Academy Awards |url=https://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/2018 |access-date=September 26, 2022 |website=oscars.org |date=April 15, 2019 |archive-date=April 17, 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180417100721/https://www.oscars.org/oscars/ceremonies/2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> Peele became the third person (after [[Warren Beatty]] and [[James L. Brooks]]) to earn Best Picture, Director and Screenplay nominations for a debut film, and the first African-American winner for Best Original Screenplay (and fourth overall nominated, after [[John Singleton]], [[Spike Lee]], and [[Suzanne de Passe]]).<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://consequenceofsound.net/2018/01/jordan-peele-makes-oscars-history-with-get-out-nominations/|title=Jordan Peele makes Oscars history with Get Out nominations|date=January 24, 2018|work=Consequence of Sound|first=Eddie|last=Fu|access-date=January 28, 2018|language=en-US|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210311044151/https://consequenceofsound.net/2018/01/jordan-peele-makes-oscars-history-with-get-out-nominations/|archive-date=March 11, 2021|url-status=live}}</ref> It also became the 6th horror film to be nominated for best picture, after ''[[The Exorcist (movie)|The Exorcist]]'', ''[[Jaws (film)|Jaws]]'', ''[[The Sixth Sense]]'', ''[[The Silence of the Lambs (film)|The Silence of the Lambs]]'' and ''[[Black Swan (film)|Black Swan]]''.

''Get Out'' divided Oscar voters, with many older members of the [[Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences]] dismissing the film or declining to watch it. "I had multiple conversations with longtime Academy members who were like, 'That was not an Oscar film,{{'"}} according to a new voter. "Honestly, a few of them had not even seen it and they were saying it, so dispelling that kind of thing has been super important."<ref>{{cite news |first= Kyle |last= Buchanan |author2= Stacey Wilson Hunt |author3= Chris Lee |url= http://www.vulture.com/2018/02/how-new-oscar-voters-are-changing-the-way-the-academy-thinks.html |title= We Polled New Oscar Voters: How Are They Changing the Way the Academy Thinks? |work= [[New York (magazine)|Vulture]] |date= Feb 2018 |access-date= January 9, 2020 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20260430194817/https://www.vulture.com/2018/02/how-new-oscar-voters-are-changing-the-way-the-academy-thinks.html |archive-date= April 30, 2026 |url-status= live }}</ref> One anonymous Oscar voter told ''[[The Hollywood Reporter]]'' they felt alienated by the Oscar campaign: "Instead of focusing on the fact that this was an entertaining little horror movie that made quite a bit of money, they started trying to suggest it had deeper meaning than it does, and, as far as I'm concerned, they played the [[race card]], and that really turned me off.
